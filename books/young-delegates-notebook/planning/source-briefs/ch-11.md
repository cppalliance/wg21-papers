# Chapter 11 Source Brief: Championing Your Paper

Fact brief for the writer. Sources: wg21-bible parts 2, 7, 8; wg21-milieu; paper-review-system. Numbers and paper IDs are verified against those files. Conflicts and unverified items are listed under Flags; link targets under Resources.

## The Bandwidth Gap and Burnout

WG21 publishes 300-500 papers per year across pre-meeting and post-meeting mailings. The median delegate reads 20-40, concentrated in their own subgroup. On roughly 90% of plenary polls the median delegate has no informed technical opinion. The sources frame this as rational ignorance (Downs 1957): reading 400 papers to sway one vote among ~200 has near-zero expected payoff.

Scale numbers: ~350 active delegates from 24-31 nations. Per-meeting attendance is smaller and hybrid. Brno June 2026 had 200 attendees (55% in person), 28 nations, 25 new guests, 22 subgroups, 6 tracks. Sofia June 2025 had ~200 (~67% in person), 30 nations, 23 subgroups, 7 tracks (Sutter trip reports). Six or seven tracks run at once, so one person covers one room.

Burnout is documented, not anecdotal. The committee pulse poll (P2260) found over 60% "often exhausted" and 46% calling the pace "unsustainable." Sutter stepped down as convener after a decade ("a decade is enough"). When a domain expert stops attending, the correction they carried is lost. Mid-tenure departures skew toward the empathetic who burn out, early departures toward action-oriented people who find the pace intolerable.

## Presocialization: Building Support Before the Room

A paper that arrives without prior hallway work and buy-in from recognized actors gets deprioritized. Pre-meeting and hallway work, not the in-room argument, sets most outcomes.

The decisive event is the five minutes before a poll: the chair's framing summary ("it seems we have consensus for X"), the first recognized voice to speak, the presenter's confidence, and applause. Applause acts as real-time social proof, and three enthusiastic clappers can seed a room-wide cascade. Reflection was voted into C++26 to "sustained applause" (Sofia 2025).

## Champions and Presenting When You Cannot Attend

A proposal needs a qualified presenter in the room or it is "not actionable." No presenter in the room means no progress that meeting. If you cannot attend, you need someone there to carry and present the paper. (The sources establish the in-room-presenter requirement and the value of buy-in from recognized actors; they do not spell out an explicit "how to find or be a champion" playbook - see Flags.)

Access paths if cost or travel is the barrier: national body membership (US body is INCITS PL22.16, charges a fee), free Boost Foundation alternate-representative status (access-equivalent), or guest attendance (free, one week's notice to the convener, full subgroup participation, no plenary vote).

## Reputation, the Expert Bubble, and How Newcomers Break In

When delegates cannot evaluate substance, they evaluate the source. Established authors draw benefit of the doubt. Newcomers doing equally good work draw extra scrutiny (Matthew effect, Merton 1968). Social standing functions as currency that buys agenda access, favorable scheduling, and the presumption of competence. The active delegate count sits near Dunbar's number (~150), so people track reputations rather than 400 papers.

How newcomers break in: the paper-review system on wg21.org is the lowest-commitment on-ramp. Review others' papers to earn standing to request a review of your own ("giving precedes asking"). Reviews are public, credited, and attached to the paper permanently, which makes chairs more likely to prioritize a reviewed paper and authors unable to ignore named feedback. Attend the Sunday 6pm newcomer orientation, and vote your real position rather than sitting Neutral.

## The Committee Evaluates the Presenter, Not Just the Paper

A widely described consequence of the bandwidth gap: the room weighs author standing and the presenter's manner alongside, or instead of, the paper's content. Present this neutrally as a structural effect, not a character flaw.

The temperament the process rewards is explicit in its own documents. P0939 describes members as "a bunch of volunteers" who "listen more than talk." The Sunday orientation tells newcomers to "assume good faith" and "do not take offense easily." Social reliability and willingness to concede read as positive signals. Escalation cuts the other way: SD-4 itself says escalation "erodes credibility," and the minority is expected to "accept group decisions." A "small vocal minority can stop any proposal at any stage of the process" (P2000), but exercising that brake carries a credibility cost. Being seen as obstructionist is damaging.

## How Long Things Take: Years, Not Months

The train model (P1000, Sutter) ships a standard every three years since C++11. The deadline is real but elastic: "now or never" became "now or three years from now."

Famous long-runners, with the paper IDs and timelines the sources actually provide:

- Coroutines. Stackless coroutines reached C++20 in about six years, backed by Microsoft with experimental compiler support before formal approval. Stackful coroutines (P0876, community authors) reached 19 revisions over 12 years with decades of production deployment and are not in C++26. (The stackless/C++20 coroutines paper number is not in the sources - see Flags.)
- Executors became senders. The long executors effort became std::execution (P2300, cited at R10). The Networking TS, built on Boost.Asio (in field use since 2003, a "twenty-one-year arc"), was set aside at Kona 2023 in favor of P2300. The sender/receiver framework ran 11 revisions, and a "symmetric transfer" gap surfaced only years later in a national body comment.
- Modules. Standardized in C++20 (2020), nearly unusable 2020-2023, with the full build-system pipeline still 12-18 months out as of mid-2026. Intel abandoned its own C++ frontend in 2021. (No single modules paper number appears in the sources - see Flags.)
- Concepts. The user lists it as a famous long-runner, but the sources give no paper number or timeline for Concepts - see Flags.

Two cases show the multi-cycle reality. Contracts: C++20 Contracts (P0542R5) were adopted at Cologne and removed at Prague with no production deployment. C++26 Contracts are P2900 (R14). Five national bodies filed comments against contracts in the C++26 committee draft. Profiles: endorsed unanimously by the Direction Group (P2759R1, 2023), voted 47-2 at Issaquah, given 18-1 consensus by SG23 at Wroclaw (P3081R0), reaffirmed by six senior members (P3970R0, 2026), and still not in C++26. Strong endorsement does not equal shipping.

## The First Discussion Is Existential: "Do We Want It at All?"

The Direction Group sets a priority list, and chairs schedule against it. The group has no formal scheduling authority. Its list works as the default chairs follow, because following it needs no justification and deviating does. A topic off the list competes for leftover time and may not be heard. Framing a proposal in terms of current Direction Group priorities helps it earn time.

Scheduling is the first gate, before any vote. A paper that never reaches the agenda never reaches a poll. Chairs control which papers are heard, in what order, and for how long. The first LEWG (library) or EWG (core language) discussion is effectively "do we want this at all?" Directional approval precedes wording work.

## Responding to Feedback, Being Sent Back, and Withdrawing

Consensus is the ISO standard: "absence of sustained opposition to substantial issues by any important part of the concerned interests." It is neither majority rule nor a minority veto. Subgroup polls use the five-point scale (Strongly Favor through Strongly Against) and a proposal normally advances when favor outnumbers against by more than two to one (the 2:1 Guideline, SD-4).

Being sent back for further work is the normal path. Papers iterate R0, R1, R2 and up. Reviewers read the latest revision, and when a paper returns the room reads what changed. Bring a paper for any objection or alternative: "if a competing alternative does not have a paper, it does not exist and will not block progress" (Bird-in-Hand, SD-4). A concern with no paper carries little weight.

Implementer feedback can be reframed as obstruction. P3962R0 (18 implementers, Ranns et al. 2026) reports that implementation feedback is "treated as adversarial, or framed primarily as an obstacle to progress rather than as essential design input." P2274R0 (Ballman 2020) states that WG21 "does not have any requirement on implementation experience to adopt a proposal." A reform attempt to require deployment evidence (P2138R4, Voutilainen 2021) failed in LEWG, 19 in favor to 12 opposed.

When to withdraw a paper is not addressed in these sources - see Flags.

## When a Poll Goes Against You: Re-litigation and Procedural Momentum

Procedural momentum means revision count is read as legitimacy. After R3 or R4, procedural fairness creates a default presumption of approval: the committee "had its chance" to object, so opposing a well-revised paper feels obstructionist regardless of its content. This is the "R4 benefit of the doubt."

Re-litigation runs asymmetrically. When leadership reopens a settled question on a "circumstances have changed" standard, it is framed as responsible stewardship. The canonical case is the C++20 Contracts removal: rejected by better than two to one five months earlier, reopened because the deadline had gotten closer, and the re-vote carried. When opponents try to reopen a settled question, the same move is labeled "out of harmony with the ISO Code of Conduct." A lost poll is not always final, but reopening one from the losing side is costly and penalized.

## Strategy and Temperament

Do not take it personally. The orientation norm is explicit: "assume good faith," "do not take offense easily." Disagreement on the merits is the normal texture of the room, and the emotional experience of having your paper discussed is real but expected. Separate disagreement (a technical objection with a paper) from opposition (a person blocking).

The "minimal version everyone accepts" maps onto the consensus rule (absence of sustained opposition) and the principle of shipping independently useful primitives before a monolith. A smaller ask clears the consensus bar more easily, and you can measure adoption at each stage. Do not cause needless delay: obstruction carries a lasting credibility cost (SD-4 "erodes credibility"; P2000). (The specific terms "max-min solution," "back-pocket alternative," "claim a territory," "start small," and "pick your battles" are not named in these sources - see Flags.)

## Corporate Resource Asymmetry

Entities with concentrated stakes participate continuously. The diffuse user population does not (Stigler 1971, regulatory capture). The five major toolchains are maintained by five organizations whose representatives are continuously present, while users meet the output only in compiler release notes. WG21 publishes attendance by national body, not by employer (N5007), so the employer-share test cannot be run on public data (confidence medium that capture operates).

Paid committee time and funded implementations advance faster. The contrast the sources cite repeatedly: institutionally backed proposals (stackless coroutines, std::execution/P2300) advanced over deployment-rich but less-resourced ones (stackful coroutines, the Networking TS). Administrative load is rising and prices domain experts out of seats. The entry cost itself (three week-long meetings a year plus travel) favors the funded professional over the volunteer.
