## Introduction

> I have not found among my possessions anything which I hold more dear than, or value so much as, the knowledge of the actions of great men, acquired by long experience in contemporary affairs, and a continual study of antiquity; which, having reflected upon it with great and prolonged diligence, I now send, digested into a little volume, to your Magnificence.
>
> Niccolò Machiavelli, dedicatory letter, [*The Prince*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1232), translated by W. K. Marriott

I wrote this for you. Not for the experts who've sat in these rooms for twenty years. For you, the one who just found out that a group of volunteers decides what goes into C++ and wondered how to join them.

The group is real, and you can join it. It's called [WG21](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee), and it's the committee that owns the C++ standard. Around two hundred people take part in each meeting. They argue, they vote, and every three years they ship a new version of the language.

These volunteers are some of the most careful people you'll ever meet. They'll spend an hour on the placement of a single word. You'll come to understand why.

Machiavelli gave a prince the one thing he valued most: what he'd learned watching powerful people up close. This notebook works the same way, smaller and plainer. It's the worn, marked-up notebook I wish someone had pressed into my hands before my first meeting.

### Who This Notebook Is For

You belong here, wherever you're starting from. Maybe you've never been to a meeting, or maybe you've been to a few and still feel lost in the room. Everyone there was new once, even the people who run the place.

You don't need to know every rule in C++. You don't need to have written a compiler. You need only curiosity and the patience to keep showing up.

You also bring something the committee needs. You use C++, and you know where it hurts. Most people who use the language will never attend a meeting. When you speak up, you speak for them.

### What This Notebook Is Not

This isn't a textbook, a manual, or an encyclopedia. It's my notebook, and I take sides in it. I value stability over novelty, and I put the people who use C++ ahead of the people who design it.

When the evidence is thin, I say so. Where the committee has written a rule down, I link to it, so you can check me. Names, fees, and dates are as of 2026, and they'll drift. If a linked rule and I ever disagree, trust the rule.

### What Each Chapter Covers

The notebook moves, in order, from "what is this thing?" to "how do I take part well?"

It starts with your bearings. Chapter 1 shows you why the standard matters and why every change you make is close to permanent. Chapter 2 introduces the committee and the many ways you can join in. Chapter 3 explains how papers work, because the paper is the unit of work.

Then it takes you inside. Chapter 4 shows you how to take part from your desk, long before you ever travel. Chapter 5 maps the rooms, so you know who decides what. Chapters 6 and 7 get you to your first meeting and help you survive it. Chapter 8 shows what a vote really means.

Next comes your own paper, when you have one worth writing. Chapter 9 lays out what a proposal must contain to earn the committee's time. Chapter 10 walks through the tools, the format, and the deadlines. Chapter 11 covers the long work of building support and surviving feedback.

Last come the hard edges. Chapter 12 names the deep design constraints that decide what can and can't ship. Chapter 13 collects the common mistakes, so you don't make them.

### How to Read This Notebook

Read the chapters in order, because each one builds on the ones before it. Every chapter also ends somewhere safe to stop, so you can set the notebook down and pick it up again when you're ready.

Follow the links. Many of them lead to the committee's own documents, and learning to read those is half the job.

### Keep Your Wits about You

One practice matters more than any fact in this notebook. The committee runs on persuasion, and persuasion can talk you out of your own judgment. Hold on to yours.

Someone you admire calls a design obviously right. Nobody objects, and your own doubt starts to feel rude. That's the moment to ask two questions. Has anyone actually built it and used it? And who does this help, the people at the table or the people who'll live with it?
