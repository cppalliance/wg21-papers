WG21 looks like a wall of acronyms at first. EWG, LEWG, and fifteen study groups blur together. This chapter turns that wall into a clear map.

### 5.1 One Committee, Many Rooms

WG21 does its work in subgroups (the smaller groups from chapter 2), and people call them *the rooms*. Each room owns one slice of the job, and every one of them is open to you, even as a guest. The rooms meet at the same time, in parallel, all week.

That parallel timetable is the *schedule grid*. Six or seven rooms run at once, so you can't attend them all.

The rooms split into two jobs. Some decide what to build (design), and others get the exact words right (wording).

### 5.2 The Evolution Rooms: EWG and LEWG

Two rooms do the design review from chapter 3, deciding whether a feature is worth building. *EWG*, the Evolution Working Group, handles the core language. It judges proposed language features.

*LEWG*, the Library Evolution Working Group, does the same for the standard library. It decides which library features the committee wants. If you have strong views on the tools you call every day, this room suits you.

### 5.3 The Wording Rooms: CWG and LWG

Two more rooms guard the exact standard text. The paper's author drafts the wording, and these rooms review it, fix it, and approve it. *CWG*, the Core Working Group, does this for the language. It reads every line for holes and for anything that could mean two things.

*LWG*, the Library Working Group, does the same for the library. These rooms ask whether the text says what the design rooms approved, not whether the feature is wanted. If you like finding the one case a sentence forgot, this is your kind of work.

### 5.4 Study Groups

Many new ideas start in a *study group*, or SG. A study group focuses on one area, like concurrency or networking, and shapes raw ideas.

They're numbered SG1 through SG23. Fifteen are active as of 2026, and the rest sit dormant until new papers wake them up. The [committee page](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee) lists them all.

When an idea is ready, it graduates to a design room. The convener (the person who runs WG21) creates and closes study groups as needs change.

<div id="try-this-today">

Open the committee page on [isocpp.org](https://isocpp.org) and read down the list of study groups. Find the one closest to the code you already write, and note who chairs it. Then look for that group's name on the audience line of a paper you care about.

### 5.5 How a Paper Moves Between Groups

A paper follows the lifecycle from chapter 3, and now you can name the rooms. A study group often shapes it first. Then a design room approves it, a wording room checks its text, and the plenary (the full committee) adopts it.

Each handoff is a vote to send it onward. The same vote can send it back for another revision instead, and a paper can face the same room more than once.

Some papers touch more than one room. A feature that changes the language and the library visits both evolution rooms, sometimes together in a joint session.

### 5.6 The Train Model

C++ ships on a fixed schedule, and that rule is *the train model*. Every three years a new standard leaves the station with whatever features are ready. C++14, 17, 20, 23, and 26 all rode it.

Herb Sutter pitched it at the committee's August 2011 meeting, and the minutes, [N3316](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2011/n3316.pdf), record his case. Before the train, C++11 slipped for years. It's been the model since 2012, and [P1000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p1000r7.pdf) lays out the current timetable. The schedule traded "now or never" for "catch the next train."

The model has a safety valve: pull what isn't ready. The committee did exactly that in 2019 with [P1823](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p1823r0.pdf), taking contracts out of C++20. A redesigned version caught a later train. In February 2025, the committee adopted [P2900](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/p2900r14.pdf), which put contracts into C++26. The valve works, but pulling a big feature is never cheap.

### 5.7 How Features Get Removed

Taking a feature out is far harder than putting one in. Once a feature ships, real programs depend on it, and removing it breaks them. So the standard almost only grows, as chapter 1 warned.

Pulling is different. It takes a feature out before it ships, the way contracts left C++20. Removing something that already shipped usually starts with *deprecation*, a formal notice in the standard that the feature may be removed in a later version. The actual cut comes years after that.

Take `auto_ptr`, an early smart pointer. It was deprecated in C++11 and removed in C++17, six years later. Exported templates were in the very first standard, but almost no compiler ever built them. C++11 cut them.

Cutting a feature from the text doesn't cut it from the world. GCC's standard library still ships `auto_ptr`, and you may meet it in old code you inherit.

### 5.8 The People Who Run It

A handful of officers keep the committee running. The convener appoints the chairs, creates study groups, sets the schedule, and judges whether the full committee agrees. [SC22](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/), WG21's parent committee, appoints the convener for terms of up to three years, with no limit on renewals. Two vice-conveners and a secretary help run things.

Two *project editors* keep the working draft. They're the people who apply every approved change to the text of the standard. It's quiet, exacting work that holds the whole document together.

Each room has its own small crew. A *chair* runs the session, and someone from the room takes the minutes. It might be you.

### 5.9 Who Sets Priorities: The Direction Group

A small senior group, the Direction Group from chapter 3, steers the committee's focus. It writes down where C++ should go in [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) and what the next standard should put first in [P5000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p5000r1.pdf). Membership is by invitation, not election.

The Direction Group leaves the votes on individual papers to the rooms. It sets the big-picture priorities that chairs lean on when scheduling. Framing your work to match those priorities helps it get attention.

### 5.10 Chair Power

The chair of a room holds more power than it looks. The chair decides which papers make the agenda, when they come up, how the polls are worded, and when the room has agreed. None of that is a vote, yet all of it shapes outcomes.

The strongest power is the calendar. A chair who never schedules a paper can stall it for years, without ever voting against it. So getting on the agenda is its own battle.

The calendar does have rules, though. [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) tells chairs what comes first: work tied to the Direction Group's priorities, work aimed at the next standard, and work that can make progress at this meeting. Everything else gets time as time allows.

You can watch the queue yourself. Every paper gets a tracking issue in a public list on [GitHub](https://github.com/cplusplus/papers). It shows you what's waiting and what's moving.

When one of those papers is yours, don't just watch it. Ask the chair for a slot.

### 5.11 The Rules: Standing Documents

The committee's own rules live in [standing documents](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents) (the numbered SD files from chapter 3). Eight are current, and the numbering has gaps because older ones were retired. They run from study-group setup to how to write papers. The main one is SD-4, the practices and procedures.

Each design room also writes down how it judges papers. [SD-9](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-9-library-evolution-policies) holds LEWG's library policies, and [SD-10](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-10-language-evolution-principles) holds EWG's language principles. Read the one for your room before you write for it.

New standing documents need the full committee's agreement, and none of them expires on its own. So old practices tend to stay.

### 5.12 The Limits of Power: Volunteers and the Implementer Veto

Here's the truth under all the structure: nobody can be ordered to do anything. On paper, WG21 only recommends, as chapter 2 said, and the countries vote to make the standard official. To WG21, everyone is a volunteer, even the people an employer sends.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

A proposal lives only as long as someone keeps showing up for it. Nobody can be assigned to do that for you. If it's your idea, the someone is you.

</div>

The compiler teams hold a quiet *implementer veto*. If GCC, Clang, and MSVC (the three implementers from chapter 1) won't build a feature, the standard can't make them. That's what stranded the exported templates from section 5.7.

It's a veto over what you can use, not over what gets adopted. The committee can standardize a feature nobody has built yet. Then the standard says one thing, your compiler does another, and you live in the gap.

This isn't only history. In January 2026, eighteen implementers wrote [P3962](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p3962r0.pdf) asking the committee to slow down, because features pile up faster than they can build them. The people who turn the standard real are stretched thin.

<div id="short-version">

EWG and LEWG decide what to build, CWG and LWG check the exact words, study groups feed them, and the plenary adopts. The train model ships C++ every three years with whatever's ready, and shipped features almost never come back out.

The convener runs the committee, the Direction Group sets priorities, and the chairs control the calendar. The rules live in eight standing documents, SD-4 above all.

WG21 runs on volunteers. On paper, it only recommends, and the countries make the standard official. And since nobody can make the compiler teams build a feature, they decide what you can actually use.
