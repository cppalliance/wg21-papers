## 8. How Decisions Get Made

Voting here doesn't work the way you'd expect. No majority rules, and no simple count wins. This chapter shows you how the committee really decides, so you can read a room and vote with a clear head.

### 8.1 The Culture of Consensus

The committee decides by *consensus*, which isn't what most people think. It doesn't mean everyone agrees, and it doesn't mean the majority wins. It means no sustained opposition is left standing.

[SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), the practices guide from chapter 3, quotes ISO's definition: "general agreement, characterized by the absence of sustained opposition to substantial issues." The test is whether people can live with a decision. A few mild objections don't block it. A small group of firm, reasoned objections can.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Consensus is social pressure with a polite name. Keep your wits about you, and don't let the room's mood stand in for your own judgment.

</div>

### 8.2 Straw Polls and the Five-Point Scale

To read the room, the chair takes a *straw poll*. In the usual kind, you pick one of five positions: strongly favor, weakly favor, neutral, weakly against, or strongly against. Where the room clearly agrees, the chair may just ask whether anyone objects.

Pick the strength as carefully as the side. Weakly against means you'd rather not. Save strongly against for something you truly can't live with. Those are the hands that sink proposals.

In a subgroup, everyone in the room can vote, guests included. The chair weighs the discussion and the numbers, then decides whether there's consensus. No fixed number settles it, so the chair judges.

### 8.3 Reading Poll Results

A rough rule guides the call. A proposal normally advances when those in favor outnumber those against by more than two to one. This is the *2:1 guideline*, and the word *normally* is where the chair's judgment lives. Neutrals stay out of the ratio, but a mostly neutral room is a warning sign.

ISO's definition has a second half, and it's why the ratio comes last. Consensus also takes a process that hears everyone out and tries to reconcile the arguments. So the chair may first ask the against voters why, and may poll again.

But the count isn't everything. A small group can stop a lot. Because the bar is no sustained opposition, a block of strong-against votes can sink a proposal even when far more people favor it. That's built into the system, not a loophole.

This really happens. In a January 2022 online poll, one large library proposal for C++23 drew 37 in favor and 17 against, 11 of them strongly. It [still failed](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2459r0.html) for "sustained strong opposition." More than two to one wasn't enough.

That power has a limit, though. SD-4 says consensus is "not a tyranny of the majority," and it's "not a tyranny of the minority" either. Once objectors have been heard, they can't just keep saying no.

### 8.4 Straw Poll versus Formal Vote

A straw poll is only a temperature check. A *formal vote* is the binding kind, taken at plenary and on the national ballots. One steers the work, the other commits it.

Still, take straw polls seriously. The committee treats them as honest predictors of the formal vote to come. How you vote in a subgroup signals where you'll land later.

Whichever vote it is, you face the same question: should you vote at all?

### 8.5 When to Vote and When to Abstain

Vote your honest position when you have one. If you favor something even weakly, say so. Neutral means you read the paper, weighed the issues, and truly land in the middle, which should be rare.

If you vote against, be ready to say why, and to name what would change your mind.

The chair calls for strongly favor, and a forest of hands goes up. Weakly favor, and more follow. When the chair calls for strongly against, yours is the only hand in the air. Heads turn. The chair asks whether you'd like to say why.

You give your reason in two sentences and name the change that would win you over. The chair thanks you and moves on. Your hand didn't stop anything, but your concern is on the record now. That's the poll doing its job.

When the topic is outside what you know, *abstain*. Abstaining means not voting at all, and it's the honest move on a paper you haven't read. Hearing a summary isn't the same as knowing the issue.

<div id="watch-out">

Voting with the room on a paper you haven't read feels safe, and raising your hand feels like belonging. But the chair reads every hand as judgment, so a borrowed vote is noise that still counts. Read it or don't vote.

</div>

The Direction Group (the senior group from chapter 5) makes the matching point about voting against. Its paper, [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf), says "I didn't have time to read the document" is no reason to oppose a paper that came in on time. So don't oppose what you haven't read, and don't support it either.

Whatever you vote, ask for evidence. Don't vote yes on enthusiasm or a friend's nod. Be moved by proof, and vote your conviction, not the room's momentum.

### 8.6 Two Gates: Direction versus Design

A proposal passes through two different gates. *Direction approval* is early: does the committee want this kind of thing at all? *Design approval* is later: is this specific design the right one?

Here's the trap. Passing either gate is not a promise to ship. A warm direction poll can sit for years with nothing delivered.

Read encouragement for what it is. "We'd like to see more work" means keep going, and nothing more. Expansion statements, [P1306R1](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p1306r1.pdf), won approval for C++20 in 2019 from EWG, the language design room. They missed C++20 anyway and were adopted for C++26 in 2025, six years later.

### 8.7 When You Can't Live with It

Can't live with where the room is headed? Say so early: on the reflector before the meeting if you can, or during the discussion, before the poll. A lone objector is weaker than one who's found an ally on the merits.

If you still think a serious mistake is being made, there's a short *escalation path* with a hard deadline. Tell the EWG and LEWG chairs as early as you can. Post the concern on the subgroup's reflector by 5 p.m. the evening before the closing plenary, and raise it with your national body.

At plenary the convener may ask your delegation's chair whether the objection is personal or national. It's only national if the delegation has already talked it through.

You can still raise a hand at plenary and force a count. But SD-4 says an objection that skipped these steps, new or repeated, "should not be given weight." After the deadline, the concern waits for a later round: a new reflector thread or a national ballot comment, backed by at least a draft paper.

### 8.8 The Plenary: Where Features Enter the Draft

The week ends with the *closing plenary* on Saturday. Each subgroup reports, and the committee polls on motions to adopt finished wording. A feature enters the working draft the moment its motion passes.

Plenary usually runs on silence. The convener (the person who runs WG21) asks if anyone objects, and if no one does, the motion passes. Raising a hand to object takes no explanation.

An objection forces a three-way poll: favor, against, or neutral. Only experts in the global directory vote, so guests watch. The convener then judges whether there's consensus. Plenary is where objections are counted, not where they're born.

Passing the subgroups isn't the final word. A proposal can clear every room and still fail at plenary. The type `std::byte` did exactly that in November 2016, [failing with 24 in favor and 15 against](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2016/n4623.pdf), after a late fight over its name.

It came back at the next meeting and [passed 44 to 2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2017/n4654.pdf), name unchanged. What did change was the paper trail. In between, [P0583R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2017/p0583r0.pdf), "`std::byte` is the correct name," answered the objection in writing. A loss at plenary isn't final. Your answer to an objection is a paper.

### 8.9 Processing Issues

Not every decision is about new features. The wording rooms, CWG and LWG, work steadily through the [core](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/cwg_active.html) and [library](https://cplusplus.github.io/LWG/lwg-active.html) issues lists, the standard's bug trackers. They settle each defect's exact fix, then move the ready ones at plenary [in one batch](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/n5015.html).

It's quiet work, and you can help without a vote. File a clear issue with a proposed fix, as chapter 3 suggested, and this is where it gets decided.

### 8.10 The Countries Vote

Before the standard is final, the countries vote, and each one gets a single vote. They usually see the draft twice, and the first round is where you can help.

First it goes out as a *committee draft* for comments. That's the round meant for changing the text. That round gave C++26 its 411 comments. A good comment works like a small paper: it says what's wrong, where, and what text would fix it. Drafting one for your national body takes no seniority.

Then the cleaned-up draft goes out for approval as a *DIS* (Draft International Standard). For C++26, that ballot was scheduled to run from July 31 to October 9, 2026. Passing takes a strong majority. Two-thirds of the voting countries must approve, and no more than a quarter of the votes can be *no*.

A country with concerns usually votes *yes* and attaches comments. A *no* is blunt. SD-4 says it means the country doesn't want the work "to progress at all in any form." Every comment gets considered whatever the vote, so a *no* buys nothing extra, except the risk of sinking the whole draft.

If the DIS passes and the text needs no technical changes, the draft goes straight to publication. Technical changes trigger one last, shorter vote on a near-final *FDIS* (Final Draft International Standard). The committee aims to make the DIS its last ballot, so a design change this late needs nearly everyone on board.

<div id="short-version">

Consensus means no sustained opposition is left standing, not that the majority wins. In a straw poll, more than two to one in favor usually moves a proposal forward, but a firm minority can still sink it. Once heard, though, it can't just keep saying no.

Vote your honest position, even as the only hand in the air. Ask for evidence, and abstain on papers you haven't read. Direction and design approval aren't promises to ship.

Plenary is where objections are counted, not born, so raise yours by 5 p.m. the evening before. Then the countries vote, and the committee draft round is where a good comment can still change the text.

</div>

You can read a room now, and you don't have to follow it. Your best question in any room is "show me the evidence," and your best answer is a paper. Chapter 9 shows you how to build a good one.
