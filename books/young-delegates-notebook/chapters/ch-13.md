## 13. Common Mistakes

You've made it far enough to be dangerous. This chapter lists the traps that catch smart newcomers, what each one costs, and how to step around them. Read it once now, and again before your first meeting.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- Showing up: why you announce yourself, and what changes after your first meeting
- Your first paper: why core language can wait, what evidence to bring, and who else is working on the problem
- In the room: when to abstain, what to keep private, when to object, how consensus works, and how much to talk
- After the poll: why you follow subgroup direction, and why no single yes is the finish line

</div>

### 13.1 Showing Up Without Announcing

The first trap is simply appearing. Chapter 2 said you can start without permission, and it's tempting to stretch that to the meeting itself. But guests give the convener at least a week's notice, and members register ahead. Show up cold, and the first thing the convener learns about you is that you didn't ask.

The same trap waits a meeting later. Guest status covers your first meeting only, so coming back needs a seat through a national body. Wait until the first meeting is over to ask, and the next one may come before your seat does.

### 13.2 Proposing Core Language Features Too Early

Your first proposal probably shouldn't change the core language. That's often where the itch is, and a language feature looks like the real prize.

But core changes face the highest bar of all, as chapter 9 said, and a cold first attempt usually fails. The room remembers how it first met you, too. Start with something smaller, and bring the big idea once people know your work.

### 13.3 Writing an Idea-Only Paper

An idea with no examples, alternatives, or proof wastes the room's time. Yours is so clear to you that examples feel like padding. But the room can only weigh what's on the page. A thin paper gets a vague poll, and you've spent a scarce agenda slot learning nothing.

No formal rule demands an implementation, but the room expects one, even on a first paper, as chapter 9 said.

### 13.4 Skipping the Search for Competing Papers

Someone may already be working on your problem. It's easy to skip the search when your idea seems new and the archive looks endless. If you don't check, you've set up a fight you didn't need.

The Direction Group asks for "no surprise competitors" in [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf). Search the mailings, write to the other authors, and say in your paper that you did. Talk first, and the rival may become your coauthor.

### 13.5 Voting on Things You Haven't Followed

Chapter 8 warned you off voting on a paper you never read. The version that catches careful people is subtler: you read R0 last spring, and the poll is on R3. You think you know it. But the design moved, and you'd be voting on a paper that no longer exists.

Either way, your vote counts the same as one from someone who read every revision, so it muddies the signal. Don't hide in neutral: it means you read the paper and landed in the middle. If you haven't read the revision being polled, abstain (the honest non-vote from chapter 8).

### 13.6 Quoting Reflectors or Notes Publicly

A great line from the reflector or a session begs to be posted. But the closed lists, the wiki, and the room are off the public record, and quoting them is a real breach. People speak freely because what they say stays private. Leak once, and you stop hearing the candid version.

Describe what was said, never who said it, as chapter 7 put it, and wait until the meeting is over to share a session's poll questions and numbers. Never post from inside a session, even in your own words.

### 13.7 Raising Concerns Too Late

Saving your objection for the closing plenary gains nothing. Waiting seems polite, and nobody wants to stop a room that's clearly happy. But by plenary the design is settled, and a surprise objection spends credibility you'll want for the next fight.

Raise it in the subgroup, with a paper, while the design can still bend. Past that, chapter 8's escalation path closes at 5 p.m. the evening before plenary.

### 13.8 Expecting Majority Rule

Counting hands and expecting the bigger number to win is wrong here. Nearly every vote you've ever cast worked that way, so it's a natural mistake. The committee runs on consensus, the absence of sustained opposition, not majority rule.

A determined minority can stall a paper, and a bare majority can't simply outvote it. Win the count and brush past the objections, and they'll be waiting at the next meeting. When you're the one objecting, make your case with evidence and expect to be heard. But being heard isn't a veto.

### 13.9 Talking Too Much Too Soon

At your first meetings, speaking constantly works against you. You've done the reading and have thoughts on all of it, so your hand goes up a fourth time before lunch. By Wednesday, nobody turns to look when it does.

You build trust by learning the room before you fill it with your voice. Listen first, and speak when you have something specific, like a worked example or a fact the room hasn't heard yet.

### 13.10 Ignoring Subgroup Direction

When a room gives you direction, ignoring it stalls your paper. You'll be sure the room was wrong, and quietly going your own way in the next revision will look like a shortcut. The people who asked for the change will read that revision first.

The design rooms exist to set direction, and working against it keeps the opposition alive. Follow the guidance, or reopen it in that same room with a paper and new evidence, but don't route around it.

### 13.11 Declaring Victory Too Soon

The first poll comes back warm, and people clap you on the shoulder in the hallway. After months of work, you'd swear it's the finish. It's closer to the start. Ease off now, and the next release leaves without your paper.

Count the gates still ahead. A warm poll means keep going. Design approval comes later, and it only starts what chapter 10 called "the other 80%." The wording rooms go over every word, plenary votes the text into the working draft, and the countries ballot the whole draft.

Budget for every stage. Even a published standard is only text until the compilers build it. Don't celebrate until it ships in a compiler you can download.

<div id="short-version">

Email the convener before you go, and line up a national body before your second meeting. Start smaller than the core language. Give every paper examples, the alternatives you weighed, and code, even the first one. Search for competing papers first.

In the room, abstain on any revision you haven't read. Describe what was said, never who said it, and never post live. Object early, in the subgroup, with a paper. Answer every objection, because the bigger number doesn't win here. And say less than you want to.

Follow the direction a room gives you, or reopen it there with new evidence. Treat every yes as one gate passed, with more ahead, all the way to the compilers.

</div>

One mistake isn't on the list, because it never feels like one: confusing being busy with making a difference.

Chapter 7 warned you about one packed week. Now stretch it to five years. Your calendar is full of telecons, and your paper is at R9. Every poll says keep going. And not one person writing C++ has used a line of it.

Chapter 2 called showing up the secret, and it is. It gets you in, but it isn't the work. Showing up and passing polls can feel like impact, but a feature that never ships helped nobody. Keep your eyes on the people who use C++.

Last, don't treat this notebook as scripture. The committee's rules and habits keep changing, sometimes from one meeting to the next, so parts of it will age. Read each new [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) and each trip report, and trust them over anything here.

I wrote this for you, back when you were only wondering how to join. This is where my notes end and yours begin. Mark it up with what you learn the hard way, and someday press it into a newcomer's hands.

By now you know the whole notebook in four sentences: keep your wits about you, be skeptical, demand evidence, and put the users first. And I'm still glad you're here. Now go do the work, and do it well.
