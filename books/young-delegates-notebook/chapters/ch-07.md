## 7. In the Room

The first time in the room, you'll worry about doing something wrong. Don't. Once you know the unwritten rules, and a few written ones, you can relax and pay attention to the work.

### 7.1 The Opening Plenary

The week opens with the whole committee in one room. This is the *opening plenary*. The officers say hello, first-time attendees introduce themselves, the agenda and the updated working drafts get approved, and then everyone scatters to the subgroups.

Don't expect drama here. The opening plenary is short and mostly logistics. The one part that's yours is the introduction. You stand, say your name and where you're from, and sit back down. The room nods and moves on. The work starts in the rooms right after.

### 7.2 Inside a Session

Most sessions run on the same loop. You slip into a seat near the back. The chair, who runs the room, calls up a paper, and its author presents. Then hands go up, and the chair keeps a queue of who speaks next.

Questions and pushback follow, and the author answers. Often the discussion ends in a quick poll of the room, which chapter 8 explains. Then the next paper comes up. Wording rooms run the loop on the text itself. After a few sessions, you'll see each step coming before the chair says a word.

### 7.3 Cycling Between Rooms

Keep your home room, but follow the paper. The room you picked in chapter 6 is still your base. When a paper you care about comes up somewhere else, go with it. Each room's agenda on the wiki shows when that paper is up.

Before you go, read that room's papers too. Walk in cold, and you'll spend the session decoding instead of listening. The discussion moves fast and won't recap for you.

### 7.4 Meeting Etiquette

The room runs on small courtesies. Don't dominate the discussion, and don't reopen a point the room has moved past. Keep your laptop for notes, not for distractions that pull your focus.

Listen more than you talk at first. Spend your early sessions learning how the room thinks.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

You'll earn your voice faster by using it sparingly. Save it for the point nobody else in the room is making.

</div>

### 7.5 How to Speak

When your turn in the queue comes, make one point, and keep it to about two minutes. The room has a lot to get through, and short is respected.

Two habits help everyone follow you. Say your name before you start, because the scribe and the remote attendees need it. Use the microphone, because without it the online side of the room can't hear you. Every room has one somewhere, so find it before you raise your hand.

### 7.6 Confidentiality and the Code of Conduct

What happens in the room mostly stays in the room, and that protects you. You can float a half-formed idea, or change your mind midweek, and never see it quoted online. That's the *confidentiality rule*.

You don't live-tweet or blog from the sessions, record them, or photograph other people's screens. A screenshot of the slides for your own notes is fine.

Quoting works like the reflector rule from chapter 4, with the same exceptions. Poll questions and their numbers are free to share once the meeting is over, as the March 2026 minutes, [N5040](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/n5040.pdf), spell out.

For everything else, paraphrase. When you write up the week afterward, describe what was said, never who said it.

WG21 follows two codes together: the [ISO Code of Ethics and Conduct](https://www.iso.org/publication/PUB100011.html) and the [IEC Code of Conduct](https://iec.ch/basecamp/iec-code-conduct-technical-work). They cover how you treat people in meetings and on social media. The short version is to be decent and assume good faith.

A slide at the start of every meeting sums them up. In a room drawn from dozens of countries, one line is worth taping to your laptop: "be aware that jokes and humor may not translate." If something goes wrong, [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) points you to ISO's process for reporting a concern.

### 7.7 Note-Taking

Every session has to be minuted, and anyone can be asked to do it. SD-4 says everyone present "should be prepared to take minutes." So you might be handed the keyboard on day one. Say yes.

The minute taker is called the scribe, and no job gets you inside a room faster. Writing an argument down is different from hearing it, because you have to understand it well enough to sum it up. A week of it teaches you what your room is wrestling with. People also remember who helped.

To volunteer, put your hand up when the chair asks for a scribe. The notes usually go straight onto the room's page in the meeting wiki. Don't try for a transcript. Get the gist of each point, and get every poll's exact wording and numbers right, since those are what people quote later.

The scribe also gets a small superpower. Everyone else waits their turn to speak, but you can usually skip the queue for one reason. If you missed a point, ask the speaker to repeat it, because the minutes have to be right. That lets you ask the question everyone else was too proud to ask.

### 7.8 Where Relationships Form

The hallway is not break time. A lot of the real work happens there, between sessions. People sort out disagreements over coffee that would stall a formal room.

Meals work the same way. Lunch and dinner groups form on their own, so join one. Introducing yourself over a meal is normal and expected, not pushy.

<div id="watch-out">

By midweek, the people at your lunch table will feel like friends. That's the good part, and it's also the trap. Liking people makes it easy to agree with them. Start nodding along, and you lose the one thing you walked in with: a fresh eye.

</div>

### 7.9 Get Out of Your Lane

Once your home room feels familiar, spend some time in rooms outside your specialty. The Direction Group's [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) puts it plainly: "try to spend at least one day each meeting in a WG that isn't 'your own.'" You'll build trust across groups and see how the whole machine fits together.

Two habits earn respect fast. Serve before you push: scribe a session, or tell an author what you think of their paper. And when someone says "that's just how it works," ask how other groups like the [Internet Engineering Task Force](https://www.ietf.org) or the [World Wide Web Consortium](https://www.w3.org) handle the same thing.

Stay skeptical, and question the room's assumptions, including your own.

<div id="short-version">

Keep a home room, but follow the papers you care about when they move, and read them before you walk in. Listen more than you talk at first. When you do speak, say your name, use the microphone, and make one short point.

Keep the room off the public record: describe what was said, never who said it. Be decent to everyone. Say yes when you're asked to take minutes. Get to know people over meals, and spend a day outside your own room. Through it all, question the room's assumptions and your own.

</div>

You'll pick up the room's rhythm faster than you expect. Then it pulls at you: sessions back to back, evenings that run late, poll after poll. A packed week passes for progress, even when nothing you care about moved. On the flight home, ask what moved. Next time, ask whether you helped move it.

The next mystery is how all this talk turns into decisions.
