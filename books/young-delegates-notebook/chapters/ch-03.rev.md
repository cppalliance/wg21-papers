## 4. How Papers Work

All the committee's work flows through one kind of object: the paper. Learn how papers work, and the rest of the machine makes sense. This chapter shows you what a paper is, how to find one, and how to read it.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics -->

### 4.1 Nothing Happens Without a Paper

One rule explains most of the committee, and it's the committee's own words: “If a proposal doesn't have a paper, it doesn't exist.” A **paper** is a written proposal or report, and it's the unit of all the work. A paper needs three things before a room can act on it. The paper has to exist, it has to arrive on time, and someone has to be there to present it. Miss one and the room moves on.

Discussion can expose a flaw, improve a design or persuade someone to change their vote. A conversation in a hallway can do any of these things. The paper gives that discussion a shared reference: what is being proposed, why it matters, and what questions remain. As the proposal develops, its author revises the paper to reflect that work.

### 4.2 What a Paper Is, and the Numbers It Carries

Papers serve different purposes. These are informal labels; I’ll call them ask-papers and inform-papers here. An ask-paper proposes a change to the working draft. An inform-paper provides information or analysis without proposing such a change.

A paper can explain what should change in C++ and why. It can also include proposed wording: the normative text to add, remove, or replace in the Working Draft. A separate kind, the standing document (§ 6.9), holds the committee's own rules instead of proposing anything.

Every paper carries a number, and the first letter tells its kind. A **P-number** marks a proposal, like P1234. An **N-number** is the ISO document number; it goes on the working draft of the standard itself (for example N5054, "Working Draft, Programming Languages -- C++"), the editors' reports, meeting minutes, and invitations. Before 2015 every paper was an N-paper, so older references use them. A D-number identifies a draft version of a paper that has not yet been published in a committee mailing as a P-paper.

After the number comes a revision, like R0 for the first version, then R1, R2, and up. Read the newest one you can find. The revision tells you history, and not necessarily the quality. R0 is brand new and untested in the room. A paper at R5 has been reworked five times, which may signal persistence or extensive design change.

### 4.3 Finding Any Paper, and the Mailings They Arrive In

You don't hunt for papers by hand. [wg21.link](https://wg21.link) turns any paper number into a link. Type the number after the slash, in lowercase, and it takes you there. For P2300 revision 10, you write [wg21.link/p2300r10](https://wg21.link/p2300r10). Mara Bos has run this service for years.

Papers reach you in batches called **mailings**. Mailings come out roughly monthly (nine in 2025), and the two largest each cycle are the pre-meeting and post-meeting mailings. The committee puts out several hundred new P-numbers a year, and more documents than that once revisions are counted. The papers live at [open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/), the official archive; § 5.3 covers the sites that make them easier to find. An unofficial C++ Alliance service, [wg21.org/mailing](http://wg21.org/mailing), provides a useful tool for accessing present and past mailings.

Timing decides what the committee can act on. Each meeting has a pre-meeting mailing deadline, a month ahead of it. Usually a paper that misses the deadline isn't actionable, so it waits for the next round.

### 4.4 The Paper Lifecycle

A paper that makes it follows a set path. It starts as an idea and ends in the published standard, and most papers stop somewhere in between. Here are the steps in order:

1. You submit the paper to the mailing.
2. A study group discusses the idea and shapes it.
3. A design group decides whether the direction is right.
4. A wording group turns the approved design into normative standard text.
5. At the closing plenary, the committee votes the proposed changes into the working draft (the living text that becomes the next standard).
6. The draft goes out to the national bodies as a Committee Draft, or CD, for comments, and the committee resolves every comment. (C++26 drew 411 of them.)
7. National bodies vote on the Draft International Standard (DIS). After the required approvals, ISO publishes the International Standard (IS).

Each step is a place to stop. A paper can stall, get sent back, or quietly die at any rung. Reaching the top takes years, and many papers never do. The committee's own [eleven-stage version](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal) of this list is worth reading once.

### 4.5 Design Review, Wording Review, and Defect Reports

The committee reviews a paper in two separate stages. **Design review** decides whether it wants the proposed change in C++ and how that change should work. **Wording review** checks that the proposed text expresses the agreed design precisely and completely. These take different mindsets. Design review weighs whether C++ wants the feature at all. Wording review assumes the answer is yes and hunts for gaps in the spec text. The two happen in order, not together. First a design group approves the direction. Then a wording group takes over and writes the standard text.

Not every paper proposes something new. Some just fix mistakes. The standard ships with bugs, and they get fixed. A suspected mistake in the standard or working draft can be raised as an **issue**. The committee reviews it and decides whether a correction is needed. It repairs wording rather than adding a feature. Separate public lists track issues in the core language and the library, recording each issue’s status. Wording groups work through these issues, and the project editors apply the resolutions approved at plenary.

### 4.6 Reading a Paper Critically

Always read the newest revision. An old one makes you raise a concern the author already fixed. When a paper has a new revision, compare it against the last version to see what changed. The mailing list gets hundreds of papers, so it’s okay to skim over or skip any papers you are not interested in.

Run a few questions on any paper you read:

- What problem does it solve, and does it say so?
- Does it weigh other options, or pretend none exist?
- Has anyone built it, or is it only an idea?
- What old code breaks, and how do users move forward?
- Is the wording clear on the hard cases?

If the paper leaves an important question unanswered, raise it with the author. A good paper should address likely concerns and acknowledge what still needs work.

### 4.7 The Documents Everyone Should Read: SD-4 and the Direction Papers

The following documents are worth reading before almost anything else. [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), "WG21 Practices and Procedures," is the committee's own rulebook, the practices everyone is expected to know. It covers polls, consensus, deadlines, ballots, and escalation, and this notebook quotes it throughout.

The Direction Group is a small group of experienced committee participants who recommend priorities for WG21. Three of its papers are particularly useful here.

P2000R5, “Direction for ISO C++” (February 2026), discusses the language’s long-term direction and how the committee works. It covers trust, proposal quality, and the competing concerns that shape decisions. Chapters 9, 11, and 13 draw on this guidance.

P5000R1, “Direction for ISO C++29” (May 2026), recommends priorities for the C++29 cycle. It asks chairs to help bring competing proposals together and stresses the importance of completing work. Finishing three proposals, it argues, is better than reaching the deadline with six that are only 80% ready.

P4024R0, “Guidance on Building Consensus and Converging Proposals,” advises authors working on overlapping or competing proposals. It encourages them to collaborate early, ideally producing a unified proposal or a joint paper explaining the differences between their approaches (§ 12.1).
