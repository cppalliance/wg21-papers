## 13. Championing Your Paper

A paper doesn't pass itself. Getting to yes is a long, social campaign, and this chapter is your field guide. It's the part newcomers underestimate most.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics -->

### 13.1 Before the Room: Presocialization and the Collaboration Ladder

Most papers are won before they're presented. **Presocialization** means talking up your idea in hallways and on the lists, building support ahead of the room. A cold presentation to a surprised room usually stalls. The decisive moment is the five minutes before a poll. The chair's summary, the first voice to speak, and the room's mood shape the result. Line up a few supporters who'll speak early.

Supporters are not conjured in the week before a poll. They are made over years by a ladder that runs in the opposite direction from the one newcomers expect. It starts with other people's papers, not yours. Find the proposals in your area that like-minded people are already carrying, read them properly, and send the authors a real review: what is unclear, what breaks, what you tried on Compiler Explorer. A careful review is rare enough that authors remember who sent one. Do it again on the next revision, and you will find yourself asked to help with the paper itself, at first with a section or an implementation, then as a co-author. Each rung buys something you cannot buy any other way. The people whose papers you reviewed will review yours, honestly, because you have shown them what an honest review looks like; the people you co-authored with will speak for your paper in a room you cannot be in; and your name in their acknowledgements tells the room, before you say a word, that you are part of a group that does its homework. The Direction Group's advice runs the same way. Its standing paper asks every member to read several papers in each mailing that are not aimed at their own working group and to spend at least a day of every meeting in a room that is not theirs, and P4024R0 counts a paper's reviewers, named and drawn from different domains, as evidence of consensus on a par with co-authorship.

### 13.2 Finding and Being a Champion

Every paper needs a **champion**, the person who presents and pushes it (§ 2.6). Often that's you, the author, but it doesn't have to be. If you can't attend, find someone who knows the work deeply, not just someone to read it aloud. You can also champion someone else's paper. Carrying a good idea you didn't write builds trust and skill. It's one of the fastest ways for a newcomer to become useful.

### 13.3 The First Gate: "Do We Want It at All?"

The first discussion of your paper is the scary one. The room asks "do we want this at all?" before it touches any detail. A no here ends the journey. So frame your idea as solving a problem the committee already cares about. Tie it to the Direction Group's priorities for the cycle, P5000 (§ 4.7). A paper that matches the agenda gets heard. One that doesn't waits.

### 13.4 When the Poll Goes Against You

A poll against you is not a death sentence. Being sent back for more work is the normal path, not a rejection. You take the feedback, revise, and return with a stronger paper. Know the one risky move: reopening a settled question. **Re-litigation** is re-debating something the room already decided, and it costs you credibility. Reopen only with genuinely new evidence, not the same argument again.

Getting direction from a room and then ignoring it stalls your paper. The design rooms exist to set direction, and working against it keeps the opposition alive. Follow the guidance, or reopen it in that same room with a paper, but don't route around it.

### 13.5 Negotiating: Back Pocket and Max-Min

Always carry a fallback. A **back pocket alternative** is a second design you're ready to offer if your first choice is rejected. Walking in with one keeps you from leaving empty-handed. When the room is split, shrink the ask. The **max-min solution** is the smallest version everyone can accept. A small win that ships beats a big proposal that stalls.

### 13.6 Disagreement vs. Opposition, and When to Withdraw

Learn to tell two things apart. Disagreement is a technical objection, usually with a paper behind it. Opposition is a person set against your idea, and the two need different responses. Sometimes the right move is to stop. If the room clearly doesn't want it and won't budge, withdraw the paper with grace. Pouring energy into a dead idea costs you the credibility you'll need later.

### 13.7 How Reputation Works, and How the Presenter Is Judged

In a room of volunteers, reputation is currency. People can't read every paper, so they weigh who wrote it. A trusted name gets the benefit of the doubt, while a new one gets extra scrutiny. You build it in small steps. Start with modest, useful contributions, become the reliable expert in one narrow area, and spend your credibility only on the battles that matter. Reputation compounds when you're right over time.

The committee weighs the presenter, not only the proposal. Are you reasonable, willing to concede a fair point, someone people want to work with? Those signals shape how your paper lands. So don't be the obstacle. Causing needless delay, or escalating every dispute, marks you as hard to work with. The formal objection tools exist (§ 9.7), but reaching for them often erodes your credibility. Persistence does pay, though. **Procedural momentum** is the benefit a paper earns from many revisions and prior polls. By the fourth or fifth revision, the room tends to assume it'll pass, so steady iteration works in your favor.

### 13.8 The Long Game

Set your clock to years, not months. The train runs every three years, and most features ride more than one. Patience isn't optional here. It's the job. The famous features all took ages. Coroutines, modules, and the executors work that became std::execution each ran many years and many revisions. Stackful coroutines have waited over a decade and still aren't in: the current attempt is [P0876](https://wg21.link/p0876), "fiber_context - fibers without scheduler", at revision 24 in July 2026, with objections still being filed against it.

### 13.9 The Structural Headwinds

Some headwinds aren't about you. The **bandwidth problem** is plain: far more papers arrive than anyone can review. Several hundred new papers a year is more than anyone follows outside their own room. Another is the **expert bubble**. Papers are often written by experts for experts, which makes it hard for newcomers and outside ideas to break in. The review system is one crack in that wall, so use it. Resources tilt the field, too. Proposals with paid committee time and funded implementations move faster than volunteer efforts. It's not a conspiracy, just the weight of who can afford to show up every cycle.

### 13.10 The Emotional Side

Having your paper picked apart stings, and that's normal. Don't take it personally. The room argues hard about ideas, not about you. Burnout is real here, not a weakness. The implementers said so in writing in January 2026 (P3962R0, § 6.9), and good people leave because of it. Set boundaries, pace yourself, and know when to step back. Protecting your energy is protecting your impact. The committee runs for decades, and it needs you for the long haul, not for one heroic burst. Pace beats sprint.
