## BOX 5: FROM ONE DELEGATE'S NOTEBOOK

- status: dropped
- section: 1.3 The Oath Is the Ideal. Here Is the Day Job.

**FROM ONE DELEGATE'S NOTEBOOK**

In 2014 the committee moved to remove trigraphs, the three-character sequences like ??= that let code be written on keyboards and character sets that lack certain symbols. The delegate’s employer, a large vendor whose customers still ran on such systems, instructed him to keep trigraphs in the language. He did the job properly: He cowrote the position paper, argued the case in the rooms, and made sure the committee understood what removal would break.

The motion came before the closing plenary in Urbana-Champaign in November 2014. The delegate now had to decide which hat he was wearing. Speaking for his country rather than his company, he withdrew the national objection and let the motion proceed.

The minutes record that the vendor would oppose the motion and the country would not. The poll was 57 in favor, 2 against, and 10 abstaining. The room thanked him for being gracious in opposition and allowing the motion to proceed.

Trigraphs left the language in C++17. It was not easy, it carried a real risk to his standing with the people who paid for his seat, and it is exactly what the first line of the oath asks for.

Here is the mirror image, from the same delegate. In 2007 the committee was writing the memory model for C++11. The draft on the table offered sequentially consistent atomics, the easiest kind to reason about, and a slightly weaker acquire-release form, with nothing looser. His employer's processors, and at the time every game console, ran on a far more relaxed memory order and got their performance from it; a standard with no relaxed mode would have taxed every one of those machines to make the easy model look native. So he was sent to draw a line: A relaxed ordering had to go in. He and his colleagues wrote the paper, argued it through the year, and made clear their country would vote No on the whole C++11 standard rather than ship without it. A senior voice whose word carried weight put the principle in one line: A standard should not single out any platform for disadvantage. The committee's own memory-model authors later wrote that sequential consistency was expensive enough on some processors that an experts-only alternative for performance-critical code was necessary. The relaxed ordering went into the model adopted at Kona in October 2007, and it ships in C++11 as memory_order_relaxed. The compromise was the default: Sequentially consistent stayed the ordering you get when you say nothing, so novices get the model they can reason about, and experts get the one their hardware needs.

The two cases look like opposites, but they illustrate the same rule. An employer’s need that is also a real user’s need belongs in the standard. The delegate supported his case with a paper and a principle the room could accept. He helped reach a compromise while making clear that his country would not accept a standard without relaxed ordering.

What he did not do, in either case, was hide the instruction. The room knew whose need it was, and judged the argument anyway.

## BOX 6: RULE OF THUMB

**RULE OF THUMB**

Do your employer's case justice in the room, then vote as the language needs. Sometimes those are the same vote. Losing well is part of the job; winning well means the room can say why you were right.

## BOX 10: THE SHORT VERSION

- status: dropped
- section: 1.8 From Standard to Compiler

**THE SHORT VERSION**

The standard is a text, not a program, and it comes as a working draft, a published IS, and experimental TSs and white papers. Whatever you add stays, and sixteen million people carry it. Cite the bracketed labels, not the section numbers. A feature exists when a compiler ships it, which gives the implementers a quiet veto.
