## 5. How to Participate Remotely

You don't have to fly anywhere to start. Most of the committee's life happens online, between meetings. This chapter shows you how to take part from your desk, today.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics, from working with no plane ticket to the three homes for a library -->

### 5.1 You Can Take Part from Your Desk

Plenty of useful work happens with no plane ticket. You can read, review, and discuss from home, and none of it needs a meeting badge. One thing you cannot do from home is scribe a face-to-face meeting: Chairs pick the minute-taker from the people in the room, and a remote attendee is asked only when the whole session is virtual, as the telecons in § 5.6 are. The lowest-effort start is reviewing a proposal. Read a proposal that interests you, then email your review to the author or discuss it on a relevant public mailing list.

### 5.2 The Reflector

The committee's main online home is email. Its mailing lists are called the **reflector**, where most discussion happens between meetings. Each subgroup keeps its own list. Attending any meeting, in person or online, qualifies you for the lists, and subscription details are on the wiki (see below), or the secretary can assist. A study-group chair can also add an expert in their field directly, and many study-group lists are publicly readable.

One rule matters from day one. Reflector posts are private, so you can't quote them in public. You can share poll questions and their numbers freely, but a person's words need that person's consent.

Be polite and respectful on the reflectors and in committee chat rooms, especially when you are sure you are right. Email strips the tone out of an argument and leaves the words, and the people reading yours are the same people who will chair your session, review your paper, and count your vote for the next twenty years. A thread has a long memory. A sharp reply feels good for a minute and costs you a reader for a decade; a courteous one, on a point where you disagree, is remembered as evidence that you can be worked with. Politeness and respect will get you a very long way here, further than being clever will.

### 5.3 The Official Sites, and One Unofficial One

Three websites cover most of what you need. [isocpp.org](https://isocpp.org) is the public-facing site, home to the standing documents and committee news. Start there for anything official. [open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/) is the official archive of every paper and draft. You can browse the archive by date, but it has no built-in search.

[wg21.org](https://wg21.org) is a friendlier front door to the same papers. The C++ Alliance, which publishes this notebook, runs it as an unofficial public service to search and sort the mailing by group, author, and revision. The papers still live on open-std.org; this view only makes them easier to find.

### 5.4 The Wiki and the Chat

The committee runs its meetings off the internal [wiki](http://wiki). It holds the agendas, schedules, video-call links, and poll pages for each meeting. If you attend, you'll spend a lot of your time that week looking at it. You can request access once you're a registered member. Edit the wiki only as directed by a chair.

When things move fast, people use chat. Mattermost, at [chat.isocpp.org](https://chat.isocpp.org), is the committee's own real-time chat, and during meetings it keeps the parallel rooms in touch. It is limited to WG21 participants, with an invitation code on the wiki, so it opens up after you are in.

For the wider community, the [C++ Slack](https://cppalliance.org/slack/) and the [#include <C++> Discord](https://www.includecpp.org) are open to anyone. They are friendly, public, and full of people who'll answer questions, and they are gentle places to learn the culture. The same rule holds in all of them, the committee's own chat and the public ones: courtesy and respect, in every message. Chat is faster than email and even easier to misread, and it is read by the same people.

### 5.5 Floating Ideas: std-proposals

Before you write a formal proposal, float the idea first. [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) is a public list for exactly that. You describe your idea and see what people think. This step saves you months. You learn fast whether anyone wants the idea, and you get early feedback that makes the real proposal stronger.

### 5.6 Telecons, Electronic Polls, and Following a Live Meeting

The big meetings happen three times a year, but the work never stops. Between them, subgroups hold online meetings called **telecons**. The committee counts about thirty such topic-specific virtual meetings in a typical month. You find them on the committee's shared calendar; the wiki lists the schedule and the links. Pick a subgroup you care about and sit in.

Some decisions even happen between meetings, by electronic polls. The library side especially runs polls online to settle smaller questions without burning floor time. The procedure is [P2195](https://wg21.link/p2195), "Electronic Straw Polls", and the Library Evolution Working Group (LEWG) publishes each round's results as a numbered paper (for example [P3712R0](https://wg21.link/p3712r0), "2025-05 Library Evolution Polls"), so you can read what was polled and how it went.

When the live meeting does happen, you can still follow along. Meetings are hybrid, so you can join the rooms by video. You won't catch everything from afar; the hallway talk, where a lot gets settled, is hard to join remotely. After each meeting, the results come out fast. Several delegates post public trip reports, like [Herb Sutter's blog](https://herbsutter.com), and every session is minuted. One catch if you're far away is time zones. A meeting on another continent can run through your night. Pick the sessions worth losing sleep over, and read the rest later.

### 5.7 Open-Source Help: The Beman Project and Tools

You can contribute to C++ standardisation without attending committee meetings. The [Beman Project](https://github.com/bemanproject) helps the development of open-source implementations of proposed standard library features. You can help write and test those implementations, or use them in your own projects and report problems.

### 5.8 Three Homes for a Library: Boost, Beman, and the Standard

Newcomers hear three names in the same breath and assume they are one thing. They are three different promises, and a library author should know which one they are asking for.

**Boost** is the oldest. It was founded in 1998 by Beman Dawes, Robert Klarer and David Abrahams as a home for high-quality, peer-reviewed C++ libraries. Many familiar standard library facilities grew out of Boost, including shared_ptr, regex and filesystem. That spirit continues in the Boost community today under the C++ Alliance. Boost's defining feature is the formal review: a library enters only after a public, week-long peer review by the community, run by a volunteer review manager, and the standard of that review is high. What Boost does not promise is that a library will stop changing. There is no ABI or API stability rule across Boost releases; a library grows as its author sees fit, and the only brake is the author's own duty to a body of existing users. That freedom is the point. A Boost library can fix a design mistake that a standard library would have to live with forever.

**The Beman Project**, named in memory of Beman Dawes, is younger and narrower, and it is one of the two organizations that sponsor the free INCITS seats of § 2.5. Beman develops implementations of proposed standard library features, using namespaces under `beman`. Developers can try the proposed interfaces in their own programs and report what works and what needs changing. That experience helps authors improve their proposals and gives the committee evidence to assess them. The Beman Project’s libraries are implementations of a specific proposal and exist to assist that proposal across the gap between "designed" and "standardised". Their success is measured by what the standard adopts.

**The ISO C++ standard library** is the destination, and it makes the strongest promise of all: a feature that enters it is meant never to change in a way that breaks anyone, in source or in binary, for as long as the standard exists (§ 1.4, § 14.4). That promise is why the bar is so high and why the committee says no so often.

Here is the way to read the three, in my judgment. They differ in exactly one thing, what each promises about change. Boost promises quality and peer review but reserves the right to evolve, so it suits a library whose design is still learning from its users, and it suits an author who values the right to fix things over the right to be permanent. Beman promises fidelity to a paper and is temporary by design, so it suits a library whose design is settled and whose next step is a vote, and it is the right place to prove a proposal rather than to publish a product. The standard promises permanence, which is a gift to the ten-million-line codebase and a cage for the design that turns out to be wrong; std::regex is the standing reminder (§ 1.4). The mistake to avoid is treating them as a ladder every library must climb. Most good C++ libraries will never be in the standard, should not be, and lose nothing by it. Ask what your users need from you: freedom to change, a rehearsal for standardisation, or a form that will never move. That answer, not prestige, picks the home.
