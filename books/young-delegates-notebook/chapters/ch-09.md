## 9. What Goes into a Proposal

You have an idea you love, and you think it belongs in C++. The room won't care how you feel about it. It will ask what you can prove. This chapter is the cold shower and the toolkit: what a proposal must bring to earn the committee's time, and why the bar is so high.

### 9.1 What Belongs in a Paper

The low bar is all in your hands: a paper, on time, with someone to present it. Then you ask the chair for a slot on the agenda, and chapter 10 shows you how. But getting a yes is a different game.

A real proposal makes four arguments. It shows the problem is worth solving, lays out the design, weighs the alternatives, and proves the thing works. Miss one and a reviewer will find the hole. And do your homework: cite the earlier papers on the same ground, because the room remembers them.

You don't need coauthors, and plenty of papers have none. In the [February 2026 mailing](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/#mailing2026-02), more than half the papers had one author. The Direction Group, the small senior group that steers the committee, agrees.

The group's [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf) says a paper from "a single author citing feedback from diverse reviewers" is just as valid as a coauthored one. So thank your reviewers by name, and say what kind of C++ each one writes. That shows your design survived readers outside your own corner.

### 9.2 The Three Pillars

Three habits make those four arguments land. Call them the *three pillars*: lead with concrete examples, argue from clear principles, and show the alternatives you weighed. [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), the practices guide from chapter 3, asks every proposal for all three. It calls them the difference between "someone's cool idea" and "a real proposal."

A paper missing a pillar feels thin. Examples do the heavy lifting, and alternatives are where you'll be tempted to cheat, so each gets its own section below.

Principles are the pillar that's easiest to skip. Name the design rules your proposal follows. SD-4 wants your design to fit the language's "principles and design philosophy," and "explicitly not" its quirks. It even suggests citing Bjarne Stroustrup's book on how C++ was designed, which chapter 12 introduces.

### 9.3 The Abstract as Elevator Pitch

Most readers decide from the abstract alone. So write it as an elevator pitch a busy stranger can grasp. The first sentence should state your finding, not wind up to it.

Keep it honest, though. Promise only what the paper delivers, because reviewers punish hype. A title that names the result beats a title that names the topic.

### 9.4 Tony Tables

One of the committee's favorite tools is the *Tony Table*, a side-by-side of old code and new. You put the current way on the left and your way on the right. The reader sees the win without you claiming it.

The name honors Tony Van Eerd, who came up with the format. His [tables of C++17 features](https://github.com/tvaneerd/cpp17_in_TTs) show it at its best.

Make the table honest to earn trust. Include the cases where your design isn't shorter or clearer. A table that hides its weak spots gets caught and loses the room.

### 9.5 Show the Alternatives: The Steel Man

Don't dodge the alternatives. Attack your own idea first. A *steel man* is the strongest possible case against your proposal, stated fairly, which you then answer with evidence.

Build two of them. One argues that no standard feature is needed at all, that a library would do. The other argues that a rival design is better, and then you show why yours wins.

This is the hard part. You've lived with this design for months, and you love it. Now you write the best case against it, so fair a rival would sign it. Go easy on it, and someone in the room won't. They'll make that case out loud, with your paper on the screen.

Don't stack the deck. The Direction Group's [P2000R5](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) says that showing only your design's strengths and only a rival's weaknesses "is not acceptable."

The strongest answer to both steel men is the same: evidence the room can check.

### 9.6 Implementation Experience

Nothing beats proof that your idea works. *Implementation experience* means you've built the feature and used it, ideally in more than one place. The committee wants that proof before it freezes anything forever.

No rule demands it, though. The [submit-a-proposal page](https://isocpp.org/std/submit-a-proposal) says there's no ISO requirement to build it first, only to win consensus. But it adds that "consensus is easier to achieve when there's a working implementation."

<div id="watch-out">

Optional in the rules, expected in the room. Show up with no code and you're asking everyone to take your word for it, and that's a hard sell. Build your feature anyway, even for your first paper.

</div>

The bar is real but not impossible. For a library, ship code that compiles, passes tests, and has real users. For a language feature, a rough compiler fork that shows it working is enough to start.

One small piece rides along with new features: the *feature-test macro*. It's a predefined name that lets code check whether a compiler has your feature yet. Including it lets people adopt the feature safely while support spreads. The rules and the full list live in [SD-FeatureTest](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-6-sg10-feature-test-recommendations).

### 9.7 Writing Standard Wording

Standard text isn't prose, it's closer to law. Two small words matter more than they look: *shall* marks a hard requirement, and *should* marks advice. Mixing them up changes what implementers must do.

Point to the right place, too. Cite the stable section labels (the bracketed names from chapter 1) instead of page or section numbers. The numbers shift between drafts. The labels don't.

Don't write your first wording alone. Once the design is settled, SD-4 expects you to find a wording expert to help you draft it. The chairs will help you find one.

CWG or LWG (the wording rooms from chapter 5) then goes through your text with you, fixes what's wrong, and approves it. Have your expert on board before that review starts, not after the room finds the holes.

### 9.8 A Short Tutorial

A great proposal teaches its feature. Include a short tutorial that shows someone how to use it. P2000 asks for one early, because a tutorial keeps a feature meant for "ordinary programmers" from drifting into expert-only territory.

If you can't explain it in a few clear lines, the design may be too complex.

### 9.9 Scope and Dependencies

Keep each paper to one topic. If your title needs the word "and," split it in two. Big, bundled proposals stall, while small focused ones move.

For a large idea, ship it in stages. Land the useful piece first, let people use it, and build the rest on top. Some features even wait on another paper to land before they can.

### 9.10 The High Bar

The burden of proof sits on you, the proposer. The default is no, because a mistake is forever. Being useful isn't enough, so show why only the standard can deliver what you need.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Be convinced by overwhelming evidence and nothing else. Not by the room, not by a big name, and least of all by how much you love your own idea.

</div>

Language features are the hardest of all. A library lives on GitHub if the committee says no, but a language feature has nowhere else to go. So the committee guards the language even more tightly than the library.

Most new-feature proposals don't make it, and that's the system working. Big efforts have stalled or died, even ones that got as far as a TS (the experimental tryout from chapter 1).

The [Networking TS](https://www.iso.org/standard/64030.html) came out in 2018 and still isn't in the standard. In 2021, the library design room's [online polls](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2453r0.html) leaned toward building networking on a different model for asynchronous code. That model, [`std::execution`](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p2300r10.html), didn't land until C++26.

The [Transactional Memory TS](https://www.iso.org/standard/66343.html) never merged. A 2019 paper, [P1875R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p1875r0.pdf), said it "has not been widely implemented and used," and proposed a "lite" version instead.

One stalled over design, the other for lack of proof.

<div id="short-version">

The low bar is in your hands: a paper, on time, and someone to present it. Getting a yes is hard. Make the four arguments, and build them on the three pillars SD-4 asks for: examples, principles, and alternatives.

Show an honest Tony Table, answer two steel men, and prove it works. Once the design is settled, find a wording expert before CWG or LWG reviews your text. Keep to one topic, and expect the default answer to be no.

</div>
