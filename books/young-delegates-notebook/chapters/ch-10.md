## 10. Producing and Submitting Your Paper

You know the bar your idea has to clear. Here's the practical path: the tools, the format, and the route from a first post to the committee's agenda.

### 10.1 Float the Idea First

Before you write a full paper, float the idea. Post a short description to [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) (the public list from chapter 4), covering the problem it solves and the alternatives you weighed. Then see who bites. The official [submit-a-proposal](https://isocpp.org/std/submit-a-proposal) page makes this its first step, too.

This saves you from writing a paper nobody wants. You'll find prior art, hear objections early, and maybe meet a coauthor. Applause on a list is encouragement, and chapter 9's bar still stands.

Floating the idea also finds your competitors before the room does. Check for other papers on the same problem, and talk to their authors first. The Direction Group even has a name for this, the "no surprise competitors" rule in [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf).

Best case, you and the other author merge your designs into one paper. If you can't agree on one design, write a joint paper that compares both and lays out the trade-offs. Chairs can ask for either one before they give your papers more time.

Alone or together, once the idea has interest, build something before you write it up. The strongest part of a paper is proof that it runs.

### 10.2 Prototyping: Compiler Explorer and GitHub

Let readers run your code, don't just describe it. [Compiler Explorer](https://godbolt.org), at godbolt.org, runs C++ in the browser across many compilers. Share a live link and the reader sees it work.

<div id="try-this-today">

Build a live Tony Table. Put the code people write now on Compiler Explorer, where anyone can run it. For a library, paste your prototype beside it. For a language feature, sketch the syntax in a comment for now. Save the link. It's often the first thing a reviewer clicks.

</div>

Put your implementation on [GitHub](https://github.com). A public repo lets people read the code, file issues, and try it themselves. It also tracks your revisions as the design evolves.

### 10.3 Writing the Paper: Tools and Template

You don't format a paper by hand. Most authors use [mpark/wg21](https://github.com/mpark/wg21), which turns Markdown into a committee-styled document, or [Bikeshed](https://github.com/tabatkins/bikeshed), which builds HTML specs. Newer tools like [COWEL](https://github.com/Eisenwave/cowel) and plain LaTeX work too.

Start from a template, which gives you the front matter and the expected sections. The tools above ship their own, and many authors begin there. The submit-a-proposal page also has an *official template*, the "Template for Library Proposals." It was built for library features, but its questions make a good checklist for any paper.

### 10.4 Make It Accessible

Use enough color contrast, a monospace font for code, and clear headings. Don't lean on color alone to make a point.

A clear paper is one the busy delegate can skim on a phone between sessions. Accessible papers get read.

### 10.5 Coauthoring and Revision History

A coauthor splits the writing, can build while you draft, and brings reviewers you don't know yet. You don't need one, as chapter 9 showed. List everyone in the front matter, and name one person as the presenter.

Few papers survive unchanged. Expect to revise, again and again. Each revision (R0, R1, R2) answers the last round of feedback. Reviewers read what changed, so spell out your changes.

### 10.6 Patent Disclosure

ISO has a patent rule, and you must follow it. If you know of a patent that covers your proposal, disclose it before the work advances. The [ISO patent policy](https://www.iso.org/iso-standards-and-patents.html) spells out the duty.

### 10.7 Submitting the Paper: SD-7

[SD-7](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-7-mailing-procedures-and-how-to-write-papers) covers the mechanics: what goes in the header, which file formats work, and when the mailing deadlines fall. Read it once before your first submission.

Remember the basics from chapter 3. Your paper gets a P-number and a revision, and it has to land before the mailing deadline. SD-7 puts that deadline at 2 p.m. UTC on the Monday four weeks before the meeting.

A new paper that misses the deadline waits for the next round. A late update to a paper that made the deadline can still be heard.

You upload the paper at [isocpp.org/papers](https://isocpp.org/papers), which needs an [isocpp.org](https://isocpp.org) login. The system hands you a P-number, or bumps the R on a revision, so make sure your title page matches. A saved draft is only a working copy, so submit the paper itself before the deadline.

Once it's in a mailing, your paper gets an issue in the [GitHub tracker](https://github.com/cplusplus/papers) from chapter 5. Watch it to see where you stand. Some chairs schedule from that tracker, and a label on the issue shows when your paper gets a slot.

### 10.8 Getting on the Agenda

A paper with no presenter goes nowhere. Email the chair (the person running the room) before the meeting to ask for agenda time. Confirm whether you'll present in person or by video.

The chair you want runs the room your paper goes to first. That's usually a study group or one of the evolution rooms from chapter 5. The [committee page](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee) lists every chair. If you can't tell which one is yours, ask any of them, and they'll point you to the right one.

<div id="watch-out">

Don't wait to be invited. A paper in the mailing isn't a paper on the agenda. If you don't ask the chair for a slot, you may find out on Monday that yours was never in the plan. Asking isn't pushy. Waiting can cost you a whole meeting.

</div>

When your slot comes, be ready to present and to listen. You'll show the problem, the design, and the proof, then take questions. The room's reaction steers your next revision.

### 10.9 The Other 80%

Winning design approval (the "this design is right" gate from chapter 8) feels like victory. You'll think you're 80 percent done. The committee's own page on [the life of a proposal](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal) grants you that, from a design point of view. Then it calls the rest "the other 80%."

After design comes the long part. A wording room polishes the exact text, and the closing plenary votes it into the working draft. Then the whole draft goes to the countries, first as a committee draft for comments, then as a Draft International Standard for approval.

Stay with your paper the whole way, and watch for ballot comments that touch it.

<div id="short-version">

Float the idea on [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) first, and talk to anyone working on the same problem. Then build something: put your code on Compiler Explorer and GitHub so people can run it. Write it up with [mpark/wg21](https://github.com/mpark/wg21) or [Bikeshed](https://github.com/tabatkins/bikeshed), and make it easy to read, even on a phone.

Name a presenter, expect to revise again and again, and disclose any patent you know of. Submit by the SD-7 deadline, then ask the chair for agenda time. Design approval will feel like 80 percent of the job. What's left is "the other 80%."

</div>

That's the route, from a short post on [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) to a slot on a chair's schedule. Get R0 into the next mailing before its deadline. Next comes the art of championing it.
