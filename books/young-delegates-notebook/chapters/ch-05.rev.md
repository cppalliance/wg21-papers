## 6. How WG21 Is Structured

WG21 looks like a wall of acronyms at first. EWG, LEWG, and a dozen study groups blur together. By the end of this chapter, that wall turns into a clear map.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list of the chapter's topics, from the rooms and the train model to the implementer veto -->

### 6.1 One Committee, Many Rooms

WG21 does its work in several groups people call the **rooms**. Each room owns one slice of the job, and they meet at the same time, in parallel, all week. Six or seven rooms run at once, so you can't attend them all. You pick one room and follow it. The rooms split into two jobs. Some decide what to build (design), and others write the spec (wording).

### 6.2 The Evolution Rooms and the Wording Rooms

Two rooms decide whether a feature is worth building. **EWG**, the Evolution Working Group, handles the core language. **LEWG**, the Library Evolution Working Group, does the same for the standard library. Both rooms answer the design question.

Two more rooms turn approved designs into exact standard text. **CWG**, the Core Working Group, reviews wording for the core language. **LWG**, the Library Working Group, does the same for the standard library. Both review the text for gaps, ambiguities and inconsistencies. Their concern is whether the wording accurately expresses the design already approved by EWG or LEWG.

### 6.3 Study Groups

New ideas usually start in a **study group**, or SG. A study group focuses on one area, like concurrency or networking, and shapes raw ideas. Each study group is a world of its own, deep in its specialty. When an idea is ready, it graduates to a design room. The convener creates and closes study groups as needs change.

### 6.4 How a Paper Moves Between Groups

A paper climbs a chain of groups. A study group shapes it, a design room approves it, a wording room finalizes it, and the plenary adopts it. Each handoff is a vote to send it onward. Some papers touch more than one room. A feature that changes the language and the library visits both evolution rooms, sometimes together in a joint session. The groups coordinate so nothing falls through the cracks.

### 6.5 The Train Model, and Why Features Rarely Leave

C++ ships on a fixed schedule, and that rule is the **train model**. Every three years a new standard leaves the station with whatever features are ready. C++14, 17, 20, 23, and 26 all rode it. The committee has used this model since 2012, following the delays to C++11. Herb Sutter explains the schedule and its rationale in [P1000](https://wg21.link/p1000), “Proposed C++ IS schedule”.

Even a feature already in the working draft can be removed if it needs further work. In 2019, the committee removed contracts from the C++20 draft because it could not resolve disagreements about the design in time. However it only works before a feature ships. Taking a feature out afterwards is far harder than putting one in. Removing a feature from the working draft requires stronger consensus than adding it. Once the feature appears in a published standard, removing it also risks breaking programs that depend on it, which is rarely an option. So for the most part the standard only grows. There's a difference between pulling and removing. Pulling takes a feature out before it ships, which happens now and then. Removing something already shipped is rare, and can itself take many years.

### 6.6 The People Who Run It

A handful of officers keep the committee running. The **convener** runs WG21, appoints subgroup chairs, creates study groups and sets the meeting schedule. SC22, WG21’s parent committee, appoints the convener for a term of up to three years. There is no limit on the number of terms. A secretary and, as of 2026, two vice-conveners help the convener with the committee’s administration.

The **project editors** maintain the working draft by applying the changes approved by the committee. They are the people who apply every approved change to the text of the standard. It's quiet, exacting work that holds the whole document together. Each room has its own small crew. A chair runs the session, and someone takes the minutes. Anyone in the room can be asked to scribe, so it might be you.

A small senior group steers the committee's focus. It's the **Direction Group**, introduced in § 4.7, and it publishes its priorities in the Direction papers. Membership is by invitation, and the chair rotates. The Direction Group doesn't decide individual papers. It sets the big-picture priorities that chairs lean on when scheduling, so framing your work to match those priorities is the proper path forward.

### 6.7 Chair Power

The chair of a room holds more power than it looks. The chair decides which papers reach the agenda, when they are scheduled, how the polls are worded, and when the room has agreed. None of that is a vote, yet all of it shapes outcomes.

One of the chair’s most important responsibilities is determining whether the group has reached consensus. The chair considers the poll results alongside the discussion and the reasons for opposition. Even when more people vote in favour than against, the chair may find that there is insufficient agreement to proceed (§ 9.3).

### 6.8 The Rules: Standing Documents

The committee's own rules live in [standing documents](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents). Eight are current, and the numbering has gaps because older ones were retired. They are issued under the convener's authority, and the ones that set subgroup policy are maintained by the chairs of those groups. Standing documents change slowly and carefully, and no rule expires on its own, so old practices tend to stay.

### 6.9 The Limits of Power: Volunteers and the Implementer Veto

Here's the truth under all the structure: nobody can be ordered to do anything. WG21 is volunteers, and it only makes recommendations. A proposal dies the moment its people lose interest.

You will hear that the compiler teams hold a quiet **implementer veto**: that if GCC, Clang, MSVC and EDG (§ 1.8) won't build a feature, the standard can't make them, so nothing gets in that the implementers won't carry. The Direction Group wishes that were true, and it should be. It isn't. In practice the committee has adopted feature after feature that no implementer had built, on a schedule the implementers had not agreed to, and the standard then says one thing while your compiler does another for years. The veto exists only in the weak form: implementers can decline to implement, and users pay for the gap. In January 2026, eighteen implementers wrote [P3962R0](https://wg21.link/p3962r0), "Implementation reality of WG21 standardization", asking the committee to slow down, because features pile up faster than they can build them. Read that paper as the implementers saying, in public, that the veto everyone assumed they had was never real. The people who turn the standard real are stretched thin, and the only durable fix is the one they ask for: adopt less, and adopt what is already built.
