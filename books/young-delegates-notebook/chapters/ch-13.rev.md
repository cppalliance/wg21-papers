## 15. The Traps

You've made it far enough to be dangerous. This last chapter lists the traps that catch newcomers, each with its fix and the section that covers it. Read it once now, and again before your first meeting.

| **Trap** | **Fix** | **See** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Showing up without announcing | Email the convener a week ahead; catch the Sunday orientation | § 2.4, § 2.7 |
| Expecting to attend as a guest for multiple times without getting accredited | Ask your NB for a Global Directory entry months before you need the vote | § 2.5, § 3.3 |
| Proposing a core language feature before you know the room | Watch how the room works first, socialize the idea, bring evidence | § 11.8, § 13.1 |
| Writing an idea-only paper | Bring worked examples, alternatives, and an implementation, even on your first paper | § 11.2, § 11.6 |
| Voting on things you haven't followed | Read it or don't vote; neutral is for the read-and-undecided | § 9.5 |
| Submitting a proposal without checking for overlapping work | Search for competitors, talk to them first, converge or write the joint comparison | § 12.1 |
| Asking for reviews before you have given or offered to give any | Climb the ladder: review, co-author, then propose | § 13.1 |
| Promising a vote on the basis of personal relationship or author identity rather than critical technical analysis. | Let people earn it with a paper you have read and judged. | § 3.3 |
| Publishing non-public reflector or meeting material | Paraphrase; share only public polls and reflectors. Ask for permission before quoting people. | § 5.2, § 8.3 |
| Raising a concern for the first time at plenary | Raise it in the room, then with your head of delegation, then on the reflector by 5pm the evening before | § 9.7, § 3.4 |
| Expecting majority rule | Consensus is the absence of sustained opposition; a firm minority can block | § 9.1, § 9.7 |
| Talking too much too soon | Listen first, contribute something specific, let the work speak | § 8.2 |
| Misreading encouragement as approval | "Come back with more" means keep going, not "this will ship" | § 9.6 |
| Ignoring subgroup direction | Follow it, or reopen it in that room with a paper; don't route around it | § 13.4 |
| Declaring victory at design approval | It's the first fifth; wording and ballots are the other 80% | § 12.7 |
| Voting No on a national ballot to get attention for comments | No means "not at all"; comments get read regardless | § 10.3 |
| Filing a ballot comment on a point your delegation lost in the room | Bring new information with a paper, or accept the decision | § 10.4 |
| Sitting on two national bodies' delegations at once where either of those NBs disallow it. | Pick one and say which; some committees now bar dual delegation | § 3.6 |

One more general mistake you should also be aware of: confusing being busy with making a difference. Showing up and passing polls can feel like impact, but a feature that never ships helped nobody. Keep your eyes on the people who use C++.

> Keep your wits about you. Be skeptical. Demand evidence. Put the users first.

One last thing. This is the current state of the guidance, not the final one. The committee's rules and habits are constantly evolving, sometimes between one meeting and the next, and a notebook printed for Búzios will be out of date in places by the meeting after. Watch for updates: read each new SD-4, each new Direction Group revision, and each trip report, and treat what you find there as overriding what you read here.

That's the end of the notebook, and the start of your part in it. Now go do it well.
