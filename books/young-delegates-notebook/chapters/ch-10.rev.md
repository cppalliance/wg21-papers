## 12. Producing and Submitting Your Paper

You're ready to write the paper. This chapter is the practical how-to: the tools, the format, and the path from your editor to the committee's agenda. By the end you'll know how to ship it.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list of the topics the chapter covers, from floating the idea first to why design approval is a fifth of the work -->

### 12.1 Float the Idea First

Before you write a full paper, float the idea. Post a short description to std-proposals (the public list from § 5.5) and see who bites. The official [submit-a-proposal page](https://isocpp.org/std/submit-a-proposal) walks you through it. This saves you from writing a paper nobody wants. You'll find prior art, hear objections early, and maybe meet a co-author. A warm reception here is a green light.

Floating the idea also does something the Direction Group now expects of you: it finds your competitors before the room does. [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf), "Guidance on Building Consensus and Converging Proposals", asks authors to check for concurrent or past proposals in the same area and, if there are any, to talk to their authors before bringing the conflict to a working group. The ideal outcome is one unified paper; the minimum is a joint paper that sets out the trade-offs between the approaches with real usage examples. Chairs have been asked to act as matchmakers and to prioritise proposals that show this kind of breadth, which means that two papers arriving cold on the same problem will both wait. Do the search, send the email, and say in your paper that you did.

### 12.2 Writing the Paper: Tools, Template, and SD-7

You don't format a paper by hand. Most authors use [mpark/wg21](https://github.com/mpark/wg21), which turns Markdown into a committee-styled document, or [Bikeshed](https://github.com/tabatkins/bikeshed), which builds HTML specs. Newer tools like COWEL and plain LaTeX work too. Start from the official template. It's posted on isocpp.org as the "Template for Library Proposals" at the foot of the [How to Submit a Proposal](https://isocpp.org/std/submit-a-proposal) page; language proposals follow the same shape, and the tools above ship their own versions.

One standing document covers the mechanics: [SD-7](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-7-mailing-procedures-and-how-to-write-papers), "Mailing Procedures and How to Write Papers". It tells you how to get a paper number, how to format the document, and how to submit it to a mailing. Read it once before your first paper. Remember the basics from Chapter 4. Your paper gets a P-number and a revision, and it has to land before the mailing deadline. A late paper waits for the next round.

Here is what submitting actually looks like, because SD-7 describes it and the page does it. Papers are uploaded at [isocpp.org/papers](https://isocpp.org/papers), which needs an isocpp.org login; the same login gives you the private papers list, and the page shows the next mailing deadline at the top. Click "Submit a new paper or paper revision". The form asks for the number type, P for a proposal or N for a committee document; a previous version, where "New Paper Number" tells the system to assign you a fresh P-number, and where, for a revision, you select your existing paper from the list and the system increments the R for you (make sure the R on your title page matches the one it assigns); the document itself, as HTML or PDF; the title, the author and co-authors; the audience, as checkboxes for the study groups, the evolution groups, the wording groups, all of WG21, the Direction Group or the ABI Review Group; and an abstract, in Markdown, which is what the mailing's index will show. Two buttons finish the job. "Save Draft" gives you a D-number and lets you upload again as often as you like, so you can fix a typo an hour later. "Save and Mark for Review" tells the mailing editor the paper is ready, and that is the one that counts against the deadline. Save drafts freely; mark for review once.

### 12.3 Make It Accessible

Write the paper so anyone can read it. Use enough color contrast, a monospace font for code, and clear headings. Don't lean on color alone to make a point. This isn't busywork. A clear paper reaches the busy delegate skimming on a phone between sessions. Accessibility is reach.

### 12.4 Prototyping: Compiler Explorer and GitHub

Let readers run your code, don't just describe it. Compiler Explorer, at [godbolt.org](https://godbolt.org), runs C++ in the browser across many compilers. Share a live link and the reader sees it work. Put your implementation on GitHub. A public repo lets people read the code, file issues, and try it themselves. It also tracks your revisions as the design evolves.

### 12.5 Co-Authoring, Revision History, and Patent Disclosure

Many papers have more than one author. Co-authors bring expertise, share the load, and lend credibility. List everyone in the front matter, and name one person as the presenter. Expect to revise, again and again. Each revision (R0, R1, R2) answers the last round of feedback. Reviewers read what changed, so spell out your changes. Plan on five to ten revisions for an ordinary paper before it is done; a big one can take far more, and the revision counters in the teens and twenties on the papers page are not failures, they are the normal cost of a large feature. Settle in for the long haul, and settle in at the meetings, because sustained attendance is what most improves a paper's chances: the people who vote on R7 remember who was in the room for R2.

Before you present, don't forget one legal step. ISO has a patent rule, and you must follow it. If you know of a patent that covers your proposal, disclose it before the work advances. The [ISO patent policy](https://www.iso.org/iso-standards-and-patents.html) spells out the duty.

### 12.6 Getting on the Agenda

A paper with no presenter goes nowhere, and a paper nobody asked to schedule can go nowhere too. Unless your paper is purely informational, you may have to work to get it on the agenda. Your paper gets an issue in the committee's [GitHub tracker](https://github.com/cplusplus/papers) when it appears in a mailing, and that is the usual place to say you will present and which meeting you want. But every chair keeps the agenda their own way: some through that tracker, some on the meeting's wiki page, some by a call for papers broadcast on the subgroup's reflector. Find out which your chair uses, and if the paper still has not appeared on the agenda, email the chair directly and ask. That is not pushy; it is how the chairs expect to hear from authors, and it is far better than discovering on Monday that your paper was never in the plan. The chair schedules by SD-4's priorities (§ 6.7), so a paper that matches them gets time first. Confirm whether you'll present in person or by video.

When your slot comes, be ready to present and to listen. You'll show the problem, the design, and the proof, then take questions. The room's reaction steers your next revision.

### 12.7 The Other 80%

Winning design approval (the "we want this" gate from § 9.6) feels like victory. It's really the first fifth of the work. Wording review and the national ballots are the other 80%. The phrase is Herb Sutter's, from the committee's own page on [the life of a proposal](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal): newer proposers think they are "80% done" at design approval, when "the remaining stages form the 'other 80%'."

After design comes the long part. A wording group polishes the exact text, then the countries vote it through the ballot stages. Plan for years, not months, and don't celebrate too early.

Treating design approval as the finish line is the last big trap. Budget for the long tail, and don't celebrate until it ships.
