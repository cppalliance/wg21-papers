## 1. The C++ Standard

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics -->

### 1.1 Standardization Is a Responsibility

The work you're joining is serious. Banks and hospitals run on C++. When you change the language, you change the ground they stand on. So this isn't a game. It isn't a place to collect a credential or win an argument. It's a place to leave the language better than you found it.

The urge to get your idea adopted is strong, and it can fool you. The goal is to make C++ better for its users, not just to get a proposal adopted.

### 1.2 The Delegate's Oath

I have a brief way to describe the responsibility of participation in the standardization committee, and I call it the Delegate’s Oath.

> I vow to do what is best for the language, to make no unnecessary proposals, and to put the needs of sixteen million users ahead of my own.

Each part guards against a real temptation. "Best for the language" outranks best for you or best for your employer. "No unnecessary proposals" puts the burden on you to show a change earns its place. The last part is the heart of it. Roughly sixteen million people use C++, and they didn't ask you for anything. You serve them first, even when they'll never know your name.

### 1.3 The Oath Is the Ideal. Here Is the Day Job.

Most people in the room don’t pay their own way. An employer usually pays for travel, accommodations, and meals because it has a stake in the standard. It may need a feature for its products, want to avoid a disruptive change, or favor a particular direction for C++.

Your national body, which accredited you, may hold a position too. Thus most delegates carry three loyalties at once, and the honest order is this: first the good of the language, second the needs of the company that sends you, third the recommendation of your national body. Say that order out loud to yourself before every poll, because the pull of your employer and national body is constant, and the first, the good of the language, has no one lobbying for it.

The two lower loyalties are not dishonorable. Your employer's need is usually a real user's need, dressed in a budget. Your national body's position is usually a considered one, reached by people who read the papers. The trouble starts only when either of them asks you to vote for something you believe is bad for the language, and the oath exists for that moment. What balance to strike is a conversation worth having in earnest, with your manager and with your head of delegation, before you are standing in the room with a hand half raised.

How your national body is built changes what these loyalties look like in practice, and Chapter 3 explains the two designs. Under a company-based system, such as INCITS, the national body of the United States, the company holds the membership and pays the fee. By design, the national vote combines the votes of the member companies. A delegate who represents the company’s position in the mirror committee is therefore doing what the system expects.

In an expert-based national body such as that of Canada or the United Kingdom, the individual is the member. The company paying for your travel may or may not be steering how you vote in national ballots and subgroup polls. Nobody assumes it is.

That is the structural reason the same person can, honestly, vote one way as an individual expert and another when representing their national body. The two systems ask you different questions, and only one of them is about your employer.

The committee's own structure gives you a way to keep the loyalties apart. In the subgroups you vote as an expert, in your own name; ISO's rules say working-group experts act in a personal capacity, and a straw poll is the place to say what you think is right. At the closing plenary, individual experts vote, and on the national ballot, your country speaks. Even at the plenary, on controversial votes, the convener may ask your head of delegation whether an objection is a personal or a national position (§ 3.4). It is legitimate to vote one way as an individual expert and another when representing your national body, because the questions differ: "Is this the best design?" and "Can my country live with this?" are not the same question. What is not legitimate is to blur them, casting a national position as your expert opinion or hiding an employer's instruction behind either. Keep your wits about you: Know which hat you are wearing, and be able to say so if asked.

### 1.4 Every Feature Is Forever, and Everyone Else Pays

Here's the hard rule: The standard almost never shrinks. Features get added. They rarely get removed. C++ got a [regular-expression library](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/regex) in 2011. The shipped version was slow, and everyone soon knew it. More than a decade later, it's still slow. It can't be fixed, because a faster version would break programs already built on the old one, so the slow version must stay. The mistake is frozen in place.

That's the pattern for everything you add. Proposing a feature costs you a few months. Maintaining it costs everyone else forever. And "everyone else" is a lot of people. The cost lands on the developers who use C++. They didn't vote for your feature, and most will never read a proposal. They meet your work as a line in compiler release notes, and by then it's permanent. The cost reaches past users, too. Every compiler team must build and maintain every feature. Every textbook, course, and tutorial has to cover it.

All of this is "the standard." That word means something more exact than you might expect.

### 1.5 The Standard Is Not the Language

You might think "C++" and "the C++ standard" are the same thing. They're not. Three different things wear that name.

The standard is a long document that specifies the rules of C++. A compiler is a program that implements those rules. The big compilers don't follow it perfectly, and they don't all agree, so the C++ you can actually use is whatever your compiler accepts. Keep three things apart:

- The **standard** is the normative text.
- The **compilers** are the programs that try to follow it.
- The **living language** is what real code relies on.

That text isn't one fixed thing either. It comes in a few forms, and the names are worth knowing.

### 1.6 What "The Standard" Actually Is

Behind every C++ release is a living document. It's called the **working draft**, and it changes all the time. You can [read the current one online](https://eel.is/c++draft/), and its source [lives on GitHub](https://github.com/cplusplus/draft). Every three years, the committee freezes the draft and publishes it. That published version is an **International Standard**, or IS. [C++23](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/23) is an IS, published by ISO as ISO/IEC 14882:2024. C++26 finished its technical work at the Croydon meeting in March 2026 and is on its way through the final ballot described in Chapter 10.

Two lighter document types show up too. A **Technical Specification**, or TS, is an experimental tryout. Implementers can offer it to let people test a feature before it joins the standard. Since 2023, ISO and SC22 have recommended an even lighter vehicle called a **white paper**: the same experimental branch as a TS, but adopted by a simple vote of SC22's national bodies, with no ISO schedule, format, or editing process attached. A **Technical Report**, or TR, is an older, informational kind. You'll rarely meet a new one.

### 1.7 How to Read the Standard

The standard looks scary, and it is dense. But it's organized, and a few habits make it readable. You don't have to read it front to back.

Most of it is **normative** text, the parts that state actual rules. Mixed in are notes and examples, which never add a rule. They only help you understand the rules around them. Each part carries a stable section label in brackets, like [\[container.requirements\]](https://eel.is/c++draft/container.requirements). Those labels stay fixed between drafts. Section numbers shift, so point to the label, not the number.

Knowing the document is one thing. Getting a feature out of it and into your hands is another.

### 1.8 From Standard to Compiler

Publishing the standard doesn't put a feature in your hands. Someone has to build it into a compiler first. Until they do, it’s merely words on a page.

A handful of teams do most of that compiler building: [GCC](https://gcc.gnu.org/), [Clang](https://clang.llvm.org/), [MSVC](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/cpp/), and the EDG front end that sits underneath NVIDIA's compilers and Visual Studio's IntelliSense. Other, though older, compilers include IBM’s VisualAge C++, Borland’s, and Sun’s C++ compiler. Almost all the C++ in the world runs through one of these compilers, which makes the implementers powerful. If they won't implement a feature, the standard can't force them. So a feature isn't real until it ships in a compiler you can use.
