## 9. How Decisions Get Made

Voting here doesn't work the way you'd expect. No majority rules, and no simple count wins. This chapter shows you how the committee really decides, so you can read a room and vote with a clear head.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics, from what consensus means to what happens at the closing plenary -->

### 9.1 The Culture of Consensus

The committee decides by consensus, which isn't what most people think. It doesn't mean everyone agrees, and it doesn't mean the majority wins. It means no strong, sustained opposition is left standing. The test is whether people can live with a decision. A few mild objections don't block it. A small group of firm, reasoned objections can.

Here's the part to watch. Consensus is social pressure with a polite name. Keep your wits about you, and don't let the room's mood stand in for your own judgment.

### 9.2 Straw Polls and the Five-Point Scale

A subgroup chair may use a **straw poll** to help determine consensus. A common format has five positions: strongly favour, weakly favour, neutral, weakly against and strongly against. Other rooms such as LWG may use three-way polls: favour, against and neutral, while CWG does not take polls at all. Where agreement is clear, the chair may simply ask whether anyone objects.

Everyone attending a subgroup session can participate in its polls, including guests. The chair considers the discussion and any poll results when determining consensus. The subgroup’s decisions guide the author’s revisions and whether a proposal advances to the next stage.

If you vote against, be prepared to explain your concerns and what, if anything, would address them.

### 9.3 Reading Poll Results

A proposal normally advances when those in favor outnumber those against by about two to one, "after discussion of the concerns of those voting against and possibly a re-poll to see if opinions have improved." This is the **2:1 guideline**, and "normally" is doing a lot of work. Not everyone has to vote: people who have not read the paper often sit the poll out, and a majority of neutrals is a warning sign even when the ratio is met.

Polls are generally worded against the status quo, so that a positive outcome means making a change or taking an action. Voting neutral instead of in favour leaves that step with less support and can favour preserving the status quo. Technically it does not count as a vote against, but practically it does.

But the count isn't everything. Because the bar is to have no sustained opposition, a block of strongly-against votes can sink a proposal even when far more people favor it. Numbers can favor it and opposition can still kill it. This really happens: proposals clear the ratio and still fail when the against votes are firm, reasoned, and won't move.

### 9.4 Straw Poll vs. the Plenary Poll vs. the Ballot

Straw polls can gauge opinion or be used to make decisions. A subgroup can decide on a design direction, request revisions or agree to forward a proposal to another group.

Plenary polls concern decisions of WG21 as a whole. These include approving changes to the working draft and administrative actions, such as adopting a schedule or directing the convener to submit a document for ballot or publication. The convener normally seeks unanimous consent, takes a three-way poll if anyone objects, and determines whether there is consensus.

National-body ballots are separate votes in the ISO process, covered in Chapter 10.

### 9.5 When to Vote and When to Abstain

Vote your honest position when you have one. If you favor something, say so, not neutral. Neutral means you read the paper, weighed the issues, and truly land in the middle, which should be rare. When you have not read the paper, or the topic is outside what you know, do not vote. Not voting is the honest move, and it costs you nothing; hearing a summary isn't the same as knowing the issue. SD-4 says the same: participants who haven't read the paper "typically do not vote on that poll."

It is tempting to do otherwise. Listening to the people you respect and voting with them feels safe, and raising a hand feels like showing you belong. Resist both, because the single most important thing a member does in a poll is keep the count honest. The chair reads a poll as a measurement of the room's considered judgment, and every hand raised for another reason is noise in that measurement.

Right now, a good many of the neutral and in-favour votes in any poll are hiding four kinds of non-opinion: people who have not read the paper and want to vote with the crowd; people who do not understand the issue and want to look as if they do; people who could not think of a serious objection and so go along with the majority; and people who have, for various reasons, given up objecting. Each of those is a vote that says nothing, counted as if it said something. The remedy is simple and slightly uncomfortable: read the paper or keep your hand down, and if you have read it and are still undecided, neutral is the honest answer, not silence.

The Direction Group's standing paper draws the same line from the other side. The paper says that “I didn't have time to read the document” is not sufficient grounds for opposing a paper submitted on time. If your objection is “I don't understand”, explain the difficulty and give examples showing why it may affect others too.

It also asks people who did not take part in the discussion to remain at least neutral. Members whose objections were considered and rejected in a working group are asked to do the same at plenary. Read those rules and this section together: do not oppose what you have not read, but do not support it either.

Whatever you vote, ask for evidence. Don't vote on enthusiasm or a friend's nod. Be moved by proof, not by the mood of the room. And vote with evidence.

Voting with the room on a paper you didn't read is a quiet mistake. Your passive vote still counts under consensus, so it muddies the signal, and four kinds of non-opinion are already hiding in the neutral and in-favour columns. Don't add to the problem. Read it or don't vote.

### 9.6 Two Gates: Direction vs. Design

A proposal passes through two different gates. **Direction approval** is early: does the committee want this kind of thing at all? **Design approval** is later: is this specific design the right one? Here's the trap newcomers fall into. Passing either gate is not a promise to ship. A warm direction poll can sit for years with nothing delivered.

So read encouragement for what it is. "We'd like to see more work" means keep going, not "this will ship." [P1306](https://wg21.link/p1306), "Expansion Statements", won its direction for C++20 and shipped in C++26.

### 9.7 A Small Minority Can Block, and How to Escalate

A small group can stop almost anything. Since consensus means no sustained opposition, a handful of firm objectors can block a proposal the majority wants. This is built into the system, not a loophole. So find the objections to your proposals early. Learn who has concerns and talk to them before the poll, not after. A lone objector is weaker than one who's found an ally on the merits.

If you still believe a serious mistake is being made, a path exists, and it has a clock. SD-4 names three places to raise a "cannot live with" objection: tell the EWG and LEWG chairs as early as you can; post it on the subgroup's reflector, as soon as possible immediately after the discussion and in no case later than 5pm the evening before the closing plenary; and raise it inside your national body so your chair can caucus and say whether it is a national position. Bring at least a draft paper. An objection that skips these steps and appears for the first time at plenary is, by the rulebook, not to be given weight.

Here is how the three routes work in practice, because they are not equal. Your national body is the first route and the one with power. A reflector thread almost never overturns a Saturday straw poll: by the time it is posted the room has voted, and the people who voted are not going to re-read a design on a mailing list on Friday night. What the reflector post actually does is put the concern on the record in time for the motion to be altered or withdrawn before plenary, so that nobody is ambushed. Reflector threads are not the mechanism for sustaining an objection. That mechanism is your delegation: when the convener asks whether the Against votes are personal or national positions, only a head of delegation who has caucused can answer "national", and a national position is what can hold a motion. So go to your head of delegation first, and early; make the reflector post because the rule requires it and it protects you; and understand that the deadline governs changing the poll, not whether your country may still object.

### 9.8 The Plenary: Where Features Enter the Draft

The week ends with the closing plenary on Saturday. Each subgroup reports, and the committee polls to adopt finished wording. A feature enters the working draft the moment a plenary motion to apply it carries. Plenary usually run on silence. The convener asks if anyone objects to unanimous consent, and if no one does, the motion passes. An objection forces a three-way poll, and if the opposition is significant the convener can ask the national body chairs whether it is a national position. Plenary is where objections are counted, not where they are born.

Passing the subgroups isn't the final word. A proposal can clear every room and still fail at plenary. The type [std::byte](https://wg21.link/p0298) did exactly that in November 2016, sent back partly for arriving after the C++17 comment draft and partly over its name. It was adopted unchanged three months later.
