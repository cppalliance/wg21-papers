## 1. The C++ Standard

Every C++ compiler in the world is chasing one document. It's the C++ standard, and you'll help shape it.

### 1.1 Standardization Is a Responsibility

The work you're volunteering for is serious. Banks and hospitals run on C++. When you change the language, you change the ground they stand on.

So this isn't a game. It isn't a place to collect a credential or win an argument.

### 1.2 The Delegate's Oath

The duty fits in a single sentence. Some delegates call it the Delegate's Oath, and it goes like this:

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

I vow to do what is best for the language, to make no unnecessary proposals, and to put the needs of sixteen million users ahead of my own.

</div>

Each part guards against a real temptation. "Best for the language" outranks best for you or your employer. It's also the hardest part to live by, because your employer's need is usually a real user's need, dressed in a paycheck.

If your employer pays for your time here, the trouble starts when it asks you for a vote you believe is bad for the language. The oath exists for that moment. Talk the balance through with your manager early, before you're standing in the room with your hand half raised.

"No unnecessary proposals" puts the burden on you to show a change earns its place. The urge to get your idea adopted is strong, and it can fool you. Passing a proposal was never the goal. The goal is a better language for the people who use it.

The last part is the crux of it. You serve those users first, even when they'll never know your name.

To understand why the oath is this strict, look at what a single feature costs.

### 1.3 Every Feature Is Forever

The hard rule: the standard almost never shrinks. Features get added. Removing one takes years. Even after it's gone from the standard, it lives on in compilers and in the code that uses it.

C++ got a [regular-expression library](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/regex), `std::regex`, in 2011, and everyone soon learned it was slow. More than a decade later, it's still slow.

It can't be fixed, because a faster version would break programs already compiled with the old one. So the slow version stays. The mistake is frozen in place.

Whatever you add to the standard freezes the same way, mistakes and all. Proposing a feature costs you several years. Maintaining it costs everyone else forever.

### 1.4 The People Who Inherit Your Work

The cost is paid by the people who use the language. More than [sixteen million developers](https://www.slashdata.co/post/global-developer-population-trends-2025-how-many-developers-are-there) write C++ today, by one recent estimate. Whatever you standardize, they inherit.

They didn't vote for your feature, and most will never read a proposal. They discover your work as a line in compiler release notes. By then it's permanent.

Users aren't the only ones who pay. Each compiler team is expected to build and maintain every feature, and every textbook, course, and tutorial has to cover it.

<div id="watch-out">

When you catch yourself saying "it's just one small addition," stop. That urge is how careful people do lasting damage. Small is not the same as free. Ask who will maintain it in 2040.

</div>

### 1.5 The Standard Is Not the Language

You might think "C++" and "the C++ standard" are the same thing. They're not. Three different things go by that name.

*The standard* is a document. It's a long, exact text that says what C++ means. It's words, not a program.

The second is a compiler, a program that tries to follow that document. The big compilers don't follow it perfectly, and they don't all agree. So the C++ you can actually use is whatever your compiler accepts.

The third is the living language: C++ as it's used in all the code already out there. That code leans on compiler quirks and extensions as well as the text. It doesn't change when the standard does, so anything you add has to work with it.

Keep three things distinct: the standard is the text, the compilers try to follow it, and the living language is what real code relies on.

### 1.6 The Forms the Standard Takes

The standard comes in a few forms, and each name tells you how settled a feature is. Behind every C++ release is a living document called the *working draft*, and it changes all the time. You can [read the current one](https://eel.is/c++draft/) online, and its source lives on [GitHub](https://github.com/cplusplus/draft).

Every three years, the committee freezes the draft and hands it to ISO to publish. That published version is an *International Standard*, or IS. [C++23](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/23) is one. C++26 [finished its technical work in March 2026](https://herbsutter.com/2026/03/29/c26-is-done-trip-report-march-2026-iso-c-standards-meeting-london-croydon-uk/). Before ISO publishes any version, member countries have to approve its final text in a vote.

Two lighter documents let people test a feature before the standard commits to it. A *Technical Specification*, or TS, is the experimental tryout, and a *white paper* does the same job with far less paperwork. The committee's own [practices guide](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) says the standards bodies have recommended the white paper since 2023.

So when someone says a feature is "in a TS," it isn't in the standard yet, and it may never be. You'll also run into the odd *Technical Report*, or TR, an older kind the other two have mostly replaced.

### 1.7 How to Read the Standard

The standard looks scary, and it is dense. But it's organized, and a few habits make it readable. You don't have to read it front to back.

Most of it is *normative text*, the parts that state actual rules. Those are the words people will spend an hour on, because a single one can change what a program means. Mixed in are notes and examples, which never add a rule. They only help you understand the rules around them.

Each part has a *stable section label* in brackets, like [\[container.requirements\]](https://eel.is/c++draft/container.requirements). Those labels stay fixed between drafts. Section numbers shift, so point to the label, not the number.

<div id="try-this-today">

Open the working draft and search for a feature you use every day. Find its bracketed label, then read one paragraph of its rules, slowly. That's the kind of text you'd be changing, and millions of people depend on it.

</div>

### 1.8 From Standard to Compiler

Publishing the standard doesn't put a feature in your hands. Someone has to build it into a compiler, sometimes before the standard is out and sometimes years after. Until they do, it stays words on a page.

The bulk of that building falls to *the three implementers*: [GCC](https://gcc.gnu.org/), [Clang](https://clang.llvm.org/), and [MSVC](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/cpp/). Most of the C++ in the world runs through one of them. If they won't build a feature, the standard can't force them. So a feature isn't real until it ships in a compiler you can download.

Picture the day your proposal is adopted. On each of those three teams, someone reads it and opens a ticket. They'll build it and chase down its bugs. Then they'll keep it working for as long as anyone writes C++.

<div id="short-version">

Changing C++ is a responsibility, not a hobby. Whatever you add stays, and sixteen million people inherit it. The standard is a text, and it comes as a working draft, a published standard, and lighter tryouts. Cite its bracketed labels, since section numbers shift. A feature isn't real until a compiler ships it.

</div>

If all this feels heavy, good. Keep that feeling. It's what will stop you the next time an idea seems too small to cost anything.
