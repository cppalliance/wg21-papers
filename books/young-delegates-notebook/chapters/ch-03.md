## 3. How Papers Work

Open the committee's paper archive for the first time and you get a page of codes. Hundreds of rows, mostly a letter, four digits, an R, and a number. All the committee's work flows through one kind of object: the paper. Learn how papers work, and the rest of the process makes sense.

### 3.1 Nothing Happens Without a Paper

One rule explains most of the committee: no paper, no proposal. The rulebook, [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), puts it bluntly: "if a proposal doesn't have a paper, it doesn't exist." A *paper* is a written proposal or report, and it's the unit of all the work.

A paper needs three things before a room can act on it. The paper has to exist, it has to arrive on time, and someone has to be there to present it. Miss one and the room moves on.

Talk still matters. A hallway conversation can expose a flaw, improve a design, or change a mind. But the room only votes on what it can read, so if you want to move the committee, write it down.

### 3.2 What a Paper Is and Its Types

Papers sort first by what they want from you. An *ask-paper* proposes something and asks for a poll. An *inform-paper* puts facts on the record and asks for nothing. Those aren't official labels, just useful ones.

Here's a quiet truth: the inform-paper often gets read more. It asks for no time on a crowded schedule, and nobody has to pick a side to read it. So it slips in where an ask-paper waits in line.

Papers also split by job. Some argue for a design, and others nail down exact standard wording. A separate kind, the *standing document*, holds the committee's own rules instead of proposing anything.

### 3.3 The P-Number System

A paper's number starts with a letter that tells you what sort of document it is. A *P-number* marks a proposal, like [P2300](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p2300r10.html). A *D-number* marks a draft that hasn't been published yet. You'll often see one during a meeting week, when an author revises a paper overnight.

An *N-number* marks an official ISO document, like the working draft itself, an agenda, or minutes. Before 2015 every paper got one, so an older N-number is often a proposal.

After the number comes a *revision*, like R0 for the first version, then R1, R2, and up. R0 is brand new and untested in the room. A paper at R5 has been reworked five times.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

A paper's revision number tells you its history, not its quality. A fifth revision means someone kept working, not that they got it right. Judge what's on the page.

</div>

Once you have a number, the paper is one link away.

### 3.4 Finding Any Paper

The papers all live in one official archive, [open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/), in a plain list by date. Every paper link in this notebook points there.

You don't hunt for papers by hand. The quickest route is wg21.link, an unofficial shortcut that Mara Bos has run for years. Type a paper number after the slash, and it sends you to the copy on open-std.org. For P2300 revision 10, that's `wg21.link/p2300r10`.

Leave off the revision, as in `wg21.link/p2300`, and you land on the newest one.

### 3.5 The Mailing System and Deadlines

Papers arrive in batches called *the mailing*, most months of the year. The biggest ones land just before and just after each meeting. In 2025 there were nine in all. Counting revisions, that's eight hundred or more papers a year.

Timing decides what the committee can act on. Each meeting has a *pre-meeting mailing deadline*, weeks ahead of it. Miss it and your paper sits out until the next round.

There's one exception. A late update to a paper that made the deadline can still be heard.

### 3.6 The Paper Lifecycle

A paper that makes it follows a route called *the paper lifecycle*. It starts as an idea and ends in the published standard. Most new-feature proposals stop somewhere along the way.

The ones that finish pass through these stages, roughly in this order:

1. You submit the paper to a mailing.
2. A small group may incubate the idea and shape it first.
3. A design group decides whether the direction is right, then approves the design.
4. You write the exact standard wording, and a wording group reviews, fixes, and approves it.
5. The plenary, the full committee, votes it into the working draft (the living text that becomes the next standard).
6. The national bodies review the whole draft and send comments, and the committee answers every one. [C++26 drew 411 of them](https://herbsutter.com/2026/03/29/c26-is-done-trip-report-march-2026-iso-c-standards-meeting-london-croydon-uk/).
7. The national bodies vote to approve it, and ISO publishes the new standard.

Any stage can be the end. A paper can stall, get sent back, or quietly die partway through. The whole journey takes years.

The committee keeps a longer, [stage-by-stage version](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal) of this path, and it's worth reading once.

When a paper catches your eye, work out which stage it's at. That tells you what kind of help it can still use.

### 3.7 Design Review versus Wording Review

Two of those stages, design and wording, ask very different questions. *Design review* asks: is this the right feature, built the right way? *Wording review* asks: is the exact text precise and complete?

The two happen in order. Once the design is approved, the author's proposed text goes to a wording group. Wording review assumes the answer is yes and hunts for gaps in the spec text.

That order matters to you. If you doubt the design, say so while the paper is still in design review. Bring the same doubt to the wording group later, and it usually treats the question as settled.

### 3.8 Defect Reports and the Issues Lists

The standard ships with bugs like anything else. Anyone can report one as an *issue*, and the committee decides whether it needs a fix. When the full committee approves a fix to the published standard, the issue becomes a *defect report*. A fix to brand-new draft text doesn't get that label.

These bugs are tracked in public on two *issues lists*, one for the [core language](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/cwg_active.html) and one for the [library](https://cplusplus.github.io/LWG/lwg-active.html). The wording groups work through them, and the fixes the plenary approves go into the working draft.

Because they're public, the issues lists are open to you today. Read a few and you'll learn how the wording groups think. Find a real bug, and the library list spells out how to file one. It even says "your chances of a successful issue greatly increase if you attempt wording."

### 3.9 Reading a Paper Critically

You can't read eight hundred papers a year, so don't try. Read the ones in the areas you care about, and skim the titles of the rest. Read deeply only where you'll vote.

Always read the newest revision. An old one makes you raise a concern the author already fixed. When a paper comes back, compare it against the last version to see what changed. Many papers list those changes near the top, in a revision history.

Start with the header. It gives the paper's number, its date, who to reply to, and its audience. The audience line names the groups it's aimed at, a clue to its stage.

Before you form an opinion on any paper, run it through a few questions:

- What problem does it solve, and does it say so?
- Does it weigh other options, or pretend none exist?
- Has anyone built it, or is it only an idea?
- What old code breaks, and how do users move forward?
- Is the wording clear on the hard cases?

The gaps are the red flags. Empty answers, like no implementation and no migration plan, are objections someone will raise in the room. A strong paper closes them before you ask. If a paper doesn't, tell the author.

<div id="try-this-today">

Open the newest mailing on open-std.org and pick one paper on something you use every day. Ask what it fixes, what it breaks, and whether anyone has built it. Write down one gap. That's how every review starts.

</div>

Two documents will save you more than any single paper.

### 3.10 The Documents Everyone Should Read: SD-4 and P2000

Before almost anything else, read the rulebook and the roadmap. The rulebook is SD-4, "WG21 Practices and Procedures," the practices everyone is expected to know. It covers polls, consensus, deadlines, ballots, and how to escalate a disagreement.

The roadmap is [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf), "Direction for ISO C++." A small group of experienced members called the *Direction Group* writes it to recommend where the committee should head over the long run. Papers often lean on it to make their case, so know what it says before you weigh theirs.

No paper, no proposal. Every paper lives on open-std.org, and its number tells you its kind and history. New papers arrive in mailings, and a paper that misses the deadline waits a meeting.
