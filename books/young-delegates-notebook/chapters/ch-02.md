### 2.1 You Don't Need Permission

No one grants you permission to help. For the first steps, you won't find a gatekeeper. You can read the proposals, review them, and talk ideas over in public today. If you want to contribute, you start.

The gates after that are a lot lower than the rumors make them sound. Their rules are posted, and the people at them want you through. You can attend a meeting as a guest for free. The [June 2026 meeting in Brno](https://herbsutter.com/2026/06/13/brno-trip-report/) had twenty-five first-time guests, so newcomers are normal here.

You also don't have to be a wizard. Plenty of useful work isn't writing code at all. Taking notes and reviewing other people's proposals both count.

### 2.2 The Nesting: ISO, JTC1, SC22, WG21

The committee has a long official name, and it tells a story. It's ISO/IEC JTC1/SC22/WG21. Read it left to right, and it nests from the biggest body down to the smallest.

[ISO](https://www.iso.org) is the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes standards for almost everything. With its sister body, the International Electrotechnical Commission ([IEC](https://www.iec.ch)), it runs [JTC1](https://jtc1info.org), the joint committee for information technology. That's why the name says ISO/IEC.

JTC1 holds [SC22](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/), the subcommittee for programming languages. [WG21](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee), Working Group 21, is the part of SC22 that owns C++.

Being at the bottom of the nest means that, on paper, WG21 only recommends. It agrees on what the standard should say, and the bodies above it run the official vote.

### 2.3 How the Committee Meets

The committee works in two modes. Most of the time it splits into *subgroups*, the smaller teams that each handle one slice of the work. At the start and end of every meeting, the whole committee gathers as one.

That full gathering is the *plenary*, where the whole committee makes its final decisions. The subgroups do the building. The plenary signs off.

This happens at a real scale. The Brno meeting drew about two hundred people from twenty-eight nations, a little over half in the room and the rest online. And the nations matter as much as the people, because the official members are countries.

### 2.4 What a National Body Is

Each country takes part through its *national body*, or NB, which is the country's standards organization. The national body holds the membership and appoints the experts who attend.

Inside each national body sits a small *mirror committee* that shadows WG21 at home. That's the group you actually join. It decides who represents your country and what your country thinks of a draft.

Within WG21, people vote, not countries. In the plenary, every expert appointed by a national body votes as an individual.

Countries get their own vote later, when a draft goes out for a formal ballot. There, and only there, it's one country, one vote, and a delegation of twenty counts the same as a delegation of one.

### 2.5 The Easiest Start: Attending as a Guest

The lowest-cost way in is to attend as a *guest*. A guest takes part in a meeting without joining a national body. It's free.

To come as a guest, [email the convener](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation), the person who runs WG21. Tell them you'd like to attend, and name the country where you live or work. ISO strongly encourages at least a week's notice.

As a guest you can do almost everything. You join the subgroups, argue, and take part in their polls. The one thing you can't do is vote in the plenary, where final decisions are made.

Guest status covers your first meeting. To keep coming after that, or to get the plenary vote, you need a seat through a national body.

### 2.6 Getting Further In: Finding a Seat

Start at home. If your country has a mirror committee, join it. Email the [national body chairs](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation), say where you live or work, and ask how to join. Some national bodies run one committee for every programming language, so say you want C++. Rules and fees vary by country, and some charge nothing.

If your country has no mirror committee, you still have a way in. You don't have to be American to join through the United States. Its national body, [INCITS](https://www.incits.org/participation/apply-for-membership), is open to anyone, with or without a company behind you. Many committee members take part through it.

You could start a mirror committee at home instead, but that takes about a year. Get a seat first, and build the committee once you know you'll stay.

INCITS charges a yearly fee per committee. In the 2026 cycle that's $2,703 at the full rate, $1,530 for small companies, and $510 for academics.

If that fee is a barrier, a free path exists. The [Beman Project](https://github.com/bemanproject) offers C++ developers free seats under its INCITS membership. You'd sit as an *alternate*, a backup to Beman's main representative. Travel is still on you, but the seat gets you into the rooms and the discussions.

Before you join, check who holds the seat. In some countries it's you. In the United States it's whoever pays the fee, which may be your employer. If your employer pays, the seat may not follow you to your next job.

### 2.7 Getting a Vote: The ISO Global Directory

A vote at the plenary counts only when you're accredited. *Accredited* means your name sits in the ISO *global directory*, the official roster of voting experts. Your national body puts it there.

<div id="watch-out">

You can go to meeting after meeting and never get your name into the global directory. Then the one vote that matters to you comes up, and you have no vote to cast.

</div>

A seat that gets you into the rooms doesn't always put your name on that roster. Ask your national body to list you, and check that it did. A guest needs a week's notice. A vote needs months, so ask long before the meeting where you want one.

A seat asks two things of you: read the proposals before you vote, and stay active in your mirror committee. You vote as yourself, but you're there on your national body's word.

A vote is only one way to matter. Many roles need no vote at all.

### 2.8 The Roles You Can Play

You can help in more ways than you'd guess. Here are the main ones:

- Note-taker, also called the scribe. Keeping a session's minutes is a clear way to be useful on day one.
- Reviewer. Read a proposal that's coming up and tell its author what you think.
- Author. Write up an idea and submit it for the committee to consider.
- Implementer. Build part of a feature in a compiler and report what you learn.
- Champion. Present a proposal and push it through the rooms. A *champion* can be the author, or someone else who believes in the idea.

The first two need no membership, just your time and attention.

### 2.9 Your First Day: Orientation and Introductions

Your first meeting starts before the work does. At 6 p.m. on the Sunday before, there's a newcomer orientation, which usually meets in the main hotel's lobby. It covers how the week works and answers your questions.

When the week opens, first-time attendees introduce themselves to the whole committee. It's brief and friendly, not a test.

### 2.10 Showing Up Is the Secret

The single biggest thing you can do is keep showing up. An old line says "eighty percent of success is showing up." In this committee, it's close to true.

Showing up builds two things: familiarity and trust. People learn your name and your judgment over many meetings. The committee runs on relationships, and relationships need time.

The reverse is true too. Stop coming, and the room quietly loses what you brought. Soon it forgets you brought anything. Presence matters here more than brilliance.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Show up, take notes, and read the proposals. Do those three things for three meetings, and you'll be a known quantity.

</div>

### 2.11 Conferences: Meeting the Committee Off Duty

Long before your first meeting, you can meet the committee at a conference. Many committee members go, so it's an easy place to talk with them in a relaxed setting.

Conferences are also where ideas get aired. A lot of C++ ideas start with an everyday problem. A product hits a limit in the language, a bug keeps coming back, or people keep writing the same code by hand. Sometimes a programmer just needs sockets, finds the standard library has none, and asks why not.

So if you want an idea worth chasing, look at what your code and your users can't do, not at what would be clever.

Two conferences stand out. [C++Now](https://cppnow.org) is small and expert-heavy, held each spring in Aspen, Colorado. It's where people push and refine new ideas. [CppCon](https://cppcon.org) is the big one, where ideas meet the wider community.

You don't have to fly to the United States, either. [isocpp.org keeps a worldwide list](https://isocpp.org/wiki/faq/conferences-worldwide), with [ACCU](https://accu.org) and [Meeting C++](https://meetingcpp.com) among the European ones. Going to any of them is a low-pressure way to start.

<div id="short-version">

You don't need anyone's permission to start. Within WG21, which sits inside ISO/IEC JTC1/SC22, people vote, not countries. Countries vote later, on the formal ballot. You can come to your first meeting free, as a guest, with a week's notice. Guests do everything but vote at the plenary.

To keep coming, you need a seat through a national body. Start at home if you can. Otherwise INCITS takes anyone for a fee, and the Beman Project offers free seats if that's too steep. A plenary vote also needs your name in the global directory, and that takes months, so ask early.

Plenty of the work needs no vote at all. Whatever you do, keep showing up.
