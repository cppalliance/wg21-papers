## 10. The Ballot from the Inside

Everything in Chapter 9 happens inside the committee. The ballot is where the committee stops and the countries take over. It's the part of the process most newcomers never see, and the one most often described wrongly. This chapter shows you what a ballot is, what a comment can and can't do, and how your country's vote actually reaches ISO.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics -->

### 10.1 Two Ballots, Not One

When the committee decides the working draft is ready, it doesn't send it to ISO for a vote. It sends it out for comment first. That stage is the **Committee Draft**, or CD, and it goes to every participating National Body with a question attached: do you want this work to go forward, and what would you change?

The CD is the ballot that matters most for the wording. It's the last point at which a country can ask for a real change and expect the committee to consider it. The comments come back, the committee works through every one, and only then does a cleaned-up draft go out again.

That second round is the **DIS**, the Draft International Standard. It's an approval ballot, not a comment ballot. Countries vote yes or no on the document as it stands, and the committee's room to change anything afterwards is nearly gone. C++26 went through this in order: the CD comments were worked off across two meetings, and the committee finished the last of its 411 national body comments at Croydon in March 2026 before producing the DIS.

### 10.2 What a Ballot Contains

A ballot has two parts, and they do different jobs. The first is the vote: approve, approve with comments, disapprove with comments, abstain, or abstain with comments. The second is the comments, a numbered table where each row names a clause, says whether the point is general, technical or editorial, explains the problem, and proposes a fix.

The table is the work product. A vote is one word; a comment is what the committee will actually read, discuss, and poll on. Every technical comment your country files becomes an agenda item at the next meeting, with a proposed resolution and a poll to accept or reject it. So a good comment looks like a small paper. It says what's wrong, where, and what text would fix it. A comment that says "this is bad" with no proposed change gets a polite "noted" and nothing else.

### 10.3 What No Means

Here is the rule newcomers most often get backwards. On an ISO ballot, a No vote does not mean "Fix my comments and I'll come round." According to SD-4 it means "My country does not want this standard to go forward at all, in any form."

There used to be a middle path. Ballot resolution meetings let a country trade a No for a Yes once its key comments were addressed. That mechanism is gone; there is no formal way to change a vote after the ballot closes. So a No is a real thing, and enough of them stop the whole standard. That's why the committee asks countries not to vote No as a way of pushing their comments up the queue. The comments get read and responded to anyway, regardless of the vote, and a tactical No risks the thing you were trying to improve. Vote Yes with comments unless your country truly wants the standard stopped.

If a DIS fails the ballot, then the convenor has four courses of action open to them. They can, once the comments are addressed, submit the standard for a second revised DIS vote, they can submit it for an FDIS vote, they can revert back to the CD stage, or they can opt to skip that particular revision and not submit it for further voting at all.

### 10.4 Two Comments That Don't Belong

Not every comment is fair game, and SD-4 names two that aren't. The first asks for a feature that isn't in the draft. The ballot is a review of what's there; a new feature needs a paper, a design room, and the years that follow. Filing it as a comment is asking the committee to skip its own process.

The second re-opens something the committee already decided while your country was in the room. If your delegation was present, heard the arguments, and lost the poll, coming back with the same objection as a national comment isn't a new technical point. It's an objection to the consensus itself, and the ISO code of conduct that everyone signed asks members to accept group decisions.

There are two honest exceptions. New information that could reasonably change minds, brought with a paper. Or a concern so serious that your country warned the committee all along it might vote No over it. What's not honest is a surprise: a No, or a hard comment, on a point nobody raised before the ballot.

### 10.5 Comments Are National

ISO treats every comment as the position of the country that filed it, and so does WG21. That has a consequence inside your mirror committee. If it collects every member's pet peeve and forwards the lot, it ends up filing comments that contradict each other, or that most of its own members don't support.

So some mirror committees vet. They read their members' draft comments, drop the ones the group doesn't back, and file the rest as one voice. Not every mirror committee does; some simply pass on whatever their members send, and it is not unknown for one country's ballot to carry two comments that contradict each other. The committee asks for the vetted kind, and delegations that do it are believed more readily on Saturday morning when the convener asks whether an objection is national. If your mirror committee is one of the ones that just forwards, that is a gap you can offer to fill. This is also where you come in as a newcomer. Reading the CD and drafting a comment for your country is real work that needs no seniority. It's the ballot-stage twin of the paper review from § 2.6.

### 10.6 How Comments Get Resolved

When the comments come back, they go to the rooms that own the wording. Design questions go to EWG or LEWG, wording to CWG or LWG, and each comment gets a proposed disposition: accepted, accepted with modification, or rejected with reasons. The committee polls each one like any other change, and the results are written up as a disposition of comments that travels with the next draft, so every country can see what happened to what it asked for.

Every change made at this stage has one purpose, written into SD-4: to improve consensus. The committee is careful here because it can't go back and ask. After a final approval ballot, a design change made between ballot and publication is expected to have near-unanimous support in the subgroups and at plenary, since there is no further ballot to find out whether a country that wasn't in the room would object. That's the reason the CD stage is where your country's real influence lives. By the DIS, the honest options are yes or no.

### 10.7 Your Country's Vote

So the path from your keyboard to ISO's tally runs like this. You read the CD and draft a comment. Your mirror committee vets it and files it with your country's vote. The committee resolves it at the next meeting, and someone from your delegation stands up for it. The cleaned-up DIS goes out, your country votes, and if the numbers clear the bar the standard is published.

None of it needs you to be famous. It needs you to be in your NB, to read early, and to write comments that propose a fix. Keep your wits about you, though: the ballot is the heaviest instrument a country has, and the committee remembers which delegations use it carefully and which reach for it every cycle.
