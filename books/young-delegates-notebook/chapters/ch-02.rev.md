## 2. Meet the Committee

You can join the standardization process, and this chapter shows you how. We'll go from "Who are these people?" to "Here's how I walk in." The door is wider than it looks.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list of the topics the chapter covers, from needing no permission to where ideas come from -->

### 2.1 You Don't Need Permission

No one grants you permission to help. You won't find a gate or a gatekeeper. If you want to contribute, you start. The real barriers are smaller than the imagined ones. You can attend a meeting as a guest for free. You can review proposals and share feedback with their authors or on public mailing lists before you ever attend a committee meeting.

You also don't have to be a wizard. Plenty of useful work isn't writing code at all. Taking notes and reviewing other people's proposals both count. So what is this thing you're joining? Start with its full name.

### 2.2 The Nesting: ISO, JTC1, SC22, WG21

The committee has a long official name, and it tells a story. It's ISO/IEC JTC1/SC22/WG21. It nests from the largest body down to the smallest.

[ISO](https://www.iso.org) is the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes standards for almost everything. The IEC is the International Electrotechnical Commission, its sister body for electrotechnology; the two run information technology jointly, which is why the name says ISO/IEC. Inside them, JTC1 is the Joint Technical Committee for information technology, SC22 is the subcommittee for programming languages, and WG21, Working Group 21, is [the part that owns C++](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee).

Here's the surprising part. WG21 doesn't publish the standard by itself. It agrees on what C++ should be, and then the bodies above it run the official ballot. Chapter 10 follows that ballot. Before we talk about joining, picture how the group actually gathers.

### 2.3 How the Committee Meets, and Who Votes

The committee works in two modes. Most of the time it splits into **subgroups**, the smaller teams that each handle one slice of the work. Once in a while the whole committee gathers as one. That full gathering is the **plenary**, where the whole committee makes its final decisions. The subgroups do the building; the plenary signs off.

This happens at a real scale. The Brno meeting in June 2026 drew about two hundred people from 28 nations, a little over half of them in the room and the rest online, and 25 of them were first-time guests. Newcomers are a regular part of these meetings.

The countries have a name in this system. Each participating country is a **National Body**, or NB, and your NB is your country's standards organization. But here is the thing newcomers most often get wrong: Inside WG21, the voters are people, not countries. At the closing plenary, each accredited expert present casts one vote, and the convener judges whether there is consensus. Countries vote as countries only later, when the finished draft goes to the National Bodies for formal ballot. There, and only there, it's one country, one vote.

You take part by joining a National Body, or by visiting as a guest. Joining makes you a full member, with a path to a vote. Visiting as a guest is the quickest way to start.

### 2.4 The Easiest Start: Attending as a Guest

The lowest-cost way in is to attend as a guest. A guest takes part in a meeting without joining a National Body, and it's free. To come as a guest, [email the convener](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation), the person who runs WG21. Tell them you would like to attend, and name the country where you live or work. ISO strongly encourages at least one week's notice.

As a guest you can do almost everything. You join the subgroups, argue, and vote in their straw polls. The one thing you can't do is vote in the plenary, where final decisions are made, although you are welcome to attend and observe the plenary sessions.

Guests are, however, limited to two meetings, so if you want to continue after two meetings, or if you want that plenary vote, then you will need to join a National Body. Three paths lead there, and this chapter covers two of them; Chapter 3 covers the one closest to home. The next section also discusses a fourth path, the free sponsored seat, which gets you into the rooms without the plenary vote.

### 2.5 Getting a Vote: INCITS, the Free Seats, and the Global Directory

You don’t have to be American to join through the United States. [INCITS](https://www.incits.org/participation/apply-for-membership) accepts members of its C++ committee regardless of nationality or employer (although the right to vote on the US National Body position, which we will talk more about when we cover voting in Chapter 10, is limited to “US citizens or permanent residents, and representatives of US-based companies”). Its members are organizations, such as companies, universities, and one-person firms. You sit on the committee as your organization’s representative.

Each organization pays a membership fee for every INCITS technical committee it joins. If your employer holds the membership, it typically pays the fee; if you join independently, you pay it yourself. The amount depends on the type of organization. Check the current schedule before you budget.

If the membership fee is a barrier, the C++ Alliance and the Boost Foundation (§ 5.8) offer sponsored seats at no cost. Both are INCITS member organizations and sponsor alternate representatives under their memberships. A sponsored seat lets you attend meetings, take part in discussions, and vote in subgroup straw polls, under the same code of conduct as everyone else. Depending on their policy, an alternate may not vote; and it carries no promise of funding, so you will need to pay for your own travel and expenses. It is the fastest free way into the building, and for most newcomers that is exactly what is needed first. Ask either organization directly for a seat.

### 2.6 The Roles You Can Play

You can help in more ways than you'd guess. The easiest roles need no membership at all.

- **Minute taker (a.k.a. “The scribe”).** Every session needs minutes, and anyone can be asked to take them. It's a clear way to be useful on day one.
- **Reviewer.** Read a proposal that's coming up and post what you think. Giving reviews earns you reviews of your own.
- **Author.** Write up an idea and submit it for the committee to consider.
- **Implementer.** Build part of a feature in a compiler and report what you learn.
- **Champion.** Present a proposal and push it through the rooms. A champion can be the author, or someone else who believes in the idea.

Notice that the first two need nothing but your time and attention.

### 2.7 Your First Day, and Why Showing Up Is the Secret

Your first meeting starts before the work does. The evening before, on Sunday, there's a newcomer orientation. Check the meeting’s wiki or ask for the time and location of the newcomer orientation. It covers how the week works and answers your questions. Soon after the newcomers get a welcome. You may be asked to say who you are and where you're from. It's brief and friendly, not a test.

After the welcome, one habit matters more than any other. The single biggest thing you can do is keep showing up. An old line says eighty percent of success is showing up, and in this committee it's close to true. Showing up builds two things: familiarity and trust. People learn your name and your judgment over many meetings. The committee runs on relationships, and relationships need time. The reverse is true too. When a regular voice stops attending, the committee quietly loses what that person carried. Presence is power here, more than brilliance.

### 2.8 Where Ideas Come From, and Where They Get Aired

Ideas for C++ often come from everyday programming problems. A product exposes a limitation in the language, a bug keeps recurring, or programmers keep writing the same idiom by hand. Other ideas come from seeing a useful feature in another language and asking why C++ makes it difficult. Sometimes a programmer simply needs sockets, discovers that the standard library has none, and asks why not.

The committee’s Direction paper makes the same point: “C++’s evolution must be driven by real problems.” If you want an idea worth carrying, look at what your codebase and your users cannot do, not at what would be clever.

Conferences are places to discuss these ideas and meet people working on the standard. You can have those conversations at events such as [CppCon](https://cppcon.org) (United States, the big one), [C++Now](https://cppnow.org) (United States, smaller), [ACCU](https://accu.org) (UK, the oldest and most reflective one), and [Meeting C++ (Berlin, the largest one in Europe)](https://meetingcpp.com). For other events, see the [worldwide conference directory](https://isocpp.org/wiki/faq/conferences-worldwide).
