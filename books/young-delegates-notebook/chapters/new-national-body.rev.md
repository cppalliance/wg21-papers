## 3. Your National Body

The committee is built out of countries, and one of them is yours. Chapter 2 showed you the fastest doors in. This chapter is about the door closest to home, the one most people in the room came through. By the end, you'll know what a National Body actually does, how to join yours, and why the person sitting at the head of your country's table matters on Saturday morning.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics -->

### 3.1 The Country Is the Member

Remember the nesting from § 2.2. WG21 belongs to SC22, and the members of SC22 are not people. They're countries, each represented by its national standards organization, and ISO calls each one a National Body, or NB. Twenty-nine of them take part regularly, from Austria to the United States, and about two dozen send experts to any given meeting.

An NB that wants a say does two things. It sends experts to WG21 meetings, and it votes on the formal ballots that turn the committee's draft into a standard. The experts are you. Everyone in the room is there because an NB put their name forward. So your NB is not a formality on the way in. It's the thing that makes you a member.

### 3.2 The Mirror Committee

Inside each NB sits a small group that shadows WG21. The usual name is the **mirror committee**, because it mirrors the international committee at home. In the United States it's INCITS/C++ (§ 2.5). In the United Kingdom it's a panel of the British Standards Institution. In Canada it runs under the Standards Council of Canada. Germany has one at DIN, France at AFNOR, Japan at JISC, and Brazil, our host at Búzios, at ABNT.

The mirror committee is where your country's C++ business happens. It decides who it will accredit as an expert. It works out what the country thinks about a draft, and it writes the country's ballot comments. Between meetings it's a mailing list or a chat platform and the occasional call; during a meeting it's a huddle in a corridor.

Here's the part that surprises newcomers: The National Bodies are sovereign. ISO doesn't tell a National Body who to admit or how to decide, and neither does WG21. Each one has its own rules, its own fees (often none), and its own way of forming a position. Two countries can run their mirror committees in completely different ways and both be doing it right.

The biggest difference is who the member is. Many countries such as Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, and others are **expert-based**: The individual is the member, there is no fee, and the accreditation stays with the person if they change jobs. Others such as Germany and the United States are **company-based**: The organization is the member, it pays a fee for each committee it joins (that is the INCITS fee from § 2.5), and the seat belongs to the employer, so a job change can cost you your place. Some countries adopt a hybrid approach. Neither is better, but it does decide whether your application form asks about you or about your company.

It also decides what a national vote means. In a company-based body the national position is, openly, the combined position of the member companies; each company paid for its seat and votes its interest, and the system is honest about that. In an expert-based body the individual votes, and the company that pays the travel may or may not be a motivating factor behind that vote. That difference is why the same delegate can vote one way as an expert in a subgroup and another way when the country speaks (§ 1.3). It also creates a duty. When one company sends several people onto an expert-based delegation, the honest practice is for each of them to say where they work, so the delegation can see whether a single employer is steering the votes. Saying it aloud is voluntary, and the good delegations do it anyway.

### 3.3 How to Join Yours

Start by finding the mirror committee, which is easier than it sounds. The committee's own [page on meetings and participation](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation) says to join the NB for the country where you live, and it gives an email address that reaches the active NB chairs. Write to it, say who you are and where you live, and ask to be pointed at your country's C++ NB chair. You may be eligible to join more than one NB; for example, the British Standards Institution (BSI) is open to non-resident citizens as well as residents, in which case you will have a choice, but this is the exception rather than the rule.

Then apply the way that NB asks. Some want a short form and a sponsor, some want your employer's name, some want a fee, and some will require an interview, while many want nothing but your interest. Expect the answer to take weeks rather than days, because the people reading it may be volunteers who also have papers to read.

Check which committee you are joining, because it may cover more than one language. Some national bodies, including Canada’s, mirror SC22, the parent committee for programming languages in general. One membership covers its working groups. Tell the chair which ones you want to work in: WG21 for C++, WG14 for C, or both. Other national bodies mirror WG21 directly, so that the thing you join is a C++ committee and nothing else; INCITS/C++ in the United States is built that way. Either way, make it clear on your application which working groups you mean to attend. A mirror committee that covers a dozen languages will not guess that you want the C++ one.

Two things happen when they say yes. The NB registers you in the ISO Global Directory, the roster from § 2.5, and that registration is what gives you the right to attend WG21 meetings. You also become part of the group that may decide your country's ballot, which is the subject of Chapter 10. Each national body has its own rules for deciding its position. Check those rules to find out how you can contribute and whether you are eligible to vote in that process.

Two things are expected of you in return, and they hold on every national body, paid or free. The first is to stay informed. Read the papers your country will vote on and take part in the mirror committee’s discussions. A vote cast in ignorance is worse than no vote (§ 9.5). The second is to stay active. Attend your mirror committee’s meetings regularly, in person or online, and respond to its calls for positions. Your national body is lending you its name every time you vote.

Renewing a paid membership can signal continued interest. Bodies that charge no fee need other ways to check whether members remain involved. Section 3.6 gives one example. Either way the duty is the same, and a member who is neither informed nor active is a seat the country cannot use. Timing matters. If you want to vote at a meeting, ask your NB months ahead, not the week before. A guest can walk in on a week's notice; an accredited expert may not.

If your country has no WG21 mirror, or no SC22 mirror at all, it is possible to start one. Two of this notebook's reviewers have done it. Expect the process to take about a year and require a great deal of paperwork with your national standards organization and ISO, and you will be expected to take on the duties of chair and head of delegation (see below) of that mirror as you will probably be the sole member at the beginning. It is the right thing to do if you want your country's voice on the ballot for the long term. It is the wrong first move if what you want is to attend and vote. Seek a seat first through the C++ Alliance or the Beman Project (§ 2.5), or another organization that already holds an INCITS membership and sponsors free alternates, and start the national committee once you know you will stay.

### 3.4 The Head of Delegation

Every country at a meeting has one person who speaks for it. That person is the **head of delegation**. In a small mirror committee it is often the chair; in a larger one each working group has its own head of delegation and the chair of the mirror committee is a separate office, which is how Canada runs it (§ 3.6). Most of the week you won't notice them doing anything different from anyone else.

Saturday is when it shows. At the closing plenary, if a poll draws real opposition, the convener does not ask the room for a show of hands from countries. The convener may ask the heads of delegation who are present whether the Against votes are personal positions or national positions. A personal objection is one expert's judgment. A national position is a country saying it may vote No on the ballot.

That question is only answerable if your delegation has talked. So when you have a serious concern during the week, your head of delegation needs to know before the Saturday plenary, not during it. The rule is written down: Raise it inside your NB so your chair can caucus, and post it on whatever communication mechanism your NB uses on the evening before plenary; § 9.7 explains why the first of those is the one that carries weight.

### 3.5 What a National Position Is, and Isn't

A national position is your country's considered view, not the loudest member's. Your mirror committee forms it however its rules say: a vote, a consensus call by the chair, a caucus in the hallway. The default is that nothing is a national position until the committee has actually discussed it.

That default protects you. It means one expert cannot commit a whole country by objecting loudly on Saturday morning, and it means the convener can trust the answer when a head of delegation gives one. It also means the work of forming a position happens early and quietly. The strongest delegations read the papers before the meeting, talk during the week, and know where they stand before the straw polls page goes up on Friday night. Keep your wits about you here too: A national position is a real instrument, and reaching for it on every disagreement blunts it fast, as Chapter 10 will explain.

### 3.6 A Worked Example: The Canadian Door

Every mirror committee is different, so here is one in full, from the inside. It's Canada's, because that’s the one this notebook's reviewers know best, and because its shape is the shape many expert-based national bodies share.

Canada is a participating member of SC22, so it votes on every ballot and sends accredited experts to every WG21 meeting. The mirror committee is called MC/ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 22, and it belongs to the Standards Council of Canada, or SCC. One committee covers the whole of SC22: C++ (WG21), C (WG14), Fortran, COBOL, Ada, Prolog, and the rest, each with its own head of delegation, so when you apply you tell the chair which working groups you want to join, and you can name more than one. The C++ group is by far the largest, twenty to thirty accredited experts, of whom five to ten attend a given meeting, and about that many are expected at Búzios; the other languages are a handful of people each and run themselves.

The whole mirror committee meets four times a year, for one hour on Zoom, on a first-Monday-of-the-quarter rhythm. Everyone contributes to a shared agenda before the meeting, helping to keep it short. The agenda covers approvals, reports from each language’s head of delegation, intended votes on upcoming ballots, and plans for attending future meetings. Between those meetings the C++ group talks on its own mailing list or other instant messaging and, during a WG21 meeting week, in the corridor.

Joining costs nothing. You apply on the SCC website with a CV and a short letter about your C++ experience; SCC's Member Services review it, and you go through an SCC onboarding session before anything else. Canada is liberal about who counts as "representing Canada": Citizens, people who live in Canada, and people who work for a company domiciled in Canada all qualify. It helps to write to the C++ head of delegation first, because the committee will tell you frankly whether you are likely to be accepted, and every new member is paired with a mentor from the group for their first meetings. Allow weeks for the review and more for your name to reach the ISO Global Directory, which is what turns membership into a plenary vote.

<!-- box: Watch Out on SCC's grounds for removing inactive members and BSI's attendance sheet -->

Other delegations could learn from how Canada forms its national position. To discuss potentially controversial questions, the Canadian delegation caucuses on Zoom in the hour before the Saturday plenary. Members attending remotely and in person share what they learned in the meetings they attended. This helps the head of delegation explain whether an objection reflects the national position (§ 3.4). For everything else, the delegation keeps a live shared spreadsheet where each member records their vote in real time, which is how the head of delegation reads the room during a plenary poll when there is no time to caucus. Ballot votes work the same way: The intended vote is recorded at the quarterly meeting and the chair or vice-chair casts the actual vote on SCC's platform, with Canada's internal deadline set a couple of weeks ahead of ISO's.

Because the individual is the member, the delegation takes care that no single employer steers Canada's vote. Some companies have several employees on the delegation. Members voluntarily identify their employers when the delegation caucuses, so everyone can see which affiliations are represented. One caution follows from the same fact: Because Canada is expert-based and INCITS accepts anyone working for a US-headquartered employer, it used to be possible to sit on both delegations at once. Some committees now bar experts from representing more than one national body, so pick one and say which.

### 3.7 Which Door Is Yours

So there are four doors, and they lead to the same room.

<!-- box: Table comparing the four ways in by cost, notice, what you get, and who each suits -->

Pick by what you want. You can take part in your first meeting as a guest. If you want to take part at no cost, take a sponsored seat. If you want to attend and vote quickly and your own NB is slow or silent, use INCITS as a member. If you want to be in the room when your country decides how it will vote, join your mirror committee and stay in it.
