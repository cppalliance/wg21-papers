## 12. C++ Design Principles

Not every no is politics. Some come from old rules the room rarely bends.

The oldest of these rules come from Bjarne Stroustrup. He wrote them down in 1994 in [*The Design and Evolution of C++*](https://www.stroustrup.com/dne.html), the book everyone calls D&E. EWG's own principles, [SD-10](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-10-language-evolution-principles), still start from his list. The book explains why each early feature is the way it is, and what was tried and thrown out.

Read it before you write a language proposal. Many of the objections you'll hear are in it, with the answers.

### 12.1 Standardize Existing Practice

The committee's safest path is to *standardize existing practice*. That means blessing a design that already works in the field, instead of inventing something new at the table. A design with years of real use behind it clears the bar far faster. Think of it as chapter 9's implementation experience, at full scale.

The pattern shows up again and again. The [fmt](https://github.com/fmtlib/fmt) library became `std::format`, and [range-v3](https://github.com/ericniebler/range-v3) became `std::ranges`, each after proving itself in the wild. Designs invented at the table are a gamble.

Why so cautious? Because the standard can't easily fix its mistakes.

### 12.2 Backward Compatibility

C++ makes a hard promise: old code keeps working. This is *backward compatibility*, and it guards decades of existing programs. The committee breaks it rarely, and usually after long warning. Break working code, and you're arguing against everyone who owns it.

That promise means a flawed type usually can't be changed in place. A fix would have to ship beside it as a second type, and the committee is reluctant to do that. A second, "safer" vector would split the library, since functions written for one kind wouldn't accept the other.

So one type has to serve everyone. When the committee does add a second type, everyone lives with both. C++20 put `std::jthread` beside `std::thread` rather than change the old one. Now you have two thread types and have to pick.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

The promise that old code keeps working blocks some good changes, maybe yours. It also protects all the code you've already written. Respect it on the days it says no.

</div>

### 12.3 ABI: The Invisible Constraint

The constraint you're least likely to see coming is *ABI*, the application binary interface. ABI is the binary contract between compiled pieces of a program: the memory layout of types and the way functions are called. Keeping it stable is backward compatibility one level down, for compiled programs instead of source code.

Remember `std::regex` from chapter 1, the slow library nobody can fix? ABI is why. A faster version would need new internals, and programs already built against the old one could crash or quietly misbehave. Their source code would compile fine, so the damage hides in the binaries.

Why not just rebuild? Because few programs come with the source for every library, plugin, and DLL they load. And ABI sits outside the standard's text. It's a promise the compiler teams make to their users. That's chapter 5's implementer veto at its sharpest: the committee can't force a break the implementers won't ship.

This is why some slow types never get faster. The hash map `std::unordered_map` is stuck twice over, because its own interface demands the slow design. So the committee keeps the slow versions, and the market routes around them.

The committee took this on at the *Prague ABI vote* in early 2020, arguing over two papers by Titus Winters. [P1863R1](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2020/p1863r1.pdf) was "ABI - Now or Never," and [P2028R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2020/p2028r0.pdf) asked "What is ABI, and What Should WG21 Do About It?"

Both sides had a real case, and Winters laid out both. Keeping the old ABI leaves speed on the table, perhaps 5 to 10 percent overall by his estimate. Breaking it means rebuilding the world, at a cost he put in engineer-millennia. Either way, somebody pays.

The committee chose not to break ABI across the library for C++23, while refusing to promise stability forever. In practice, that left existing binaries safe and the frozen types frozen.

That's still roughly where things stand. SD-10 makes a stable ABI the default and allows a break case by case, with the reasons written down. The [ABI Review Group](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee) advises on proposals that touch layout or calling conventions.

<div id="watch-out">

If your proposal changes the layout of a type people already use, expect the ABI question. Answer it in your paper, where you have time to think. On your feet in the room, the same question is much harder.

</div>

### 12.4 The Zero-Overhead Principle

C++ lives by the *zero-overhead principle*. It has two halves: you don't pay for what you don't use, and what you do use runs as fast as hand-written code. Features that break it die in the room, or get switched off in the field.

Exceptions are the famous case: just turning them on makes programs bigger, even if nothing ever throws. Herb Sutter's [P0709R4](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p0709r4.pdf) counts them as one of two language features that break the rule. In a 2018 survey cited there, 52 percent of developers said exceptions were banned in part or all of their code.

The rule shapes every proposal. If your feature slows down code that doesn't use it, expect to defend that cost in writing. The committee protects the people who came for speed.

### 12.5 Freestanding versus Hosted

C++ runs in two very different worlds, and the standard names them. A *hosted* environment has an operating system and the full library behind it. A *freestanding* environment has neither, like a microcontroller, a kernel, or a bootloader.

This split shapes what a library feature can assume. Something that needs files or threads usually won't work freestanding, since there may be no operating system to provide them. A proposal that forgets the freestanding world will hear about it from the people who live there.

<div id="short-version">

The oldest of these rules come from Stroustrup's D&E, so read it before you write a language proposal. Your safest bet is a design that already works in the field. Old code keeps working, so one type has to serve everyone. When a second type does ship, like `std::jthread`, everyone lives with both.

Watch for ABI, since changing a type's layout breaks programs already built, and nobody can rebuild them all. In 2020 the committee chose not to break it for C++23, without promising it never will.

Don't make people pay for a feature they don't use. And remember the freestanding world, where there may be no operating system at all.

</div>

These constraints explain the quiet noes: an idea can be right and still impossible. Knowing them saves you years on a proposal the rules killed before you wrote it.

If you ever present a faster version of an old type, someone will ask what it does to programs compiled a decade ago. You'll have to say it breaks them. The no that follows protects sixteen million users who'll never read your paper. That's the Delegate's Oath from chapter 1, kept by someone else.
