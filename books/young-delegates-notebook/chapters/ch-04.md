## 4. How to Participate Remotely

You don't have to fly anywhere to start. Most of the committee's life happens online, between meetings. All you need is a desk and a browser.

### 4.1 You Can Take Part from Your Desk

Plenty of useful work happens with no plane ticket. You can read, review, and discuss from home, on your own schedule. None of it needs a meeting badge.

The lowest-effort start is reviewing a proposal. The volunteer-run [wg21.org](https://wg21.org) hosts a public [paper-review list](https://lists.wg21.org/mailman3/lists/paper-reviews.lists.wg21.org/) for exactly this. You read a paper that's coming up and post what you found, even if it's one point. A free login with a GitHub or Google account is all it takes.

Each paper gets a one-week review window, and afterward the reviews go into a credited summary that's attached to the paper on [wg21.org](https://wg21.org). And giving reviews earns you reviews of your own.

Much of what follows is open to you today. The rest waits on one step: attending one meeting as a guest, in person or by video. After that, the reflector's closed lists open up.

### 4.2 The Official Sites, and One Unofficial One

Chapter 3 showed you how to find a paper, and these are the sites you'll work from. [isocpp.org](https://isocpp.org) is the public-facing site, home to the standing documents and committee news. Start there for anything official, like the [meetings page](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation) that tells you how to email the convener.

[open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/) is the official archive of every paper and draft, and it's where your own paper will land someday. It's sorted by date, with no search. It's the source of truth, even if it's bare.

[wg21.org](https://wg21.org), home of that review list, is the unofficial one. It's where you'll do most of your browsing: filter the mailing by group, search the full text, and download each group's report. The papers themselves still live on [open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/). Don't confuse it with the wg21.link shortcut from chapter 3.

### 4.3 Floating Ideas: std-proposals

Before you write a formal proposal, float the idea first. [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) is a public list for early-stage ideas. You describe your idea and see what people think.

This can save you months. People often point you to earlier papers on the same problem, and they raise the first objections while changing course is still cheap. Take a warm reply as encouragement, not a verdict. The real test is still the bar in chapter 9.

Subscribe to [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) today and read it for a week before you post. You'll see what a warm reception looks like, and a cold one, before your own idea gets either.

### 4.4 Following Along from Anywhere

Every meeting gets written up. After each one, the results come out fast. Several delegates post public trip reports, like [Herb Sutter's blog](https://herbsutter.com). The official minutes come out as an N-paper, like [N5040](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/n5040.pdf) for the March 2026 meeting, so the record is there to read.

Start with a trip report, since it tells you what mattered and why. Check the minutes when you need the official version.

If you'd rather ask than read, the [#include \<C++\> Discord](https://www.includecpp.org) is open to anyone. It's friendly and full of people who'll help a newcomer. It's a gentle place to learn the culture. Bring your beginner's questions there first.

### 4.5 Open-Source Help: The Beman Project

You can help with code without ever attending. The [Beman Project](https://github.com/bemanproject) builds open-source implementations of proposed standard library features, so people can try them for real.

You can help write and test them, or use them in your own code and report what breaks. A bug report from real code is the kind of evidence a proposal needs before the vote.

### 4.6 One Meeting Opens the Rest

The one step is attending one meeting as a guest, in person or by video. Email the convener first, the way chapter 2 described, and say you're coming.

Meetings are hybrid, so even this step works from your desk. You won't catch everything from afar. The hallway talk, where a lot gets settled, is hard to join remotely.

The other catch if you're far away is time zones. A meeting on another continent can run through your night. Pick the sessions worth losing sleep over, and read the rest later.

So put your effort where distance doesn't count. The rulebook from chapter 3, [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), prefers a concern raised on the committee's email lists before a meeting over one raised in the room. A careful review that lands weeks early often shapes a paper more than a remark from the floor.

### 4.7 The Reflector

The committee's main online home is email. Its mailing lists are called *the reflector*, and most discussion between meetings happens there. Each subgroup (smaller group) keeps its own list.

You can join the lists once you've attended one meeting as a guest, in person or by video. Before that, many topic lists are [readable in public](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi), and the person who runs a topic group can add an expert directly. Once you're in, start with the list for the papers you care about.

One rule matters from day one. Posts on the closed lists are private, so you can't quote them in public. You can share poll questions and their numbers freely, but a person's words need that person's consent. In practice, almost nobody asks, and almost nobody says yes.

Be courteous on the lists, especially when you're sure you're right. Email strips the tone out of an argument and leaves only the words. The people reading yours will run your sessions and review your papers.

A sharp reply on the lists feels good for a minute and costs you a reader for a decade.

### 4.8 The Wiki and Mattermost

The committee runs its meetings off *the wiki*. It holds the agendas, schedules, video-call links, and poll pages for each meeting. If you attend, you'll live in it that week.

It's password-protected, so ask the convener how a guest gets in. Don't edit it unless someone asks you to.

When things move fast, people use chat. [Mattermost](https://chat.isocpp.org), at [chat.isocpp.org](https://chat.isocpp.org), is the committee's own real-time chat, and during meetings it keeps the parallel rooms in touch. Like the wiki, it isn't public, so ask the convener about it too. Keep it open whenever a meeting is running.

The courtesy rule from the reflector holds in every chat, too. Chat is faster than email and even easier to misread.

### 4.9 Telecons between Meetings

The big meetings happen three times a year. Between them, subgroups hold online meetings called *telecons*, [about thirty a month](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation) across the various groups.

The wiki lists the schedule and the links. Telecons take guests too, with the same heads-up to the convener. Pick a subgroup you care about and sit in. Once you're there, you may be asked to take the minutes.

### 4.10 Voting from Afar: Electronic Polls

Not every vote waits for a meeting. Some groups, the library side especially, run polls online between meetings. They use these to settle questions without burning floor time.

The rules are in [P2195R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2021/p2195r2.html), "Electronic Straw Polls." A straw poll is a quick vote that steers a group's work, and chapter 8 shows you how to read one. Each round and its results are published as papers. The May 2025 round, for example, is [P3712R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/p3712r0.html), and its outcomes are in [P3713R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/p3713r0.html).

You can vote in these yourself once you've joined a national body, as chapter 2 described. Otherwise, you qualify for about a year after attending a week-long meeting in person. Vote only on what you've followed. That May poll paper asks anyone who missed the discussion to sit out unless they understand the topic.

Twenty-three people took part in that round, and it sent a `std::format` paper on toward C++29. None of them needed a plane ticket.
