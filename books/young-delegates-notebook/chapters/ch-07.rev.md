## 8. In the Room

The first time in the room, you'll worry about doing something wrong. Don't. This chapter gives you the unwritten rules, and the written ones, so you can relax and pay attention to the work.

<!-- box: In This Chapter list previewing the chapter's topics -->

### 8.1 The Opening Plenary and the Week's Rhythm

The week opens with the whole committee in one room. This is the opening plenary. The officers say hello, the agenda gets set, the updated working drafts are adopted, and then everyone scatters to the subgroups. Don't expect drama here. The opening plenary is short and mostly logistics. The real work happens in the rooms right after.

Once it breaks up, your day becomes a series of choices. You can be in only one room at a time, and six or seven run at once. Choose sessions based on the papers you want to follow. Go where the paper you care about is being discussed. Whatever room you pick, arrive having read its papers. Walking in cold is the classic rookie mistake. The discussion moves fast and won't recap for you.

### 8.2 Meeting Etiquette and How to Speak

The room runs on small courtesies. Don't dominate the discussion, and don't reopen a point the room has moved past. As a newcomer, listen more than you talk. Spend your first sessions learning how the room thinks. You'll earn your voice faster by using it sparingly.

When you do speak, a little technique goes a long way. Different rooms use different queuing methods - smaller rooms usually use raised hands, larger rooms a physical queue at the microphone that you join when you want to speak. Make one point, and keep it to about two minutes. The room has a lot to get through, and short is respected. Two habits help everyone follow you. Say your name before you start, because the scribe and the remote attendees need it. Use the microphone, because without it the online half of the room can't hear you.

### 8.3 Confidentiality and the Code of Conduct

What happens in the room mostly stays in the room. The confidentiality rule means you don't blog, tweet, photograph, or record the sessions. Treat everything said as off the public record by default. Two narrow exceptions exist. You can share poll questions and their numbers freely, but only once the week-long meeting is over. You can quote a person by name only with that person's consent, which is rarely asked and rarely given, so paraphrase instead.

The ISO Code of Ethics and Conduct and the IEC Code of Conduct, which WG21 adopts as its own, cover how people treat each other. They apply everywhere the committee gathers, including chat, meals, and social events. Concerns go through ISO's "Guidance and Process for Addressing Misconduct and Breaches of the Code of Conduct", and the slide shown at the opening of every meeting summarises the expectations. The short version is to be decent and assume good faith.

### 8.4 Minute Taking

Every session has to be minuted, and anyone can be asked to do it. And that is a rule. SD-4 says everyone present should be prepared to take minutes, the result of a plenary poll at Wrocław in 2024. So you might be handed the keyboard on day one. Say yes.

WG21 refers to the minute taker as the “scribe”. Scribing is the best seat in the house for a newcomer, and not only because you follow every word. Writing an argument down is a different act from hearing it. Taking minutes requires you to understand the arguments well enough to summarise them. A week of scribing can teach you a great deal about the design questions your subgroup is considering. In addition the scribe holds a small superpower that nobody else in the room has. The chair keeps a queue of speakers, and the queue is sacred; you wait your turn like everyone else. The scribe does not. If you did not catch a point, or a speaker was unclear, you may interrupt the queue and ask them to say it again, because the minutes are the record and the record must be right. Used honestly, that licence lets you ask the clarifying question everyone else was too proud to ask, and get it answered on the spot. People also notice and remember the person who helped.

### 8.5 Where Relationships Form

The hallway is not break time. A lot of the real work happens there, between sessions. People sort out disagreements over coffee that would stall a formal room. Meals work the same way. Lunch and dinner groups form on their own, so join one. Introducing yourself over a meal is normal and expected.

### 8.6 Get Out of Your Lane

Spend some time in rooms outside your specialty. The Direction Group's own paper asks for it: "Try to spend at least one day each meeting in a WG that isn't 'your own'" ([P2000](https://wg21.link/p2000), "Directions for ISO C++"). You'll build trust across groups and see how the whole machine fits together.

Two habits earn respect fast. Serve before you push: help onboard newcomers and explain procedure. And when someone says "That's just how it works," ask how other groups like IETF or W3C handle the same thing. Above all, keep your wits about you. The meeting has a rhythm that can sweep you up, and belonging can quietly bend your judgment. Stay skeptical, and question the room's assumptions, including your own.
