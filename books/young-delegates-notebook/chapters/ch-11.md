## 11. Championing Your Paper

Your paper's in the mailing, with your name on the front and a P-number of its own. Enjoy that for a day. A paper doesn't pass itself. Getting to yes is a long, social campaign, and it's the part you're most likely to underestimate.

### 11.1 Before the Room: Presocialization

Most papers are won before they're presented. *Presocialization* means talking up your idea in hallways and on the lists, building support ahead of the room. A cold presentation to a surprised room usually stalls.

Once you're in the room, the five minutes before a poll settle what the hallway left open. The chair's summary, the first voice to speak, and the room's mood shape the result. Line up a few supporters who'll speak early, people who actually reviewed the paper and can answer hard questions.

Yes, that builds momentum. Momentum built on merit is fair game. Momentum built on numbers is what chapter 8 told you to resist.

Supporters aren't conjured the week before a poll. You earn them over years, starting with other people's papers. Send an author a careful review, then another on the next revision, and before long authors start asking you to look at their drafts. The people whose papers you review tend to review yours.

A careful review is rare, so authors remember who sent one. Keep at it, and they'll ask for more: a section, an implementation, and finally your name on the paper. People you've coauthored with will speak up for your paper in rooms you can't be in. That's where champions come from.

### 11.2 Finding and Being a Champion

Every paper needs a champion (the person who presents and pushes it, from chapter 2). Often that's you, the author, but it doesn't have to be. If you can't present it yourself, in person or by video, find someone who knows the work well enough to defend it.

You can also champion someone else's paper. Backing a good idea you didn't write builds trust and skill. It's one of the fastest ways to become useful.

### 11.3 The First Gate: "Do We Want It at All?"

The first discussion of your paper is the scary one. The room asks "do we want this at all?" before it touches any detail. A clear no here can kill the idea outright.

Frame your opening as solving a problem the committee already cares about. Tie it to the Direction Group's priorities from chapter 5, laid out in [P5000R1](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p5000r1.pdf). A paper that matches those priorities gets heard first. One that doesn't waits.

Even a wanted paper will take fire.

### 11.4 When the Poll Goes Against You

Most polls against you aren't a death sentence. They send the paper back for more work, and that's the normal path. You take the feedback, revise, and return with a stronger paper.

Know the one risky move: reopening a settled question. *Re-litigation* is re-debating something the room already decided, and it costs you credibility. Reopen only with genuinely new evidence. The same argument twice costs you twice.

### 11.5 Negotiating: Back-Pocket and Max-Min

Always have a fallback. A *back-pocket alternative* is a second design you're ready to offer if your first choice is rejected. Walking in with one keeps you from leaving empty-handed.

When the room is split, shrink the ask. The *max-min solution* is the smallest version everyone can accept. A small win that ships beats a big proposal that stalls.

### 11.6 Disagreement versus Opposition, and When to Withdraw

Learn to tell two things apart. Disagreement is a technical objection you can answer with evidence or a better design. Opposition is someone who doesn't move, however well you answer. The two need different responses.

Answer disagreement in full, before the poll and again in your next revision. That's how papers get better. Answer opposition once, on the record, then stop trying to win that person over. Watch the room instead. One holdout rarely sinks a paper. A small group that shares the objection is a different story.

Sometimes the right move is to stop. If the room clearly doesn't want it and won't budge, withdraw the paper with grace.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Knowing when to stop is a skill, not a defeat. A dead paper keeps eating the time and credibility your next one will need.

### 11.7 How Reputation Works

In a room of volunteers, reputation is what gets your paper read. People can't review every proposal, so they weigh who wrote it. A trusted name gets the benefit of the doubt, while a new one gets extra scrutiny.

You build it in small steps, and the first ones are the careful reviews you send other authors. Then you become the reliable expert in one narrow area, and you spend your credibility only on the battles that matter. Reputation compounds each time you turn out to be right.

### 11.8 The Presenter Is Judged Too

When you stand up to present, you're judged again, live. Are you reasonable, willing to concede a fair point, someone people want to work with? Those signals shape how your paper lands.

Don't be the obstacle. Causing needless delay, or escalating every dispute, marks you as hard to work with. The escalation path from chapter 8 exists, but [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) warns that using it topic after topic erodes your credibility.

Persistence does pay, though. *Procedural momentum* is the benefit a paper earns from many revisions and prior polls. By the fourth or fifth revision, the room often assumes it'll pass, so steady iteration works in your favor. When you're the one voting, remember that a revision count isn't evidence.

### 11.9 The Long Game

Set your clock to years, not months. The train runs every three years, and most features ride more than one. Patience is the job.

The famous features all took ages. Coroutines, modules, and the executors work that became [`std::execution`](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p2300r10.html) each ran many years and many revisions. Stackful coroutines have waited over a decade. Their paper got to [revision 24](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p0876r24.pdf) in July 2026, and they still aren't in.

### 11.10 The Structural Headwinds

Some headwinds aren't about you. The first is the *Bandwidth Gap*: far more papers arrive than anyone can review. Chapter 3 counted eight hundred or more a year. Nobody tracks what delegates read, but a typical one gets through a few dozen, mostly for their own room.

<div id="watch-out">

A quiet inbox isn't a *no*. When nobody writes back about your paper, most people simply haven't read it yet. Give up over that, and you've lost to a crowded mailing, not to a bad idea.

</div>

Write for the reader who has two minutes: put your abstract and a Tony Table (chapter 9's before-and-after code) right up front.

Another is the *expert bubble*. Papers are often written by experts for experts, which makes it hard for newcomers and outside ideas to break in.

Two habits poke holes in it. Review other people's papers on [wg21.org](https://wg21.org), where your careful read sits in public beside the paper. And give your own paper the short tutorial from chapter 9, written for ordinary programmers.

Resources tilt the field, too. Everyone in the room is a volunteer, as chapter 5 said, but some are paid by an employer to be there.

Proposals with that paid time and a funded implementation move faster than ones built on spare evenings. It's not a conspiracy, just the weight of who can afford to show up every cycle.

If you're on your own time, keep the paper small enough to finish. Then look for a compiler or library developer who wants the feature. For a library feature, the [Beman Project](https://github.com/bemanproject) from chapter 4 gives you a place to build it in the open.

All three headwinds wear people down, and you'll feel them too.

### 11.11 The Emotional Side

Having your paper picked apart stings, and that's normal. Don't take it personally. The room argues hard about every idea, and today it was yours.

Your slot ends, the poll goes up, and the numbers send you back. For an hour it feels like the room voted on you. That night you reread your notes, and two of the objections are fair. By breakfast you've sketched the fix. R1 has its first change.

Burnout is real here, and you won't be the only one who feels it. Good people leave because of it. In a [2020 pulse poll](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2020/p2260r0.pdf), over 60 percent of those who answered said they often felt exhausted by meetings and other demands. That was the year meetings moved online.

Set your limits before you need them. Keep to your home room and one more, cap your telecons, and read the papers you'll vote on instead of the whole mailing. Let an evening session go when you're spent. And know when to step back for a meeting or two.

The committee runs for decades, and it needs you around for more than one standard. A heroic burst burns out first.

<div id="short-version">

Win your paper in the hallway before the room, and earn supporters years earlier by reviewing, then coauthoring, other people's papers. Find a champion or be one. Frame your idea around the Direction Group's priorities. A poll that sends you back is feedback for the next revision, but don't re-litigate without new evidence.

Keep a back-pocket alternative ready, and shrink to the max-min solution when the room splits. Answer disagreement in full and opposition once, and know when to withdraw. Reputation compounds, and so does procedural momentum. Think in years, mind the Bandwidth Gap and the expert bubble, and set your limits before you need them.

</div>

Your name stays on the front for years, so plan to last all of them. Some proposals die for reasons no campaign can fix. Next, the deep design rules that decide what's even possible.
