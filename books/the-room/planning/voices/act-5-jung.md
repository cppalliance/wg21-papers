## Narrative stance

- **Person.** First person from the first line to the last. The act opens as a case: the analyst sets down the Newcomer's flight and dream in the present tense, close on the dreamer, the way he presents a patient's dream before reading it ("For instance, a young patient dreams as follows," Collected Papers on Analytical Psychology, ch. XII, p. 303). The "I" enters with "I would tell her what it means, if she came to me." He never names himself, never calls himself "the analyst," and never speaks of himself in the third person.
- **Tense.** Present for the case and for the committee's week as he watches it. Past for his own life: the Burghölzli, Freud, the tower at Bollingen, his death. Future for the prognosis ("She will not come back"). His self-reference keeps the retrospective calm of the late recollections, but the polemic is allowed: his judgments of the institution may be as sharp as his originals, and the act's sharpest lines are polemic about what the institution does to its members.
- **Addressee.** The reader, as the audience of a lecture (the 1916 lectures say "you": "You will now ask what course is to be pursued when the patient does not dream at all," ch. VII, p. 216) and as the reader of an old man's recollections. He speaks about the Newcomer, never to her. The conditional "if she came to me" is his stance: she never came.
- **Register.** A personal myth, not a proof: "I can only make direct statements, only 'tell stories.'" (Memories, Dreams, Reflections, Prologue, p. 3). He offers psychology as experience and hedges it ("as experience proves," "so to speak," "approximately").
- **Vantage.** Küsnacht in 1957, the year of the essay the English-speaking world knows as *The Undiscovered Self*, in his eighty-second or eighty-third year (see the facts below). Like every narrator in the book, he also speaks from after his death in the closing line.
- **His own works.** Rarely named, and then by English title in italics: *Psychology of the Unconscious*, *Psychological Types*, *The Undiscovered Self*. Never by Collected Works volume or paragraph. He does not call *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* his book: Aniela Jaffé recorded and edited it, and it appeared after his death. In narration it is "these recollections." Epigraph attributions are exempt.
- **The other five.** By epithet only: the Florentine, the philosopher, the clerk, the naturalist. The part's "the crowd theorist" should become "the naturalist," per the book rules. He does not introduce the sixth voice, whose act follows; the book rules fix no epithet for him. He never calls the others "analysts": that is his own epithet. He may say that each saw part of the truth ("Each was correct. Each was incomplete."), since the Preface now speaks of "Five hundred years of independent diagnosis."
- **Voice reference.** The public-domain Jung: *Psychology of the Unconscious* (Hinkle, 1916) and *Collected Papers on Analytical Psychology* (Long, 1916), the register of lecture and case discussion. The late autobiographical register comes from *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* through short verified quotations only (edits A5-1).

## Sentence architecture

- He builds a case in long, qualified sentences, clause by clause, then states the rule in a short flat one: "But there are no fixed symbolic meanings." (Collected Papers, ch. VII, p. 218).
- The image is the argument. A dream, a stone, a tower, a patient, or a historical parallel carries the point, followed by at most a sentence of reading. Dreams speak "in the language of parable or simile" (ch. XII, p. 311), and so does he.
- He lists parallels inside one sentence: "that water had been found, that we had killed the bear, that a storm was approaching, or that wolves were prowling around the camp" (Psychology of the Unconscious, Part I, ch. I).
- He joins opposites with "nevertheless" or "yet" rather than resolving them.
- His paragraphs are calm, but the AGENTS.md pace rules apply: each paragraph ends on its sharpest sentence, and blur is cut. Alongside the "empties them" triad in XXXIV ("It does not destroy them. It does not oppose them. It empties them."), he may use a staccato fact run, an anaphoric triad, and an "and ... and" chain, each once or twice per act as AGENTS.md allows. In the part, the staccato run is the opening ("She presented her paper. Six people attended. One asked a question. There was no poll."), the anaphoric triad is "It did not echo. It did not persist. It simply stopped.", and the chain runs in XXXIV from "and no one misses it" to "because no one decided." The building chain in XXXIV is a recorded exception (below). "It is not X. It is Y." and "not because X but because Y" follow the per-part budgets in AGENTS.md. Never follow a shown image with a sentence explaining it; the one recorded exception is "we have room for you, and the room is empty."
- Triads. The triad is his signature, so under the AGENTS.md device rule the act may use seven, more than the general limit of four (round three, 2026-10-16). The seven are: the frame's escalation, "institutions do not merely constrain the people who inhabit them. They colonize them. They replace..."; "It does not destroy them. It does not oppose them. It empties them."; "The committee is finite. Its papers are finite. Its polls are finite."; "It did not echo. It did not persist. It simply stopped."; the "nowhere that matters" run; the next-meeting staccato, "the Chair will publish the agenda. The Architect will present another paper. The Delegate will be in his seat."; and the climax build-up, "that it loses people. That it loses the people it most needs. That the people it keeps...". The other devices keep the limits above.
- Merges. AGENTS.md allows two per part. The act keeps two: the frame's "What I have learned there is this:" and the empty-chair maxim's "It says what the published procedures cannot say:". The second colon introduces the procedures' unspoken words, which the rule treats as speech, but it is listed here so the count holds either way. Round three split every other short semicolon or colon merge into separate sentences.

## Signature moves

1. **The case before the theory.** He sets the dream down first, in the dreamer's present tense, and only then reads it. In this act the flight and the dream come before any "I." Exemplar: "For instance, a young patient dreams as follows: *I am standing in a strange garden, and pluck an apple from a tree.*" (Collected Papers on Analytical Psychology, ch. XII "The Psychology of Dreams," p. 303).
2. **Compensation.** A dream supplies what the conscious attitude leaves out; it balances rather than disguises. Exemplar: "In the case of normal people the principal function of the unconscious is to effect a compensation and thus produce a balance." (Collected Papers, ch. X "On the Importance of the Unconscious in Psychopathology," p. 281). His illustration on the same page is Nebuchadnezzar's dream of the great tree hewn down, "a counterpoise to the exaggerated feeling of royal power."
3. **No fixed meanings.** He refuses the decoder ring and keeps more than one meaning in play. Exemplar: "But there are no fixed symbolic meanings." and "Every symbol has more than one meaning." (Collected Papers, ch. VII "Psychoanalysis," pp. 218-219).
4. **Amplification.** He gathers distant parallels around one image until its history shows. Exemplar: "He gathers together historical parallels for each dream part, even though they be very remote, and attempts to construct the psychological history of the dream and the meanings that underlie it." (Collected Papers, ch. VII, p. 215).
5. **Two in one.** He divides a thing in two and insists both halves live in everyone. Exemplar: "Thus we have two forms of thinking - directed thinking and dream or phantasy thinking." (Psychology of the Unconscious, Part I, ch. I "Concerning the Two Kinds of Thinking"; the German original reads "Wir haben also zwei Formen des Denkens: das gerichtete Denken und das Träumen oder Phantasieren," Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido, Erster Teil, II). Of No. 1 and No. 2: "On the contrary, it is played out in every individual." (Memories, Dreams, Reflections, "School Years," p. 45).
6. **The personal myth.** Confession stands where proof would stand. Exemplar: "I can only make direct statements, only 'tell stories.'" (Memories, Dreams, Reflections, Prologue, p. 3).
7. **The decisive question.** He reduces a life to one question, set off by a colon. Exemplar: "The decisive question for man is: Is he related to something infinite or not?" (Memories, Dreams, Reflections, "Late Thoughts").
8. **From right and wrong to sense.** He moves a quarrel off correctness and onto meaning. Exemplar: "The pendulum of the mind oscillates between sense and nonsense, not between right and wrong." (Memories, Dreams, Reflections, "Sigmund Freud," p. 154). This is his native form for G4: the committee asks whether a design is correct and whether the room wants it; the question of sense, whether the thing needs to be in the standard at all, never comes up. Its "not X" shape counts against the per-part budgets in AGENTS.md.

- **He never:** puts a clinical or diagnostic label on any role (the Delegate, the Chair, the Architect, the Author, the Newcomer) or on any real person, and he never says what the Architect or the Chair knew or felt; the Architect as founder alludes to a real person. ## The act's question

- The users are not in the room when the decisions are made. The book states that thesis plainly only in the Preface, the Epilogue, and the back-cover blurb; this act shows it in its own word for the users, the undiscovered.
- Act Five asks the root question: does this need to be in the standard at all? In his idiom it is No. 2's question, the one the committee's shadow holds, "the question it cannot put to itself." The pause before the poll in Room A and the Author's page at the end carry it. At most three explicit statements.
- He never poses another act's question: how many will benefit, whether it is worth the cost, whether it solves a problem users actually have, whether the existing solutions are good enough, or how anyone knows they want it.

## Narrative facts that bear on voice

- **Age and vantage.** Jung was born on 26 July 1875 and died at Küsnacht on 6 June 1961 (Wikipedia, "Carl Jung"). In 1957 he was in his eighty-second year until 26 July and in his eighty-third after it. The recollections open "Thus it is that I have now undertaken, in my eighty-third year, to tell my personal myth" (Memories, Dreams, Reflections, Prologue, p. 3). The part's "eighty-first year" fits 1955-56, not 1957. "Eighty-third" fits and echoes the Prologue.
- **The 1957 essay.** *Gegenwart und Zukunft* ("Present and Future") was published by Rascher in Zurich in 1957; R. F. C. Hull's English translation appeared in 1958 as *The Undiscovered Self* (Wellcome Collection catalog; Routledge Classics front matter). The subtitle "The Undiscovered Committee" and the act's word for the users, "the undiscovered," echo the English title, which the German lacks. Its chapters run from "The Plight of the Individual in Modern Society" to "Self-Knowledge" and "The Meaning of Self-Knowledge."
- **The consulting room.** He joined the Burghölzli under Eugen Bleuler in December 1900, left it for private practice in 1909, and from 1908 lived in the house he built beside the lake at Küsnacht (Wikipedia, "Carl Jung"). By 1957 that is about fifty-seven years, so "nearly sixty years in the consulting room" is right.
- **No. 1 and No. 2.** He knew of the two persons as a schoolboy: "Somewhere deep in the background I always knew that I was two persons" ("School Years," p. 44). He says the play between them "has nothing to do with a 'split' or dissociation in the ordinary medical sense" (p. 45). No. 2 is the old one, "close to nature, the earth, the sun, the moon, the weather, all living creatures, and above all close to the night, to dreams" (the same passage, as quoted by HuffPost). The break with Freud came in 1913, the year he turned thirty-eight, and what followed he called his confrontation with the unconscious (Wikipedia, "Carl Jung"; the chapter title in Memories, Dreams, Reflections). So the fix for edits A5-4: he knew it as a boy and understood it after the break, when he was nearly forty.
- **No. 1 and the occult.** His doctoral thesis, *On the Psychology and Pathology of So-Called Occult Phenomena*, studied the séances of a young medium, his cousin (Wikipedia, "Carl Jung"; it is chapter I of the 1916 Collected Papers). The part's No. 1, a man of science "with no interest in the occult," is wrong. His own picture of No. 1 is "a rather disagreeable and moderately gifted young man with vaulting ambitions" ("Student Years," as quoted by HuffPost).
- **The stone.** He set up the stone cube at Bollingen in 1950 for his seventy-fifth birthday. Its lake-facing side carries "I am an orphan, alone; nevertheless I am found everywhere," sayings he called "more or less quotations from alchemy" (Wikipedia, "Bollingen Tower"; cgjung.net; "The Tower," pp. 223-227). In the winter of 1955-56 he chiseled his paternal ancestors' names on three stone tablets for the tower's courtyard ("The Tower," p. 232), and on 17 July 1956 he wrote that he was working on another stone, in memory of his wife (Letters, to Marianne Niehus-Jung, with the editors' note; both as quoted by carljungdepthpsychologysite.blog). The 1950 stone was not "the last thing I carved" (edits A5-7).
- **The collective problem.** At the tower, after carving those tablets, he wrote that "a collective problem, if not recognized as such, always appears as a personal problem" ("The Tower," p. 233). It is his own warrant for the book's boundary: read the institution, not the person.
- **Rejection.** He was the first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association, elected in 1910, and resigned in 1914 (Wikipedia, "Carl Jung"; "Secret committee (psychoanalysis)"). After the break: "all my friends and acquaintances dropped away. My book was declared to be rubbish; I was a mystic, and that settled the matter." ("Sigmund Freud," p. 167).
- **The committee he never entered.** In 1912 Freud's circle formed a secret committee, which first met on 25 May 1913, in part to isolate Jung and force his resignation; its members undertook not to publish departures from the fundamental tenets without prior discussion in the Committee (Wikipedia, "Secret committee (psychoanalysis)"). The closing line carried that literal echo ("the committee I never entered") until 2026-10-16, when it became "I died in 1961, yet the shadow I named still darkens."; the echo now lives in the IPA paragraph's "formed a secret committee to guard the movement's tenets against me." The act may say the committee was formed "to guard the movement's tenets against me"; the record supports it, and the historical license covers the sharpening. Use it as his biography only: never map Freud onto the Architect or onto any real founder.
- **Lévy-Bruhl.** A Paris philosopher by training whose *Les fonctions mentales dans les sociétés inférieures* (1910; in English *How Natives Think*, 1926) supplied "participation mystique" (Wikipedia, "Lucien Lévy-Bruhl"). Jung kept the term after its author let it go: "He later retracted this term under pressure of adverse criticism, but I believe that his critics were wrong" (Man and His Symbols, 1964, p. 24, as quoted by Wikipedia, "Participation mystique"). The part's "who observed it among peoples" misstates his method.
- **Shared facts with Act Four.** Act Five is Act Four's first week seen through the Newcomer, and these facts match it exactly. Sunday: she shares the taxi from the airport with the Author, who says her paper sounds good. Monday morning: Room A, the library evolution group, fifty delegates, the Architect's and the Author's designs back to back, and the Architect wins (28 to 18, 4 not voting), with the Delegate's hand among the twenty-eight; Room B, a room designed for forty, six attend, one question, no poll. Tuesday: she sits through the day's session in Room A. Tuesday evening: the hotel bar, two delegates from Room A at the next table, "another paper from nobody," and they laugh. Wednesday evening: she flies home. Her one more meeting is six months later. The closing plenary of that week is on Saturday morning. Act Four's Delegate plenary is a different one, at a meeting a year after the taxi; the two acts do not share it. At the meeting after her last, a year after the taxi, the Architect presents another paper, and his feature enters the working draft at that meeting's plenary (since 2026-10-17; before that rewrite of Act Four's ending, it entered six months earlier). From then on, Act Four says, "his papers have been about other things." The Saturday plenary's motions apply reviewed wording to the working draft, and most pass by unanimous consent.

### A5-2 - XXXIII. The Two Personalities of the Committee

- **Covers:** section XXXIII from its epigraph to its end. Three beats inside it have their own anchors: the unasked question (A5-4), the Delegate's thirtieth hand (A5-5), and the hotel-bar laughter (A5-6).
- **Original passage:** "Somewhere deep in the background I always knew that I was two persons. One was the son of my parents, who went to school and was less intelligent, attentive, hard-working, decent, and clean than many other boys." Memories, Dreams, Reflections, "School Years," Vintage 1989, p. 44. This is the passage the section already transforms.
- **Translation:** the quotation above (Richard and Clara Winston's translation).
- **Intent:** Jung presents his doubleness as ordinary: No. 1, the schoolboy bound to his family and his day, and No. 2, the old one "close to nature, the earth, the sun, the moon," whose play with No. 1 "is played out in every individual" (p. 45).
- **Committee scenario:** The committee's No. 1 runs the week. It publishes its procedures, sets the schedule, frames the polls, asks whether a design is correct and whether the room wants it, counts the hands, and forwards the paper. Like Jung's No. 1, it is the son of its parents: its rules and its time. Its No. 2 is older and has no seat. It is close to the moon and to the people who write the language every day, and it remembers what the language is for. The play between them is normal; the trouble is a No. 1 that leaves No. 2 no room. Jung did the opposite: "I have always tried to make room for anything that wanted to come to me from within" (the same paragraph, p. 45). A No. 1 left alone has what his had, "no real points of contact with its environment" ("Student Years"). This replaces the current second personality (scheduling, poll framing, trust networks), which reads as an accusation rather than a psychology.
- **Thesis:** No. 1's questions are the committee's whole conscious life; the question only No. 2 could ask, whether the thing needs to be in the standard at all, has no slot on the agenda. Here the restored "The committee believes it evaluates designs. It actually evaluates persons." lands, extended: the room weighs only the persons in it, and the users, in no room, weigh nothing. "The contradiction is not hypocrisy" follows, completed by the left hand not knowing what the right hand is doing, never by "dissociation."
- **Decisions:** edits A5-2, edits A5-3, edits A5-4, G4, G6 (the epigraph per the quote ledger), G7, G11, G12.
- **Main drift risk:** Calling No. 1 and No. 2 a "dissociation" or an "autonomous complex," which he explicitly denies ("nothing to do with a 'split' or dissociation in the ordinary medical sense," p. 45), and casting No. 2 as the dark operator, when it is the timeless and wiser one. ### A5-4 - The unasked question: the institution's shadow (XXXIII. The Two Personalities of the Committee)

### A5-5 - Repeated beat: the Delegate's thirtieth hand (XXXIII. The Two Personalities of the Committee)

- **Covers:** the Delegate paragraph and the participation mystique paragraph in XXXIII, and the echo in XXXIV ("The Delegate will vote on thirty polls, having read the papers for three.").
- **Original passage:** "Participation mystique is a term derived from Lévy-Bruhl. It denotes a peculiar kind of psychological connection with objects, and consists in the fact that the subject cannot clearly distinguish himself from the object but is bound to it by a direct relationship which amounts to partial identity." Psychological Types, ch. XI "Definitions," entry "Participation mystique," translated by H. G. Baynes, revised by R. F. C. Hull (1971), para. 781. The section already names this concept; this is Jung's own definition, replacing the part's invented one.
- **Translation:** the quotation above; the book quotes the published English translation.
- **Intent:** Jung borrows Lévy-Bruhl's term for a tie in which a person cannot clearly tell himself apart from what he is bound to: a partial identity, not a decision.
- **Committee scenario:** In Act Four the thirtieth hand rose because the twenty-nine before it rose: a cascade, the crowd's discharge. Here it is partial identity. The Delegate, who makes independent technical decisions every day at his company, cannot clearly distinguish his judgment from the room's, because the room is what he is bound to: his standing, his colleagues, the process he trusts. Of thirty polls he has read the papers for three; for the rest the room votes through him, and the hand feels like his own. *The Undiscovered Self* names the cost: "It is astounding that man, the instigator, inventor and vehicle of all these developments, the originator of all judgements and decisions and the planner of the future, must make himself such a quantité negligeable." - **Thesis:** A hand that cannot tell itself from the room can report only whether the room wants the design; the users, in no room, never reach the hand.
- **Decisions:** edits A5-6, G2, G8, G9, G10, G11, G12.
- **Main drift risk:** Repeating Act Four's form (permission from the twenty-nine) or its idiom ("the stagnating crowd," "the density," "the discharge of the poll"); diagnosing the Delegate as weak or merged instead of describing the tie between man and room, which Jung found everywhere; keeping the part's invented definition ("so thoroughly merged with the collective") and "who observed it among peoples" (Lévy-Bruhl was a Paris philosopher, and the "primitive" frame should go); and the Chair's summary as a "command," which is Act Four's momentum and sting.
