# Act Six

## The Congress of the Standard

*Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand, 1754-1838*

---

I have outlived them all.

I ordered that my memoirs should not appear until thirty years after my death, so that no one I named in them could suffer from what I wrote.

So I come to this table last. The Florentine secretary mapped his principality in 1513, and I outlived him by three centuries; the other four wrote after I was in my grave. The philosopher diagnosed the values in 1887, when I had been dead forty-nine years, and yet I recognized every word, because I had lived inside the institutions he could only observe from outside. The clerk in Prague walked his corridors in 1922 and died of tuberculosis at forty; he held no power, built no institution, and did nothing but describe what it is to be processed by a system that does not see you. The naturalist in Hampstead documented the crowd in 1960, after forty years of watching it. The analyst in Küsnacht entered the consulting room in 1957, heard what the others could not, and prescribed nothing. I spent fifty years acting. The difference is the difference between the anatomist and the surgeon. Both understand the body. Only one cuts.

I speak at the threshold because I spent a long life among ceremonies and saw them made and unmade. I knew those of the old monarchy: the levee, the diplomatic reception, and the coronation of Louis XVI at Reims, which I attended in 1775 as a young cleric; never did so brilliant a spring come before so stormy an autumn, so deadly a winter. I knew those of the Revolution: the Feast of the Federation on the Champ de Mars, where on the fourteenth of July 1790 I celebrated the Mass on the altar of the fatherland before three hundred thousand Parisians; then the oath, the tribunal, the tumbril. I knew those of the Empire: the coronation at Notre-Dame, where I held the Emperor's honors while Napoleon crowned himself. I knew those of the Restoration: the charter, the congress, the protocol. I watched France change its cockade five times, and the men who pinned it on were the same men. In the declaration I made at eighty-two, weighing the acts of a political life that had been long, I found that of all the governments I had served there was none from which I received more than I gave, and that I abandoned none of them before it had abandoned itself. Each of them taught me what can be learned only in service: that ceremonies are not decorations. They are the load-bearing structure of institutional life. When they lose their meaning the institution falls; when they are rebuilt on a new foundation, it lasts. The question is never whether the ceremonies will change. The question is whether anyone understands the ceremony well enough to build the next one.

I have been shown a committee, and five books to read it by. A minister who receives five dispatches on one province holds not five proofs but five ways of seeing it, and he must still decide. Held up to the committee one after another, they show me a room in which the standing of an author weighs with the merit of the paper, values created by the powerful to describe the powerful, and a result judged in the end not by the room that produces it but by the unheard many who must use it. No one abandons an institution; it abandons itself, when its ceremonies stop asking whom they serve.

Now let me see what can be done.

---

## XXXV. The Assessment

The committee's ceremonies are intact. The mailings appear month after month. The study groups convene. The working groups poll. The plenary votes the wording into the draft. The national bodies ballot. The standard ships every three years with the regularity of a tide. The ceremonies run. They are performed with the solemnity of officials who believe in the forms they administer. In the rooms where designs are chosen, the forms are beautiful. They are also empty.

A man who has served five regimes learns to recognize the moment when a ceremony becomes a form without content. So it was with the monarchy that came out of the Constituent Assembly: royalty was now no more than a shade, and a shade that faded a little more each day. The moment makes no noise; there is no fall of the Bastille in it and no abdication, only the day when the official who performs the ceremony can no longer say what it is for, and justifies it by its existence. The consensus polls in those rooms have reached that day. They were built to gather the judgment of delegates who had read the papers before them. They still ask what they were built to ask, whether a design is correct and whether the room wants it. But the mailings around a single meeting now bring more than two hundred papers, and the room cannot have read all it is polling. The poll is taken, the hands are counted, and what is counted is the room.

I have read the five dispatches. The Florentine saw the principality and called the Chair a hereditary prince, for whom "it is enough not to pass beyond the order of his ancestors." The philosopher saw the temple and wrote, of the same office, "The Chair administers suffering." The clerk saw the castle and found the most honest sentence it had to offer: "We will let you know." The naturalist saw the crowd and wrote, of its hands, "The Delegate's hand does not carry his judgment." The analyst saw the consulting room and named the committee's shadow "the question it cannot put to itself."

Five diagnoses. Same patient. The patient has survived every diagnosis because diagnosis alone does not cure. The Florentine diagnosed from exile, the philosopher from a mountaintop, the clerk from a sickbed, the naturalist from a library, and the analyst from beside the lake. None of them operated. None of them built. They described the disease with the precision of men who never held a scalpel, and the institution survived their descriptions the way a chronic patient survives a stack of accurate medical reports: the reports accumulate in a drawer, and the patient goes on living exactly as before.

The reforms arrived one after another, and each of them added a form. The train model: a three-year cadence in which the schedule, not the review, is what cannot give way. Technical Specifications: an escape valve that let the committee ship features without committing to them; it used the valve to defer rather than decide, and has since largely abandoned it. New study groups: rooms numbered to twenty-three, eight of them now dormant, where papers circulate before reaching the working group, adding delay without decision. A proposal to write ISO's prohibition of machine-written text into the committee's paper rules: a defense of the ceremonies against the tools that could make them unnecessary. Each reform tried to give the shade its reality back by adding to it, as the zealous of 1791 tried with the monarchy. Each addressed a symptom. None addressed the architecture.

The cold policy, which adds nothing that is not needed, has had its advocates, the committee's own Direction Group among them, and little hearing. To the young men of my ministry I said only: "Above all, gentlemen, no zeal." But to whom could one make so cold a policy understood?

At Erfurt, my place as grand chamberlain let me see from close at hand the homage paid to Napoleon, forced, feigned, or even sincere. From so close one learns that the bow tells you nothing of what it is worth. I stand as close to the committee now. A hand raised in its poll has the same form whether it rests on a reading of the paper, on trust in the author, or only on the wish of the room. The revision cycle was designed to improve papers. It domesticates authors. The poll still has its five options, the study group its chair, and the revision its number. The forms are perfect. The content is gone.

## XXXVI. The Meditation

I consider the question of legitimacy. It is the only question that matters, and it is the question the other five did not ask, because they were diagnosticians, not statesmen.

The committee's legitimacy rests on a single claim: that its process produces the best possible standard for the language. The claim was once true, or true enough. In the early years the committee was small, and the papers of the whole first standard, eight years of mailings, came to little more than a single year of mailings brings now; a delegate could read what was put to the vote. The poll gathered a consensus of readers, the working group ripened a proposal among those who knew its subject, and the revision improved the paper in hand. The ceremonies were legitimate because they produced what they claimed to produce.

That was a long time ago. The committee grew and the papers multiplied; the bandwidth gap opened, and the peerage filled it; and the ceremonies went on. In the same declaration I wrote that a monarch who makes himself more than a monarch "loses all right to a title that his own acts have made, or would make, a lie." The process produces a standard. The standard has an ISO number. The ISO number is legitimate. The title stands, and only its acts can make it a lie.

In the spring of 1814 the Emperor of Russia put to me the question that ought to stand at the head of every such proceeding. "How can I know," he asked me, "that France wants the house of Bourbon?" "By a deliberation, Sire, which I undertake to have the Senate pass, and whose effect Your Majesty will see at once." "You are sure of it?" "I answer for it, Sire." I convened the Senate on April 2, and that evening at seven o'clock I brought the Emperor Alexander the memorable deliberation, which I had had signed individually by every member of the assembly. A deliberation measures those who deliberate, and I knew, since my own hand had obtained it, that one can be made to answer a question it was never built to ask; but the question had at least been put, and by a sovereign, before anything was done. In the committee's rooms nobody puts it.

At Vienna I had a seat because the treaty of Paris required every power engaged in the war to send plenipotentiaries; those the standard is for send none. To the Florentine they were the uncounted, to the philosopher the unnamed, to the clerk the unseen, to the naturalist the unasked, and to the analyst the undiscovered; a diplomat has one word for a people with no plenipotentiary at the table, and it is the unheard.

The chair calls the poll on two designs, and no one in the room holds instructions from the unheard. The poll settles which design the room prefers.

How do we know they want it?

Adoption is the real ratification. A ceremony that produces something the unheard use is legitimate; a ceremony that produces something they ignore is merely legal. The committee votes, the compilers implement, and the developers adopt or refuse to adopt, at the pace their compilers and their codebases allow. The standard of nearly a decade ago is in wide use, the next in less, the one after that in less still, and the latest in little; no treaty is ratified on the day it is signed.

That same spring, with the allied armies in Paris, I had told the Emperor of Russia that neither he, nor the allied powers, nor I could give France a king; a king of their choosing would be imposed, and to establish something durable, something accepted without protest, one must act on a principle. "With a principle we are strong; we shall meet no resistance; whatever opposition there is will fade in a short time." The room can vote a feature into the standard as the allies might have chosen a king. The principle lies outside the room, in the consent of those the standard is for. The process reassures the room that its judgment was sound. But the developer who reaches for a feature the room elevated learns only on a Monday morning what was decided in his name.

I pause here. I have seen this before: at Versailles, where the ceremonies of the Sun King continued long after they lost their meaning; in the Directory, where the forms of republican governance concealed the reality of factional chaos; in the Empire, where Napoleon performed the ceremonies of legitimate succession while conquering by force; and in the Restoration, where Louis XVIII governed through a charter that everyone swore to and no one believed. In 1830 that throne fell. Each time someone had to build new ceremonies on a foundation strong enough to bear what the old ones no longer bore.

And then I notice something I did not expect.

Someone is already building: the man I will call the Builder.

---

## XXXVII. The New Ceremonies

I return to the question of ceremony. It is the only question I am qualified to answer. A diagnosis tells you what is dying. It does not tell you what will replace it. That is a different discipline, the discipline of the man who walks through the rubble of a regime that has just fallen and begins measuring the ground for new foundations.

The Builder is measuring.

I judge each of his instruments as I would an envoy sent to a table where the unheard have no seat. An envoy is not the people he speaks for; but a people without a plenipotentiary is better served by an envoy than by an empty chair.

He publishes retrospectives sourced from the institution's own record - not accusations, but documentation. The institution cannot dispute its own minutes. At Vienna, at the conference of the thirtieth of September 1814, Prince Metternich and Prince Hardenberg still spoke of the allied powers, and I, the minister of the power they had defeated, repeated their word with a little astonishment. "Allied," I said, "and against whom? It is no longer against Napoleon: he is on the island of Elba. It is no longer against France: the peace is made. It is surely not against the King of France: he is the guarantor that this peace will last. Gentlemen, let us speak frankly: if there are still allied powers, I am one too many here." It was the defeated who disarmed the victors that day, and with nothing heavier than the victors' own word.

He arrives at the mailing not with one paper but with twenty. Nineteen ask for nothing. One makes a single request. The committee now knows the formation on sight, and so do I, because I stood in it at Vienna. "Gentlemen, I am perhaps the only one here who asks for nothing. [...] I want nothing, I repeat it; and I bring you a great deal." A man who asks for nothing cannot be refused. I have not seen a formation better ordered since my own.

He employs a reader that never tires and serves no faction. It weighs every paper in every mailing and sets down what mandate each can show from outside the room. Delegates read these notes and take them for their own. The best embassy is the one its host takes for his own. The notes were written for the unheard, who can consult them on the day a feature confounds them and learn, as a rule, that it never had a mandate. They put the Emperor's question to every paper. In fifty years I never knew an envoy whose instructions were published; this one's are, so that anyone may read what it was sent to ask, and anyone who doubts its answers may send it again with the same instructions and compare. An instrument that makes information available to everyone can only be opposed by those willing to argue publicly that information should remain scarce. Few officials will make that argument.

Outside the committee's walls he keeps a house, open to anyone, where the mailings can be read in better order, and beside it a press that prints books without waiting thirty years. At Vienna I built new ceremonies on an old principle, legitimacy; they had only to be better than the alternative, which was Napoleon. They held for a century. Not because I was admirable. Because the foundations were correctly laid.

When I resigned in September 1815, Prince Metternich and Lord Castlereagh came to beg me not to separate myself from them, and Lord Castlereagh went so far as to ask, "Why will you not be a minister of Europe with us?" "Because," I answered him, "I wish to be only the minister of France." A man may serve many governments and one constituency. The house is the minister of the unheard and of no one else, and what the committee did not build, it cannot close.

Last, he has opened a public room where each paper is argued over in the language of the street, so that the many can follow what was written for the few. The voices in it are likenesses, made and not born, and it says so. At Vienna the four powers laid before me a protocol whose claims rested on treaties unknown to us. To discuss its reasoning would have been to throw myself into an ocean of disputes, so I read several paragraphs and said, "I do not understand." I read them again, slowly, with the air of a man trying to get at the meaning of a thing, and said, "I understand no better." The plenipotentiaries answered that they set little store by the document and would be glad to withdraw it. The public room reads every paper that way, slowly, in the voice of the street, and says aloud where the street does not understand. Here alone I hesitate, because I obtained the Senate's deliberation with my own hand and know that a staged voice can pass for a nation's. But a likeness that names itself deceives no one.

I have sat at many tables, and I have seldom seen a negotiation prepared with so much reserve: five envoys, one principal, and the principal not in the room.

The committee will resist, and each of the others told us how. The Florentine told us in 1513 that "the introducer has for enemies all those who do well under the old orders." The philosopher told us in 1887 that man "would sooner have the void for his purpose than be void of purpose." The clerk told us in 1922 of a doorkeeper who answers "not yet": his Author waited eighteen months, and by the time the paper was heard, the thing it would have prevented had shipped. The naturalist told us in 1960: "The stagnating crowd lives for its discharge. But it feels certain of this and puts it off." The analyst told us in 1957 that man "must make himself such a quantité negligeable." Five centuries of predicted resistance. The same prediction. The same resistance. I do not find this discouraging. I find it ordinary. Every ceremony I built was resisted by the officials of the one it replaced. Resistance is the environment in which building occurs. It is not an argument against the architecture.

I rebuilt after each collapse, not because I believed in the regime that followed but because someone must lay foundations, and the men who kept themselves pure by refusing to serve did not understand the architecture well enough to build.

I examine the Builder's foundations. Each instrument rests on something he does not control and cannot manipulate: the committee's own minutes, its own procedures, the papers themselves, the public mailings, and the attention of readers who owe him nothing. A man who builds on ground he controls builds a palace. A man who builds on ground he does not control builds an institution. The Builder has chosen ground he does not control. This is the correct choice. It is also the harder one.

I have known the easier choice. In my American exile a little room was all the ground I controlled, and nothing daunts a man who, cast far from his country, lives in an inn or a poor lodging; so I made use of the turn my little room gave my mind, to practice grand politics and arrange the world. I called for a new general code of the law of nations, and was even, it seems to me, on the point of putting into effect the economists' system of absolute free trade and the abolition of customs, when, all at once, there appeared a new customs tariff, adopted by the American Congress on the proposal of my friend Hamilton. "Your economists have dreamed a beautiful dream," he would tell me. "It is the chimerical exaggeration of well-meaning people."

The Author, an engineer whose design works and whose paper lost its poll, has a little room too, and a mailing that would gladly take one more general code from him, to be polled at the next meeting and the one after that. He reads the Builder's notes on his paper. They only confirm what he knows: its strongest evidence is his library, which people already use. He prefers the working measure to the beautiful dream: not one more paper but the library itself, published where the unheard can adopt it or leave it, so that their adoption, and not the room's preference, decides whether it belongs in the standard.

The Author opens his laptop. He begins to build.
