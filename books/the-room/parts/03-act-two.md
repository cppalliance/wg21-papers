# Act Two

## The Genealogy of Process

*After Friedrich Nietzsche, 1887*

---

I write this in the clear air of the Engadine, three hundred and seventy-four years after the Florentine secretary sat at his desk and described the principality. In my last book I wrote that his *Prince* lets one breathe the dry, fine air of Florence; I answer him from a higher valley. He mapped the power. He showed how the prince takes and holds his territory, how the fortress is built and disguised, how the unarmed prophet is destroyed. His analysis was correct. It was correct in 1513. It is correct now. And yet the principality endures. Three hundred and seventy-four years. Only the names changed: the prince became a chair, the court a working group, his favor the consensus of the room. A new name is how an old value hides. The prince still governs by inertia. The fortress is still disguised as furniture. The unarmed prophet is still destroyed.

But the Florentine, my kinsman in the unconditional will to deceive oneself about nothing, did not ask the question I ask. He asked how power operates. I ask where the values came from. Who created the committee's concept of "good"? Who decided that a paper with four revisions is better than a paper with one? Who determined that a co-author from a compiler vendor is a credential and a co-author from a startup is not? And has that "good" hindered or furthered the flourishing of the unnamed, the people who use the language every day from outside the room?

The Florentine would say: the prince created these values. I say: the prince created these values in his own image, and his heirs now take them as given, as factual, beyond all questioning, as if they were the natural order of the world. A value that stands beyond all questioning is a value nobody in the room has ever been permitted to question. I was not gentle in my *Genealogy*, which I called a polemic, and I will not be gentle here. The reader who wants comfort should close this book and read something that confirms what he already believes.

## X. First Essay: "Correct and Incorrect," "Good and Bad"

> "It was out of this pathos of distance that they first took for themselves the right to create values and to coin the names of values: what did utility matter to them!"
>
> -- Friedrich Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morals*

### 1.

Those committee psychologists, who up to the present are the only engineers to be thanked for any endeavor to get as far as a history of the origin of technical governance - these men, I say, offer us in their own personalities no paltry problem. We always find them voluntarily or involuntarily at the same task: pushing to the front the most flattering explanation of their own procedures. My English psychologists, at least, pushed the partie honteuse of our inner world to the front; these push forward the most becoming face of theirs. The committee, they tell us, was built for merit. Papers compete on engineering quality. The room evaluates designs. The best design wins. I grant them the first half of my title: the room does ask whether a design is correct or incorrect, and it asks hard. But their explanation has always been wrong about the second half, and it has always been wrong in the same way: it locates the origin of the committee's concept of "good" in the wrong place.

The judgment "good" does *not* stem from those to whom "goodness" is shown. It did not arise from the users who benefit from a well-designed feature, who find their Monday morning code shorter, who discover that the standard library finally does what they have been doing by hand for a decade. The users did not coin the word "good" and attach it to proposals that served them. They have no vote. They have no seat. They experience the committee's output the way a population experiences legislation: as accomplished facts, arriving in compiler release notes, accompanied by explanations written by people who were in the room.

No. It was "the good" themselves, that is, the noble, the powerful, the higher-placed, and the high-minded, who felt and ranked themselves and their doings as good, as of the first rank. In this institution the noble are the peerage. It was the twenty delegates who between them chair the evolution and study groups, write the papers, and frame the polls who decided what "good" means here, and they decided it in the most natural way imaginable: they defined "good" as what they *are* and what they *do*.

A good paper has co-authors from compiler vendors. A good paper has survived four revisions. A good paper has a reflector thread with supportive analysis from recognized names. A good paper has been pre-socialized in hallway conversations. A good paper has a patron. These are the criteria, and every one of them is a description of the peerage itself. The peerage defined "good" as its own reflection, and then judged all papers against that reflection, and found - what a coincidence - that its own papers were the best.

I have called this the pathos of distance. The standpoint of utility "is as foreign and unsuitable as it could possibly be to such a hot welling-up of the highest value judgments, judgments that establish rank and set rank apart." From a height above the mailing, the peerage took for itself the right to coin the name "good paper," and the name answers to whether a proposal comes from the right kind of person, with the right kind of backing, through the right kind of process. Utility never enters. Nobody notices the absence, because a definition does not list what it leaves out.

### 2.

The Architect is such a creator of values.

He has been in the committee for fifteen years. He chairs no group, but he is chaired by everyone who matters. His paper is on R6. It carries co-authors from two compiler vendors and a platform company. It has survived four working group presentations and the straw poll that closed each one. It has a reflector thread in which recognized names have written "this is the right direction." It has been pre-socialized at dinners and in hallways and on flights between meetings. It is, by every criterion the committee has established, a good paper.

The Architect did not arrive at this position by accident. He arrived at it the way the nobles of my First Essay arrived at theirs: by being strong, by being present, by being connected, and by belonging to the class that sets the measure of merit. In the noble mode of valuation the circle never shows, because the valuer stands at its center and calls what he sees there natural. Of course a paper with co-authors from compiler vendors is better - it has implementation support. Of course a paper that has survived four revisions is better - it has been refined. Of course a paper with a supportive reflector thread is better - it has been vetted. These are reasonable criteria. They are also criteria that only the Architect and people like him can meet, because meeting them requires exactly the resources that the Architect and people like him possess: institutional standing, corporate backing, time, and the network of relationships that converts standing into votes.

In this mode "good" springs from the valuer himself. The class looks at its own papers and sees what is good: polish, institutional weight, revision history, support from names it respects. Then it looks at other papers and measures them against this standard. Papers that look like its own are good. Papers that do not are bad - not evil, not threatening, just bad. *Schlecht*. Low. Common. Let the word testify: *schlecht* is the same word as *schlicht*, plain, and it once meant nothing more than "the plain, the common man," without a suspicious sideways glance, simply in contrast to the noble. The Author's paper is *schlecht*. It is plain. It comes from no one the room recognizes, carries no institutional weight, has no revision history. It is, by the values the Architect's class has created, simply not "good."

Indifference of this kind is far more stable than hostility could ever be. Of noble contempt I wrote: "In fact, too much carelessness is mixed into contempt, too much taking lightly, too much looking away and impatience, even too much of one's own good cheer, for it to be capable of turning its object into a real caricature and monster." The Architect does not hate the Author's paper. He barely notices it. It is scheduled second, in the afternoon, when the room is thin. He was not in the room when it was presented. He heard about it from a colleague who said "someone presented an alternative approach" with the same tone one might use to say "someone brought a different brand of coffee." That tone is the nonchalance of a class that has never needed to take seriously anything that arrives without its markers. A design that is deployed, with users who depend on it, is filed as plain and left there.

### 3.

Now let us look at the other side.

The Author arrives at the committee with a design that is correct and thorough, a library that is deployed, and eighteen months of users who depend on it. He is, in my terms, a man of the knightly-aristocratic type, whose value judgments "presuppose a powerful physicality, a flourishing, rich, even overflowing health, together with what its preservation requires: war, adventure, the hunt, the dance, contests, and everything else that strong, free, cheerful action contains." In his trade the physicality is a running system and the health is a codebase that ships; the hunt is a bug report at midnight, the contest a benchmark, the dance a release. The Author built something. It works. He shipped it. Users depend on it. His virtues are the virtues of the warrior - courage, competence, the willingness to stand behind his own work.

But the committee is not a battlefield. It is a temple. And in the temple, the priestly mode of valuation prevails.

Yet the priests of this temple did not begin as priests. Many of the peers began as knights, and many remain knights at home: they build compilers, they maintain the libraries the Author's own code links against, they ship. The priestly mode of valuation can "branch off from the knightly-aristocratic and then develop into its opposite," and it does so whenever priest and warrior will not agree over the prize. In the room the prize is the standard, and the room hands out no weapons but priestly ones. An engineer who ships every quarter takes his seat at the long table, and for the length of a session his knightly mode is of no use to him; the priestly mode grows out of it the way a shoot grows from the stock, on the same sap, in the other direction.

I said long ago where the branching leads: "Priests are, as everyone knows, the most evil enemies - and why? Because they are the most powerless. Out of powerlessness their hatred grows into something monstrous and uncanny, into the most spiritual and the most poisonous." In the room the powerlessness is literal: no compiler runs at the agenda table, no library ships from a straw poll, and the knight who builds at home sits down here with nothing in his hands but the agenda. What grows from that powerlessness, in place of hatred, is the most spiritual weapon there is, and the subtlest: procedure.

In the room, then, the peerage does not fight with code. Its weapons are procedural: scheduling, framing, poll questions, the quiet accumulation of institutional titles that convert standing into authority. They are priestly weapons: the management of values, the administration of rites, the control of who is clean and who is unclean. A paper that has been through the study group is clean. A paper that arrives without study group history is unclean. A paper with co-authors from the right companies is clean. A paper from an unknown author at a small company is unclean. These distinctions began as something practical - "the 'clean' man is from the beginning merely a man who washes himself, who forbids himself certain foods" - but they have hardened into absolute categories that settle a proposal's fate.

Would anyone like to look into the workshop where these names are made? Who has the courage? Very well. Take a chair at the back of the session room, my dear Mr. Curious and Daring, and wait until your eyes get used to the light of the projector. Now tell me what goes on in there. I am the one listening.

"I see nothing, and I hear all the more. A paper with no study group history is being called premature. A paper that waited a year is being praised for its patience. A fifteen-minute slot at the end of the day is called 'time permitting.' Someone says 'interesting,' and the room moves to the next item."

Go on!

"No one is lying. Every word is sincere, and every one of them was coined in the front rows. Enough! Enough! Bad air! Bad air! This room has no windows."

The Author does not understand this. He thinks he is entering a technical discussion. His paper has already been classified, by criteria he cannot see, as unclean. It lacks the markers of the priestly class. He is a warrior in a temple, and the temple's values are not the warrior's values. His strength - the raw, creative strength of a man who built something and shipped it - is the one thing the temple has no rite for, because the temple's power depends on such strength finding no outlet inside its walls.

### 4.

But there is another moral system at work, and it operates outside the temple entirely.

Nothing in the rules forbids the unnamed to write a paper or to come to a meeting. The room belongs to those who can afford to come back three times a year, every year. Most of the unnamed never come at all. Most have never heard of their national body.

What they lack is the deed. The language that governs their working day is settled in a room where they hold no seat, so the one reaction that would count - to stand up in the session and show what the feature does to their code - is denied them.

And so their reaction comes late, and it comes from outside: a forum thread, a blog post, a quiet migration to something else.

I wrote a formula for another revolt, the revolt of beings "to whom the true reaction, that of the deed, is denied, and who can compensate themselves only with an imaginary revenge."

The Blogger writes a post titled "Three Days and a Workaround." She has never attended a committee meeting. She does not know what an NB comment is. She tried to use the Architect's feature in her codebase and it did not work for her use case. Her post is exact. She shows the code, shows the error, shows the workaround. Nothing in it is imaginary. Nobody outside the room comes nearer to saying, with evidence, what the feature costs. If anything here is imaginary, it is the room's belief that she had a channel and chose not to use it.

Her commenters create a value from the post - "works in my code" set against "has institutional backing." They are saying: "The committee called this good. We, who actually use the language, call it bad. And if the committee's good is our bad, then perhaps the committee's entire concept of good is wrong."

I named this, when I first saw it, the reversal of the value-positing glance: the glance that must look outward, at the other, before it can find a good of its own. Every word the room is proud of comes back from those commenters, turned inside out. The room says "mature." They read "bloated." The room says "well-backed." They read "captured." The room says "it has survived four working group polls." They read "the committee rubber-stamps its own preferences." From the inverted words they derive a new, opposite good: a feature is good if it works for ordinary developers on Monday morning, regardless of how many revisions it went through or who co-authored it.

I will say what I said in my own century, though it pleases neither side. The reversed glance is no truer than the glance it reverses. It needs the room in order to see at all. The committee says "we are good" and sets everything else down as merely plain. Under the Blogger's post, the commenters say "the committee is evil" and call good whatever stands against it. Each side holds its "good" at arm's length from the other, and neither puts it to the question I set in my preface: "the value of these values must itself, for once, be called into question." What do they cost? What do they produce? What kind of life do they enable? And when the room's "good" lifts a design into the standard, is the design worth what the unnamed will pay for it?

### 5.

The Author stands between the two moral systems, and this is what makes him interesting.

He arrived at the committee with the warrior's values: build, ship, deploy, test, demonstrate. These are positive values. They spring from action, not reaction. They are the values of a man who can say "I am good" without needing an enemy to define himself against. But the committee's priestly valuation has no column for them. The committee counts procedure. And the reactive valuation of those commenters has no use for him either. It needs enemies, not allies, and the Author gives it nothing to push against.

The Author's paper is correct, and the room will check that. It will also ask whether it wants the design, and it will count hands. What neither the room nor the commenters can count is his real credential: eighteen months under the weight of users who depend on his library. That is the warrior's value - the value of a man who tested his design against reality and found that it held. It is the oldest and most dangerous value, because it answers to no institution and no crowd. It is answerable only to the thing itself.

When the Author loses the first poll, nobody has found an error in his design. He loses because his paper is unclean. When the Blogger writes her post, it is about her own code, and the Author's paper appears in it once, in passing, favorably. The forums pick it up and throw it at the Architect's paper, the way a man throws a rock at a wall. The rock does not benefit from the transaction.

I know this position. I occupied it myself: no knight, no priest, and no partisan of the reaction against either. I was the genealogist, the one who stands outside both valuations and asks where the values came from. That night the Author writes out the marks by which the room knows a good paper, and against each mark the name of whoever first said it. He is asking: where did these values come from? Who created them? Whom do they serve? And why does the institution that claims to serve the unnamed operate on values that have nothing to do with serving them?

He does not yet have answers. But I recognize the first motion of my own work in those names: a calm count of what the values are worth, which is a different act from turning them upside down.

The lambs bear a grudge against the birds of prey. But the Author is not a lamb. He is something rarer: a bird of prey who has been told that flying is against the rules, and who must now decide whether to walk, or to find a sky the temple does not govern.

## XI. Second Essay: "Process," "Bad Conscience," and the Like

> "To breed an animal that is entitled to promise - is that not the very paradoxical task that nature has set itself with regard to man?"
>
> -- Friedrich Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morals*

### 1.

To breed a delegate who is entitled to promise - is that not the very paradoxical task that the committee has set itself with regard to its members?

The committee is a promise-making machine. Every paper submitted is a promise: I will address your concerns. Every revision is a promise: I will come back. Every poll is a promise: I will abide by the result. Every consensus determination is a promise: we will not revisit what has been decided. The entire structure of the committee - the three-year standard cycle, the three meetings per year, the revision numbers climbing from R0 to R6 to R12 - is an architecture of promises, a vast apparatus for ensuring that commitments made in one meeting are honored in the next, and the next, and the next.

Mark the word: *entitled*. Any engineer can promise. The right to have his promise counted is the room's to grant, and the room grants it slowly, to those who have shown that they will come back, revise, and abide. So the question the room puts to a new author is whether he *may* promise.

But how do you breed a creature that can keep promises of this kind? How do you take an engineer - a wild, creative, building animal, accustomed to shipping code and moving on, to solving problems and forgetting them, to the blessed forgetfulness of the productive mind - and transform him into a delegate who will return to the same room three times a year for six years to revise the same paper?

You do it with pain.

The oldest psychology on earth runs on one axiom: "Something is burned in so that it stays in the memory: only what does not stop hurting stays in the memory." No one is branded or flogged, but the committee's mnemonics are real. The first poll defeat. The first questioner who frames your paper as the challenger. The first time you hear "this paper needs more study group time" and understand that it means your proposal will not be discussed today, or this week, or this cycle. The first time you sit in a room for six hours watching other papers advance while yours waits in a queue that never reaches it. These are the committee's instruments of memory. They burn. They are meant to burn.

What does he remember? He remembers the rules. Not the written rules - anyone can read SD-4 - but the unwritten ones. He remembers that a paper without co-authors is vulnerable. He remembers that a paper without pre-socialization faces a hostile room. He remembers that the first questioner sets the frame, and that the frame determines the outcome, and that the frame is set by people who have been in the room longer than he has. He remembers that the Chair's summary before the vote is the last thing the uncommitted middle hears, and that the summary is not neutral, and that arguing about the summary is worse than accepting it. He remembers these things because they hurt, and because they hurt they stay, and because they stay he can promise. He can promise to come back. He can promise to revise. He can promise to address concerns. He can promise, in short, to submit.

### 2.

The Author arrives at the committee as a creature who is not yet entitled to promise.

He can build. He can ship. He can deploy. But he cannot promise in the way the committee requires, because the promises the committee counts are promises to endure. The committee does not want the Author's code. It wants his patience. It wants him to sit in rooms and listen to objections and revise his paper and sit in rooms again and listen to more objections and revise again. It wants him to transform himself from a builder into a supplicant - from a man who creates into a man who petitions.

This transformation is old work, older than any committee: "with the help of the morality of custom and the social strait-waistcoat, man was actually made calculable."

The committee's morality of custom is the revision cycle. R0, R1, R2, R3 - each revision is the social strait-waistcoat tightened one notch further. At R0, the Author is free. His paper says what he wants it to say, in the form he wants to say it. At R1, he has addressed concerns from the first presentation. He has not changed his design, but he has added paragraphs explaining why the concerns are unfounded, or how they are addressed, or why the tradeoff is acceptable. At R2, he has added a co-author - someone the room recognizes, someone whose name on the paper signals that the paper is not an orphan. The word asks after lineage, as the old nobility did, and never after use. At R3, he has pre-socialized. He has had the hallway conversations. He has emailed the delegates who voted against him. He has asked "what would it take?" and he has done what they said it would take.

Let the word testify. "Revision" is *revidere*, to see again. At each revision the room sees the paper again, and the Author learns to see himself as the room sees him. He becomes more calculable. His paper becomes more predictable. His behavior in the room becomes more regular. He learns to present in the style the committee expects - confident but deferential, technical but brief, acknowledging concerns before they are raised. He learns the rhythm of the poll: the Chair's framing, the first questioner's tone, the cascade of hands. He learns to read the room the way the Chair reads it. All this labor ripens, very late, into a single fruit: "to be entitled to say yes to oneself." The R3 delegate says yes to himself already: the ripe fruit on an unripe branch. The self he says yes to is no longer the wild, building animal who submitted R0, but the domesticated, calculable, promise-keeping delegate who submitted R3.

This is what I meant by "the sovereign individual, who resembles only himself, who has broken free again from the morality of custom, the autonomous, supra-moral individual." But I was describing the end of the process. The Author is still far from it, still inside the morality of custom, still being squeezed into the social strait-waistcoat, still being made calculable. The sovereign individual - the man who is entitled to promise because he is strong enough to keep his word even against fate - emerges only after the morality of custom has done its work, carrying the scars of the process but no longer governed by them.

But most do not emerge. Most are consumed by the process. Most become the process.

### 3.

The Newcomer does not stay.

She was a young engineer from a small company, with a paper that solved a real problem. The room liked it so well that it asked to see the idea folded into an established paper whose authors it knew. The next revision of that paper took in her idea, and her name went into its acknowledgments. Her own paper was never called again. This is the lordly right to give names: the masters say what a thing is, seal it with a sound, and so take possession of it. The room said the idea belonged to the established paper, and from that moment it did. She read her name there once, and by the time she closed the file she had begun to forget the room. She did not come back.

I recognize her at once, and not as a casualty. She is the animal that could not be made to promise - not because she lacked the capacity, but because the cost of the promise was too high and the reward too uncertain. The committee's morality of custom offered her an exchange - years of revising and coming back, for the possibility that one day a paper would bear her name at its head instead of at its foot. The exchange rate is brutal. The Newcomer looked at it and decided that the price was more than the thing was worth.

I do not sentimentalize this. I do not say she was wronged, though she was. The morality of custom serves a function: it produces the kind of delegate the committee needs. The committee needs delegates who will come back. It needs delegates who will revise. It needs delegates who will endure the process without breaking. The Author keeps his wound open and returns with it. She did the arithmetic and went home. The morality of custom selects for the Author and against the Newcomer, not because the Author is better but because he is harder. With her it loses whatever she would have built next.

The custom is honest about this. It does not pretend to be fair. It claims to value the process, not the Newcomer's paper, and the process requires endurance, and she did not stay to endure it. What remains of her is gray, the color a hundred times more important to a genealogist than blue: a mailing entry with her name on it. Her paper lies in the archive beside many others from every mailing that met the same quiet end. The archive is what I have called "the whole long, hard-to-decipher hieroglyphic script of humanity's moral past," a record of everything the process consumed in order to produce the few things it retained.

### 4.

Now let us examine what happens to the Author - the animal that survives.

He revises. He finds a co-author. He pre-socializes. He addresses concerns. He learns the system. He comes back. He comes back again. He loses a second poll, by a narrower margin. He revises again. He comes back a third time. By now he has been in the committee for eighteen months. He has attended four meetings. He has spent twenty-four days in windowless rooms. He has read several hundred papers, most of them not related to his proposal, because reading them is part of being a good delegate. He has had dozens of hallway conversations. He has eaten two dozen hotel dinners. He has missed weekends and holidays and his daughter's piano recital.

Look closely at that second loss, because in this room it is a graduation. Between the first poll and the second, nothing new about his users came before the room: the same deployment, the same users, the same numbers. What changed was the Author. The margin narrowed because he had become calculable, and the room rewarded the calculability. The loss proves that the memory has taken. It is what I called a memory of the will: a willing on and on of what was once willed, so that between R0's "I will" and the paper's eventual act a whole world of new and strange things may be laid (a co-author, four meetings, a defeat) without the long chain of the will snapping.

And something else has changed: the man's language. In the hallway he no longer says "my design." He says "the proposal." He no longer calls the process an obstacle. He calls it "how things work." He no longer complains about the Chair's scheduling. He explains it to newer delegates. He no longer bristles at the first questioner's framing. He prepares for it. He has become an animal entitled to promise - and the price of the entitlement is that the instinct which made him want to build something in the first place has nowhere left to go.

This is the bad conscience.

I described its origin for the whole species: "Just as it must have gone with the water animals when they were forced either to become land animals or to perish, so it went with these half-animals, happily adapted to wilderness, war, roaming, and adventure - all at once all their instincts were devalued and 'unhooked.' From now on they were to walk on their feet and 'carry themselves,' where until then the water had carried them."

The Author was a water animal. He built things. He shipped them. He solved problems by building solutions, and the water of his own codebase carried him. Now he must walk on his feet. He must solve problems by writing papers, by attending meetings, by pre-socializing, by playing the game. The building instinct - the instinct to solve a problem by making something that works - is still alive in him. The committee asks him to argue, to present, to revise, to sit in a room and wait for his turn and raise his hand when the Chair calls the poll. It has no session in which he could build.

"All instincts that do not discharge themselves outward turn inward - this is what I call the internalization of man: only with it does there grow in man what is later called his 'soul.'"

So the room grows a soul in him. Not the poet's soul - a committee soul, furnished like the room itself: a long table, an agenda, a queue.

At the far end of this road lies "man's will to find himself guilty and reprehensible to the point where no atonement is possible." Not guilty of a crime. Guilty of being the wrong kind of engineer - the kind who builds things instead of writing papers, who ships code instead of pre-socializing, who solves problems instead of navigating process. The turned instinct speaks in a single refrain: his original approach - submit a correct paper, point to the users who already depend on it, and let the work speak for itself - was naive. Was wrong. Was, in the room's valuation, bad. He should have found co-authors first. He should have pre-socialized. He should have known the study group structure. He should have talked to the Chair before the meeting. He should have, he should have, he should have. Each "should have" is a wound turned inward, a building instinct redirected against the builder.

### 5.

The genealogists of morals before me never dreamed, even remotely, that the cardinal moral concept *Schuld*, guilt, took its origin from the very material concept *Schulden*, debts. I was the first to see it. The Author owes the committee. He owes it for the opportunity to present. He owes it for the time the room spent listening to his paper. He owes it for the feedback he received, even when the feedback was dismissive, even when the feedback was a single "interesting" and a glance at the clock. He owes the delegates who voted against him, because they spent a fraction of their attention on his proposal when they could have spent it on something else. He owes the Chair, who scheduled his paper when the Chair could have deferred it.

This debt is real. It is also unpayable. No matter how many revisions the Author produces, no matter how many concerns he addresses, no matter how many meetings he attends, he will never have paid enough to earn the committee's trust. The revision cycle is not a path to completion. It is a debt structure with no discharge.

I described the oldest contract this way: "The debtor ... pledges to the creditor by contract, in case he does not pay, something that he otherwise still 'possesses,' something over which he still has power."

What does the Author pledge? His time. His weekends. His holidays. His creative energy. His capacity to build things that are not committee papers. The committee never asks for any of this in writing; the debt structure requires it all the same. To come back is to pay. To revise is to pay. To pre-socialize is to pay. Each payment demonstrates his seriousness, his commitment, his worthiness of the committee's eventual trust. And each payment, once made, creates the expectation of the next payment, and the next, until the Author cannot remember a time when he was not paying.

The creditor - the committee - grows more powerful with each payment, and a creditor who grows powerful grows mild. At last the richest creditor can afford the noblest luxury there is, to let the one who cannot pay go free, and that luxury goes by a beautiful name, mercy, the privilege of the most powerful. The committee is rich enough for mercy, and it never grants it. Nor does it punish. Instead of rejecting the Author's paper outright, it defers. It asks for revisions. It suggests study group time. It invites him to come back. Each of these looks like leniency, and none of them is a pardon. The account stays open, and nothing is forgiven, because nothing is ever closed.

I called it the creditor's pleasure: "the pleasure of being permitted to vent his power, without a second thought, on someone powerless." In the committee the pleasure is structural. The committee derives satisfaction from the Author's willingness to revise, to return, to pay, because that compliance validates the system. Each revision is evidence that the system works. Each returning author is proof that the process produces results. The committee wants him to succeed - slowly, painfully, at great cost - because his success, achieved through the process, justifies the process. His suffering is the process's proof of its own seriousness.

Read the contract once more and look for the signatures. The creditor is the room. The debtor is the Author. The unnamed, to whom the design is supposed to answer, are not party to it at all.

## XII. Third Essay: What Does the Ascetic Ideal of Process Mean?

> "Man would sooner have the void for his purpose than be void of purpose."
>
> -- Friedrich Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morals*

### 1.

What does it mean when a committee worships its own procedure?

The committee that writes the specification for a programming language used by the unnamed devotes much of its institutional energy to maintaining the process by which designs are judged. Study group chairs manage agendas. Working group chairs frame polls. The direction group recommends priorities. The convener appoints chairs. The mailing publishes papers. The reflector hosts discussion. Each of these is a procedural function, and each is performed with a seriousness, a solemnity, a devotion that in any other context would be called religious. After the designs are chosen comes another kind of work: the plenary votes reviewed wording into the working draft, the national bodies read that wording and file their comments, and the ballot confirms. That reading checks what the room has already decided.

I devoted my entire Third Essay to the question of what ascetic ideals mean. To the artist they mean nothing at all, or so many things that it comes to the same; to the philosopher, the best conditions for his boldest thinking. For priests they are something else entirely: "the true priestly faith, their best instrument of power, and also the 'very highest' license to power."

The Chair is the ascetic priest of the committee.

I mean the office. From the chair he administers the process. He schedules. He frames. He summarizes. He is organized, prepared, courteous, and fair. The office's power derives not from the warrior's ability to create but from the management of the values by which creation is judged. The priest does not hunt. The priest blesses the hunt. The priest does not fight. The priest consecrates the war. The priest does not build. The priest determines whether what has been built is clean or unclean.

### 2.

The ascetic ideal of process means, for the Chair, a great many things. It means that there is always more process to be done. It means that no proposal is ever ready, because the process can always find another concern to raise, another study group to consult, another revision to request. It means that the process is self-justifying. It means that anyone who questions the process is questioning the institution, and anyone who questions the institution is questioning the people who built it, and questioning the people who built it is an act of ingratitude that the institution will remember.

The Chair administers suffering. He needs no cruelty for it. The ascetic ideal requires suffering. The revision cycle is suffering. The scheduling queue is suffering. The poll defeat is suffering. The reflector silence is suffering. Each of these is a form of ascetic discipline imposed on the Author, and each is justified by the ascetic ideal: the suffering proves that the process is serious, and the seriousness of the process is the source of the Chair's authority.

I wrote of the oldest priesthoods: "From the beginning there is something unhealthy in such priestly aristocracies and in the habits that rule there, habits turned away from action, partly brooding, partly emotionally explosive."

The committee shows this unhealthiness in its habits. They are turned away from action: a proposal can spend years in the revision cycle without reaching the working draft. They brood: in 2001 a committee paper said that criteria for evaluating proposals would need to be developed, and twenty-one years later the direction group was still encouraging a discussion of them. And they explode in the rare moments when the process is challenged from outside: when an outsider asks why a feature that has been in development for a decade has not shipped, when a blogger posts her workaround, when a national body reviewer, reading the wording with care, finds a tradeoff that was never disclosed. These moments produce convulsions of defensive rhetoric from the priestly class - rhetoric that frames the challenge as ignorance, as ingratitude, as a failure to understand the difficulty of the work.

Watch what becomes of the Blogger's post when it reaches the room. She misused the feature. She misunderstands the constraints. She was not there when the tradeoffs were discussed. Someone is to blame for the code that did not work, and the someone is she. I put the value of priestly existence into the shortest formula: "the priest is the *changer of direction* of ressentiment." Her post held no ressentiment, only evidence, and its direction was changed all the same.

### 3.

But the ascetic ideal serves another function too, and this is the function I consider most important. The ascetic ideal provides meaning.

Why does the Author come back? Not because the process is pleasant. Not because the revision cycle is efficient. Not because the polls are fair. He comes back because the process gives his work a meaning it would not otherwise have. A library that is deployed at his company serves five hundred engineers. A feature that is adopted into the international standard serves the unnamed many. The standard is the meaning. The process is the path to the meaning. And the path, however painful, however irrational, however structured to favor the incumbent and disadvantage the challenger, is the only path there is.

And where the standard is the meaning, being in the standard is good in itself, as truth was good in itself for us knowers. I wrote of that faith that "truth was not *permitted* to be a problem at all. Does one understand this 'permitted'?" Not in the least, sir? Then let me show it to you in the room.

It is the last afternoon of the Author's third meeting. The questions have been asked: is the design correct, does it compose with the rest of the library, can it be implemented, does the room prefer it to the other. Then someone at the long table calls the other design more "mature," the word goes around the room, and the Chair reads the poll. The hands go up, are counted, come down: twenty-five in the room, fourteen for the other design, eight for his, three that never rise.

The word is the Latin *maturus*: ripe. Ripe for what? It does not say, and in this room it need not, for there is only one harvest. So "mature" stands where the question would stand. Is this worth what standardizing it will cost the unnamed? No rule forbids the question, and none is needed. The value of this value is simply not *permitted* to be a problem.

The sentence set above this essay should frighten anyone who thinks about institutions: "Man would sooner have the void for his purpose than be void of purpose." The committee's process is not efficient. It is not fair. It does not reliably produce the best designs. But it produces something: a standard, an ISO number, a specification that compilers implement and developers adopt. This something - however flawed, however encumbered by the tradeoffs the process did not evaluate - is better than nothing. It is better than the void. And so the Author endures the process, and the Chair administers the process, and the peerage maintains the process.

The ascetic ideal of process is "the 'faute de mieux' par excellence." The committee is not the best way to standardize this language. It is the only way anyone has found. And because it is the only way, its flaws are treated not as flaws but as features. The length of the revision cycle becomes "thoroughness." The opacity of the scheduling becomes "efficiency." The dominance of the peerage becomes "experience." Let the words confess their origin. Each was coined by those who would otherwise have to answer for the flaw. The renaming is sincere, because the alternative - acknowledging that the process is structurally biased toward the powerful and against the proven - would destroy the meaning that justifies the suffering.

### 4.

Now we arrive at the cruelest question in my *Genealogy*, and the one that applies to the committee with the most uncomfortable precision.

I asked it where the ascetic ideal seemed to have closed every exit: "Where is the counterpart to this closed system of will, goal, and interpretation? Why is the counterpart missing? ... Where is the other 'one goal'?" The answer was plain: the ideal ruled because it was the only ideal so far, because it had no competitor.

The Author has no counterpart to hold up against it. He cannot create a standard on his own. He cannot convene a committee. He cannot issue an ISO number. Some authors build a library, publish it, and let adoption create pressure, and some of them succeed; theirs is the one counterpart in view, the one other goal with a will of its own. The Author has already built his library, and his users already depend on it, and still he queues for the poll.

The Chair also has no counterpart. He cannot administer a process that does not exist. He cannot exercise the office outside the temple. His authority depends entirely on the ascetic ideal - on the belief, shared by delegates and authors and national body representatives, that the process is necessary, that the process is serious, that the process produces good outcomes. If that belief collapsed, the office's authority would collapse with it: a priesthood without a congregation, robes on a hook beside an empty altar.

And the public has no counterpart either, or believes it has none. The unnamed developers who write the language use the standard because the compilers implement the standard, and the compilers implement the standard because the committee produces the standard. The chain is long and each link is weak, but the chain exists and no one has built another. The public can complain, but it cannot produce a standard of its own. It is dependent on the committee the way a population is dependent on a government it did not elect.

### 5.

I ended my *Genealogy* without telling my readers how to create new values. I told them that they must, and I named what stands in the way, and beneath it the fundamental fact of the human will, its horror vacui: it would sooner have the void for its purpose than be void of purpose.

The committee's ascetic ideal of process is this void-avoidance made institutional. The process exists because the alternative is intolerable: no process, no standard, no institution, no meaning. The process persists because the people inside it have no language for what is wrong with it, and the people outside it have no lever to change it. The process produces bad outcomes - features that do not serve users, tradeoffs that are not evaluated, correction papers that accumulate in the archive - and these bad outcomes are absorbed into the process as evidence of its own seriousness. Even the failures prove that the process is necessary. *Especially* the failures. Because if the process, with all its thoroughness, with all its revisions, with all its polls and ballots and national body comments, still produces features that need correction papers, imagine how much worse things would be without the process!

This is the ascetic ideal at its most impregnable. It cannot be defeated by pointing to its failures, because its failures are its proof. It cannot be defeated by offering a better process, because any better process would have to be evaluated by the existing process, and the existing process will find the better process insufficient. It can only be defeated by what I have called self-overcoming: the moment the ideal turns against itself and asks the question it was never *permitted* to ask, whether the suffering it demands is producing the life it claims to serve.

All great things perish through themselves, and in the end the call goes out to the lawgiver himself: patere legem, quam ipse tulisti, submit to the law you yourself have made. The room's law is evidence. Of every paper it demands proof that the design is correct, an answer to every concern, a revision for every objection. The process overcomes itself on the day that law is put to the lawgiver: when the evidence the room demands of every paper is demanded of the process itself and of each feature it ships.

The Author, reading the correction papers as hieroglyphs, begins to put the room's law back to the room. He has been made calculable. He has carried the bad conscience. He has kept up his payments. And now he is asking: what did the process produce? Was it worth the cost? Who paid it? He asks it of the process, and of the Architect's feature. He does not yet ask it of his own.

His paper is still in the queue, at a revision number the room respects. The Architect's paper is in the standard. It works worse than the Author's library, and the unnamed pay the difference. The Author would have the process honest - honest about what it values, honest about whom it serves, honest about being a meritocracy in its promises and a priesthood in its practice. An honest temple is still a temple. He wants it cleaned so that the next design, his next design, can win in it.

The last sentence of my *Genealogy* could stand as the epigraph for the committee's entire history: "man would sooner have the void for his purpose than be void of purpose." The committee would sooner ship a flawed standard than ship nothing at all. The Author would sooner endure the process than abandon the standard. The public would sooner go on using the standard it is handed than go without one. Each of these is a choice to accept the ascetic ideal rather than face the void.

The Author opens his laptop. R0 again, a new number, the same long chain of the will, and it does not snap. He is not free of the ascetic ideal. No one is. But if the will to standardize is ever to become conscious of itself as a problem, it will happen first in us, my unknown friends - and that consciousness is the last thing the ascetic priest wants his congregation to have.

I died in 1900, yet the temple I exposed persists.
