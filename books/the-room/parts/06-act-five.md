# Act Five

<!-- body -->

The Newcomer flies home on Wednesday evening, halfway through her first meeting.

She does not know why she feels the way she feels. She presented her paper. Six people attended. One asked a question. There was no poll. The chair was polite. No one was unkind. Nothing happened. And yet something happened, and it happened inside her, in a place she cannot name, and it will take weeks to reach the surface. In the bag beneath the seat in front of her, the printed copy of her paper sits in the same fold as the morning she printed it. Nobody asked to see it.

Through the window beside her, the moon is pale in the evening sky. The committee cannot poll it.

On the flight home she falls asleep, and she dreams.

She dreams she is standing in a large room - not the conference room but something older, something with stone walls and high windows through which no light enters. The air does not move.

She is holding a sheaf of papers. She is looking for a door.

There are many doors, and behind each one she can hear voices - many voices, speaking with authority, making decisions. She opens a door. The room behind it is full. Every seat is taken. No one looks at her. She opens another door. Same. Full. No one turns.

She opens a third door and finds a room with six empty chairs and a man sweeping the floor. He looks up and says: "Your room is ready."

She sits down. She waits. No one comes.

She wakes over the Atlantic with the dream still vivid - the stone walls, the closed doors, the voices she could hear but could not reach, the man with the broom, the empty chairs. She writes it down on the back of a boarding pass, because someone once told her that dreams should be written down. She does not know what it means. She puts the boarding pass in her bag and does not look at it again.

---

I would tell her what it means, if she came to me.

I write this from Küsnacht, beside the lake, in the eighty-third year of a life spent listening to what people cannot say. I have spent nearly sixty years in the consulting room. What I have learned there is this: institutions do not merely constrain the people who inhabit them. They colonize them. They replace the individual's own psychology with a collective substitute so gradually that the individual cannot locate the moment of surrender. He enters the institution as himself. He leaves it as the institution's version of himself.

A dream supplies what the day left out. The waking mind takes one side of a matter, as it must, and in sleep the neglected side comes up in images, so that a balance is kept which the dreamer did not know was needed. An institution has no sleep of its own. Its neglected side comes up in one of its members, on a night flight over the Atlantic, in a dream written on the back of a boarding pass. The unconscious is faster than the ego. It always is.

The Florentine mapped the power. The philosopher traced the values to their source. The clerk told his Author's story without a word of comment. The naturalist stood at a distance and watched the hands rise together, at the very meeting the Newcomer is flying home from. Four voices across four centuries, each describing the persona the committee wore in his own time. Each was correct. Each was incomplete. None of them entered the consulting room.

## The Undiscovered Committee

*After Carl Gustav Jung, 1957*

The dream is not complicated. There are no fixed symbolic meanings, and every symbol has more than one. A dream read off like a code tells the reader only what he brought to it. I have only her week to set beside the images, and her week is enough. On Sunday she shared a taxi from the airport with the Author, who said her paper sounded good. On Monday morning she spoke in Room B, a room designed for forty, to six people, while every delegate who cared about her domain was in Room A, where two designs were presented back to back. On Tuesday evening, in the hotel bar, she overheard a sentence. On Wednesday evening she flew home. Against that week the dream sets rooms so full that no one turns, one room swept and made ready, and a man with a broom who tells her, without cruelty and without comfort, "Your room is ready."

The empty chairs are the six who came to hear her, present and courteous, and the dream, which owes nothing to courtesy, empties their chairs. They are also the chairs of the people who were in no room that week: the engineers who will use whatever the committee forwards. The committee keeps no seats for them. The man with the broom is the only one in the dream who looks at her, and whether he is sweeping up after a meeting or before one the dream does not say. Of her week, both are true.

She was not rejected. She was something worse than rejected. She was accommodated. Rejection at least acknowledges that the rejected thing existed. The institution gave her everything she asked for - a paper number, a time slot, a room, a chair - and it gave her nothing that mattered. She was processed. She was not heard.

The empty chair is the committee's most honest symbol. It says what the published procedures cannot say: we have room for you, and the room is empty.

## XXXIII. The Two Personalities of the Committee

> "Somewhere deep in the background I always knew that I was two persons."
>
> -- Carl Gustav Jung, *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*

I knew it as a schoolboy, long before I had words for it. One of the two was the son of my parents, who went to school and was a rather ordinary pupil, and him I called No. 1. The other, No. 2, was old, and belonged to the night and the weather and the moon, and above all to dreams. Knowing it was one thing. I understood it only after the break with Freud, when I was nearly forty and went down alone into my confrontation with the unconscious. The committee, too, has two personalities. It knows only one of them.

Its No. 1 runs the week. Like mine, it is the son of its parents, and its parents are its rules and its calendar. It publishes its procedures, sets the schedule, frames the polls, counts the hands, and forwards the paper, and in conference keynotes it describes itself as a technical meritocracy where proposals compete on engineering merit and the best design wins. It is sincere, as my No. 1 was sincere.

Its No. 2 is older than any of its members and has no seat. It belongs to the moon, as mine did, and to the people who write code in the language every day and never come to a meeting. It remembers what the language is for. It has a question of its own, and the question has no slot on the agenda, since the agenda belongs to No. 1.

No. 1 and No. 2 have played against each other in me, as they do in every individual. The trouble comes when No. 1 leaves No. 2 no room. All my life I have tried to give a hearing to whatever came up from within. The committee has given every hour of its week to No. 1, and its rooms are full.

The Delegate sits in the plenary on Saturday morning. The Newcomer has been gone since Wednesday evening. On Monday he was in Room A, and his was one of the twenty-eight hands for the Architect's design. The motions come one after another, each asking the body to apply reviewed wording to the working draft, and most pass by unanimous consent, with hands raised only when someone objects. He has voted in thirty polls this week, most of them earlier, in the working groups, where the designs were chosen, and he has read the papers for three. For the other twenty-seven he had what the room gave him: the Chair's framing, the first question from the floor, the confidence of the presenter, the faces in the front row whose judgment he has learned to trust. He calls it pragmatism. He cannot read a mailing of more than a hundred papers. No one can. At his company he makes independent technical decisions every day. Here he trusts the people he trusts. His hand rises when theirs rise, and when it rises it feels like his own.

I borrowed a name for this from Lévy-Bruhl, a philosopher in Paris: participation mystique. He gave the term up in later years, when his critics pressed him. They were wrong, and I kept it. It is the state to which we all return the moment the effort of individual judgment becomes too expensive. This year I wrote: "It is astounding that man, the instigator, inventor and vehicle of all these developments, the originator of all judgements and decisions and the planner of the future, must make himself such a quantité negligeable."

The Delegate is bound to the room by his standing in it, by the colleagues he has sat beside for years, and by the process he trusts, and the hand that rises is partly his and partly the room's. The room's decisions pass through him like water through a channel, meeting no resistance, because repetition has worn the channel smooth. A hand so bound can report only whether the room wants a design.

Go back to Monday morning, to Room A, at the hour the Newcomer was speaking to six people in Room B. Fifty delegates of the library evolution group sit in rows. The Architect's design and the Author's have been presented back to back. All morning the questions have swung between right and wrong: whether each design is correct, how each interacts with what the standard already has, which of the two the room prefers. They are good questions. The first question put to the Author was "Who else has reviewed this?" The Chair asks whether there are further questions before the poll. There is a pause of the ordinary length, and in that pause one question is present in the room that would settle more than either design: whether either needs to be in the standard at all. Nobody asks it. The Chair reads the poll, the hands rise, and the Architect's design, the incumbent, wins.

The question goes unasked because a "no" would reach back through every year the committee's members have given to this feature and to the standard. That is its shadow, the question it cannot put to itself, which belongs to no one in the room and so is seen by no one in it. The committee believes it evaluates designs. It actually evaluates persons. The contradiction is not hypocrisy, for the left hand does not know what the right hand is doing. The committee can weigh only the persons in the room. The users, who are in no room, weigh nothing.

On Tuesday evening two delegates from Room A are having dinner in the hotel bar, at the table next to the Newcomer's. "Another paper from nobody," one of them says, and they laugh, and that settles the matter. Neither has read the paper. The Newcomer hears them, does not ask whether they mean her, and goes upstairs to book the next evening's flight. I know that sentence from the inside. Of the years after the break with Freud I have said: "My book was declared to be rubbish; I was a mystic, and that settled the matter."

The accusation is a confession. To call the paper's author nobody is to name the one thing the room has weighed. The institution is telling you what it cannot tell itself.

## XXXIV. The Cost

> "The decisive question for man is: Is he related to something infinite or not?"
>
> -- Carl Gustav Jung, *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*

She will not come back. One more meeting, six months from now, is in her company's budget, and she will spend it at the back of a room, presenting nothing, watching the proceedings the way a traveler watches a city she has already decided to leave, noticing everything, attached to nothing. Then she will stop coming. She will experience the stopping not as a decision but as a loss of interest, a turning away, a feeling that perhaps the committee is not for her, that perhaps her contributions are better spent elsewhere. Her colleagues will take it so, and the committee, if it notices, will take it so too. I do not. A collective problem that no one names always passes for a personal one.

The cost is the institution's. This is what the institution does to the people it cannot use. It does not destroy them. It does not oppose them. It empties them.

She will not write her trip report. She will not submit to the next mailing. She will go back to her company and her codebase, and to the problem her paper would have solved for tens of thousands of engineers, who will go on solving it by hand. When, after that last meeting, a colleague asks whether she is going to the next one, she will say she is busy. She is not busy. She is empty.

The collective offers belonging. The individual offers judgment. A group gathered in one feeling stands below the level of the individuals in it. On Saturday the Delegate goes home. He has participated. He has not judged. The room keeps no place for the will that would have said "I have not read this paper and therefore I will not vote." That will is surrendered on the way in, along with his coat, and no one misses it, and the room is warm, and the Chair is fair, and the process is orderly, and the consensus is reached, and the feature ships, and the correction papers follow, and the users discover what the room never weighed, and no one is to blame, because no one decided. The room decided, which comes to the same thing.

The committee is finite. Its papers are finite. Its polls are finite. Its features ship, are corrected, are corrected again, and at last, in some future revision, are deprecated with a note that says what the Author's paper said fifteen years before. It translates a proposal from the infinite of what someone saw into the finite of specification text, and every translation loses something. What the committee's translation loses is the one thing no group can carry for its members: the specific, unrepeatable, non-transferable act of seeing clearly and saying what you see.

The Newcomer saw clearly. She said what she saw, to six people. The institution could not hear her, because the institution does not hear individuals. Her voice was absorbed by the empty room the way a sound is absorbed by stone. It did not echo. It did not persist. It simply stopped.

I would have told her, if she had come to me, that the stopping is not the end. That the psyche does not lose what consciousness discards. What she understood flies home with her above the clouds, yet goes underground, into the unconscious, where it will work on her in ways she cannot predict and the institution cannot prevent. It will come up again in her code, in the library she builds without the committee's permission, and in a blog post, three years from now, where an engineer who meets the same problem names it, in plain language, to a larger audience. That audience is the undiscovered, the millions who write code in the language every day, who do not know what a national body is and will never raise a hand in a straw poll, and they will hear it because they are not a committee. Some Tuesday morning the institution will read the blog post and discover, as it always discovers, that the thing it could not hear inside its walls was heard perfectly well outside them.

I have seen this in every institution I have known, and first in my own. I was the first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association, elected in 1910, and I resigned in 1914; by then the closest of Freud's followers had formed a secret committee to guard the movement's tenets against me. The movement rejected my work and went on without me for decades, publishing papers, holding congresses, training analysts, maintaining the orthodoxy that Freud built and that I challenged. And the ideas it would not hear - the collective unconscious, the archetypes, individuation, the shadow, introversion and extraversion - entered the culture anyway, around the institution rather than through it, by way of books and lectures and the slow osmosis of ideas true enough to survive the rejection of the bodies that should have recognized them. The institution is still there. The ideas are everywhere.

The institution rejects. The rejected builds. The built thing reaches the undiscovered. The undiscovered adopt it. The institution, confronted with adoption it did not authorize, must choose: absorb or resist. If it absorbs, the institution changes - slowly, reluctantly, claiming credit it does not deserve, but it changes. If it resists, the thing it rejected becomes the standard in fact if not in name, and the distance between fact and name is the distance between the living language and the dead specification, between the code that runs and the document that describes what the code should have been.

On the stone I carved at Bollingen in 1950, for my seventy-fifth birthday, the side that faces the lake carries the alchemists' words: I am an orphan, alone; nevertheless I am found everywhere. Her paper is such an orphan, alone in the archive, unfathered, unsponsored, unrevised. Nevertheless the need it met is found everywhere: in every codebase where the workaround exists, in every engineer who solves the problem by hand, in every Monday morning when the language fails to do what the language should do. The need is found everywhere. The paper that met it is found nowhere - nowhere that matters, nowhere that the institution's memory reaches, nowhere that the consensus model counts. It is a letter delivered to the right address, signed for, and filed unopened.

The committee will go on meeting. At the meeting after her last, the Chair will publish the agenda. The Architect will present another paper. The Delegate will be in his seat. The Newcomer will not be there. Her seat will be empty. No one will remark on it. The committee counts hands, and an absence raises none. The hands that rise will be the hands of those who stayed, and the committee will take their number for the whole of itself, as a man takes his waking attitude for the whole of his psyche.

The empty seat is the committee's dream, the dream it will not interpret, the one it will not write down, and it speaks in the language of absence. The seat is not empty because the Newcomer chose not to come. The seat is empty because the institution emptied it. It is the thing the committee cannot say about itself: that it loses people. That it loses the people it most needs. That the people it keeps are the people who learned to stop judging, and the people it loses are the people who could not.

On Saturday night the Author sits in his hotel room with his laptop open. He means to begin the next revision of his paper. He does not begin. A question comes up in him, bringing what the week left out, and he types it at the top of an empty page, as the Newcomer wrote her dream on the back of a boarding pass: "Does either design, mine or the Architect's, need to be in the standard at all?" He will keep that page for years without once putting it to a room.

I have no prescription for this. I am a diagnostician, not a legislator.

I died in 1961, yet the shadow I named still darkens.
