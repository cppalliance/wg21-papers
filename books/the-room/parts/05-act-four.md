# Act Four

## Crowds and Power in the House of C++

*After Elias Canetti, 1960*

---

I write this in Hampstead, thirty-eight years after the clerk, the man in Prague, put down his pen without finishing. He did not need to finish. The castle does not end. No writer knew power better than he did. He followed one man through the corridors and set down what the corridors do to him: the officials, the forms, the waiting. My method is the other one. I stand among the many and set down what they do together: why the corridors fill, how the crowd that fills them behaves, and what happens when it discharges.

I have spent forty years on crowds. I learned them in the streets of Vienna on the fifteenth of July 1927, when the crowd that burned the Palace of Justice took me along with it. I read about them in the Reading Room of the British Museum during the war. I found them in the myths and chronicles of every people that left a record of its masses. And now I am shown a committee. It meets three times a year. Nearly every month a mailing brings it papers, more than a hundred in the busiest. It votes in rooms where shoulders touch shoulders and hands rise together. I see in it every crowd I have studied. The stagnating crowd of the room. The invisible crowd of the mailing. The double crowd of competing proposals. The crowd crystals of the peerage. The discharge of the straw poll. None of this is new to me.

The Florentine described the principality in 1513. The philosopher diagnosed the values in 1887. The clerk walked the corridors in 1922. Four hundred and forty-seven years of analysis. The institution adopted a three-year release cadence, created twenty-three study groups, and proposed rules against the use of new technologies in its own papers. The crowd is the same crowd. The discharge is the same discharge. The hands change at every meeting. The rising never does.

Every crowd shares four traits, and the committee shows each of them. A crowd must increase, and the mailing increases. Inside a crowd each member counts as one, and the poll counts each hand as one. A crowd seeks the press of bodies, and the room presses them close. A crowd must have somewhere to go, and the next standard gives it somewhere to go. "A crowd exists so long as it has an unattained goal." The committee's goal is never attained. When one standard is finished, the next is already on the calendar, three years off. A crowd that cannot live without a goal takes whatever goal is offered.

## XXIII. The Fear of Being Touched

> "There is nothing that man fears more than the touch of the unknown."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

The taxi from the airport passes through a gray suburb of identical houses. Above the rooftops, the moon. The committee cannot schedule it. Two passengers sit in the back, a careful distance between them. They do not know each other. They established this in the first thirty seconds: a nod, a name, an employer, a country. Now they are quiet. The Author checks his phone. The Newcomer looks out the window.

They are both going to the same hotel, because they are both going to the same meeting. The committee that writes the specification for a programming language used by unasked millions convenes three times a year, wherever a host offers it rooms. This week it is a conference center attached to a hotel in a country neither of them has been to before. They have come to spend six days in windowless rooms deciding what the unasked must use for the next decade. Neither of them has done this before.

The Author is a mid-career engineer at a mid-size company. He has written a paper - his first. It proposes a small, clean solution to a problem he encounters every day in his own codebase. He has tested the design. He has implemented it, and for a year the engineers around him have depended on it. It is correct, and it is used. The committee asks of every paper whether it is correct. It does not ask whether what the users already have is good enough.

The Newcomer is younger, from a small company in a country whose national body sends one delegate to the committee and does not always fill the seat. Her paper also solves a real problem - a problem that tens of thousands of developers have worked around for years with ugly hacks and half-measures. Her solution is simple. She carries a printed copy in her bag, in case the projector fails.

For most of the ride they do not speak about their papers. Each sits enclosed in a private architecture of distance. Men keep such distances wherever they go. "They shut themselves in houses which no-one may enter, and only there feel some measure of security." The Author has his employer, his title, his publication record - thin as it is. The Newcomer has her expertise, her domain knowledge, her small company's name that no one in the committee has heard. These are houses. They do not protect against much, but they are what these two have, and they keep them apart from each other and from the two hundred delegates they are about to meet.

Tomorrow they will enter the committee, and the fear will reverse.

The fear turns into its opposite at one point only. "It is only in a crowd that man can become free of this fear of being touched." For a newcomer to the committee, that point comes on the first morning. The registration table. The badge with one's name on it. The corridor full of people wearing the same badge, walking with the same purposeful uncertainty toward the same rooms. The first handshake with someone whose name one knows from a paper. The second handshake with someone whose name one does not know but who seems to know everyone. Little by little, almost without being felt, the distances brought from home dissolve.

By Monday afternoon, the Author and the Newcomer will be sitting in rooms where their shoulders touch the shoulders of strangers, where they breathe the same recycled air, where they listen to the same presentations and raise the same objections and vote on the same polls. For one week, the hierarchies of their ordinary lives - employer, title, country, seniority - are suspended. They are delegates. They have badges. They are equal, or they appear to be, which in the life of crowds amounts to the same thing.

I called the moment of equality the discharge. "This is the moment when all who belong to the crowd get rid of their differences and feel equal." In the committee, the discharge comes at the straw poll. Hands rise together. For that instant, a senior architect from a platform company and a first-time author from a startup weigh the same. One hand, one vote. The distances collapse. The crowd exists.

The equality does not last. "The people who suddenly feel equal have not really become equal; nor will they feel equal for ever." When the hands come down, each returns to his own distances. The Author and the Newcomer will learn this. They will learn it at different speeds, and the lessons will cost them different things. The Author will pay in years. The Newcomer will pay in a single evening.

In the taxi, none of this has happened yet. They are two engineers with proposals, two creatures approaching the same watering hole. The city outside is gray and unfamiliar. The hotel is close now. The Author asks the Newcomer what her paper is about. She tells him. He says it sounds good - clear, practical, the kind of thing he would use himself. She smiles.

They will not speak again.

## XXIV. The Mailing: An Invisible Crowd

> "Over the whole earth, wherever there are men, is found the conception of the invisible dead."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

Three months before the taxi, three papers entered the mailing.

The mailing is the committee's circulatory system. Nearly every month a deadline passes and papers pour in - proposals, revisions, position statements, issue resolutions, field reports. They are assigned numbers and posted to a public archive. A quiet month brings a few dozen. The mailings around a meeting often bring more than a hundred, and the largest more than a hundred and sixty.

The Author submitted his paper on the last day. He had rewritten the introduction four times. He had checked every code example. He had asked a colleague to read it, and the colleague - who had never heard of the committee - said it was good. He clicked "submit" the way a man lowers a bottle into the ocean. Then he waited.

The Newcomer submitted hers two days earlier. She had written it in English, which is not her first language, and she read each sentence aloud before she let it stand. She had no colleague to review it. Her company's other C++ developers knew the language well but had never read a committee paper. She submitted it the way a student hands an exam to a teacher she has never met.

In the same mailing, occupying the same numbered sequence, the Architect's paper appeared as R5. It had been revised across three years and four prior presentations. It listed co-authors from two compiler vendors and a platform company. Its revision history alone was longer than the Author's entire paper. It arrived not as a bottle in the ocean but as a ship that had already been sighted, charted, and assigned a berth.

One hundred and sixty-five papers in total. The mailing is an invisible crowd.

I gave a section of my book to crowds that cannot be seen, and the first of them is the dead. Every people pictures its dead gathered somewhere. "It is tempting to call it humanity's oldest conception." The dead are imagined as being together, in enormous numbers, in a place that the living cannot reach. The Pygmies of Gaboon sing of them in a cave whose gates are shut:

"The souls of the dead are crowding there in droves, / Like a swarm of flies, / Like a swarm of flies, dancing at evening time."

The mailing is this cave. Its gates open with every deadline, and the papers crowd in - droves of them, each one the distilled labor of weeks or months or years, each one containing someone's attempt to touch the standard that touches the unasked many. The gates shut. The papers are in the archive. And there they remain, most of them, unseen and unread, like the dead in their cave, "crowding there in droves."

No one reads the whole crowd. I watch one delegate open the mailing. He reads the papers for his own working group, twenty or so. The rest exist for him as titles in a table of contents - names on a memorial he walks past on the way to his own room. He slows at the names he knows and passes the others with respect and without reading.

The Author's paper is now in the cave. He does not know who will read it. He does not know that most will not. He refreshes the archive page to confirm that it appears, that his number is correct, that the formatting survived the upload. It is there. It exists. It has entered the crowd of the dead.

Among the invisible crowds I counted one more, the seed: two hundred million in a single crowd, of which one survives. "Each of these animalcules carries with it everything of our ancestors which will be preserved." So it is with the mailing. A hundred and sixty-five papers arrive. A handful will reach the working draft. The rest will fertilize nothing. They will lie in the archive, each holding its author's best thinking, each invisible, each one of a multitude that exists so that something might emerge.

The Newcomer's paper is also in the cave. It is number P-something, four digits, a designation that tells the reader nothing about the content, the author, or the quality of the work. It sits in the same table of contents as the Architect's R5, which is also P-something. The table does not distinguish between them. This is the only equality the committee offers unconditionally: the equality of the mailing, where every paper is a number in a list, identical in font and format, undifferentiated by the system that receives it. It is the equality of the dead.

This equality is destroyed at once. The Architect's paper has a reflector thread, a prior poll history, a network of delegates who have already read it and spoken for it. The Author's paper has nothing. The Newcomer's paper has less than nothing - it arrives from outside the committee's memory. Papers from unknown authors lie below the threshold of the hunt.

## XXV. The Reflector: Wind and Whisper

> "The strength of wind varies, and, with it, its voice."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

The reflector is the committee's mailing list - a members' channel where delegates discuss papers, raise objections, and declare their positions before the meeting. Every member may read it, and most members do not.

I chose wind as one of the crowd symbols. Its voice rises and falls with its strength, and men have always heard a living thing in it. "Apart from its voice, the most striking thing about wind is its direction."

The reflector is this wind. It is invisible, but it is felt. Its direction changes. A paper that arrives at a meeting with favorable discussion behind it has the wind at its back. A paper that arrives after hostile discussion meets a headwind. And a paper that arrives with no discussion at all meets dead air, the worst condition of all.

The Author's paper generates a thread. Seven replies come in over two days: three from people he has never heard of, each asking where the design has been used; one from a vendor engineer, courteous and noncommittal; and three that argue with one another and not with him.

Each question touches the man before it touches the design. I have told how the Bushmen feel game approaching in their own bodies - a tapping on the ribs that means springbok, a tapping at the back of the neck that means ostrich. "The presentiment speaks the truth," they say. The Author feels the reflector's questions as a tapping in his flesh. He cannot see who is coming. He answers each one carefully. He gives implementation details. He cites deployment at his company. The thread goes quiet.

The Architect's paper generates a different kind of thread. It is longer - twelve replies across ten days. His allies post supportive analysis. A well-known name writes: "This is the right direction. We should advance this at the next meeting." Another delegate, from a compiler vendor that co-authors the paper, provides benchmarks. A third flags a minor issue and immediately proposes a resolution, so that the issue reads as manageable rather than fundamental. The wind blows steadily in his favor.

Like a river, wind has a direction, but no banks hold it. On the reflector the first voices set that direction. Delegates who did not read the paper read the thread, or its first and last replies, and lean the way it blows. This is not weather. It is politics.

The Newcomer's paper receives no replies at all. Not opposition. Not skepticism. Nothing.

Two weeks pass. Three weeks. The mailing deadline comes and goes. The meeting agenda is published. Her paper is listed - but in a parallel session, at the hour when the working group will be discussing the Architect's paper in the main room. The agenda does not say what the placement will cost her.

The silence is its own kind of wind - not a headwind, which at least admits there is something to resist, but the absence of air. Her paper does not reach the condition of prey. "The hunting pack moves with all its force towards a living object which it wants to kill in order subsequently to incorporate it." The pack must first sight its quarry. Her paper has not been sighted. It will not be chased. It will not be killed. It will simply never be discussed, a death so quiet that no one, including its author, will know exactly when it happened.

She refreshes her email for another week. Nothing comes. She begins preparing her slides anyway. She practices her presentation in her apartment, speaking to an empty room, adjusting her timing, making sure the examples are clear. She is thorough. She is competent. She is preparing for a room that will have six people in it and no poll at the end.

"Secrecy lies at the very core of power." Silence is a verdict that requires no judge.

## XXVI. The Room: The Stagnating Crowd

> "The patience of a stagnating crowd becomes less astonishing if one realizes fully the importance this feeling of density has for it."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

Monday morning. Two rooms.

In Room A, the library evolution group convenes to discuss the design space that both the Author's paper and the Architect's paper address. Fifty delegates sit in rows of chairs that were never designed for comfort. The air conditioning hums. Laptops open on every lap. Coffee cups on the floor beside every chair. The density is real. Body presses against body. Elbows negotiate armrests. The room is full because the topic is important, and it is important because two competing proposals have been scheduled back to back, and everyone who cares about the design space - and many who do not but have heard that the discussion will be contentious - has come to watch.

This is the stagnating crowd. It is packed so close that no one can move freely, and it is passive. "It waits for a head to be shown it, or for words, or it watches a fight." The delegates have waited for this. The reflector threads, the hallway conversations at the last meeting, the pre-meeting dinners - all of it was prelude. Now the crowd is assembled, and it gathers pressure. Latecomers take the last chairs.

At the front of the room stands the Chair.

Like a conductor, the Chair is the only person who stands. In front of him sit the delegates - his orchestra. Behind him, on the screen, are the slides he will call up one at a time. He holds the agenda. He holds the speaking order. He holds the clock. He will word the poll question. He will read the results. "He has the power of life and death over the voices of the instruments; one long silent will speak again at his command."

The Chair is scrupulously neutral. His voice and his hands give away no preference. He gives each presenter the same time. He is fair. And yet his power is enormous, because fairness is not neutrality. The Architect presents first. This is not chance. The Architect's paper is the later revision, the one with prior poll history, the one the room expects to see first because it has been here before. The Author presents second. He is the challenger. The order is fair. It is also decisive.

The Architect's presentation is polished. His slides have the typographic consistency of a conference keynote. His code examples compile - he shows this live, in the room, which draws a murmur of appreciation that functions as applause. He speaks for twelve minutes. He has given this talk before, in different rooms, at conferences, and the room knows him. Few of them know his paper. But they know his face, his voice, the easy confidence of a man who has stood in this position many times. The stagnating crowd receives him with patience. "The stagnating crowd lives for its discharge. But it feels certain of this and puts it off."

When he finishes, two colleagues ask questions from the floor. The questions are friendly - clarifying, not challenging. "Could you elaborate on the interaction with feature X?" "Have you considered the case where Y?" The Architect answers smoothly. The room is warm. The temperature has been set.

The Author presents second. His slides are adequate - clean, but without the polish of someone who has had three years to refine them. His design is technically sound. In some respects it is the more elegant: it handles certain edge cases more naturally, it composes better, it requires less boilerplate. The room asks whether the design is correct, and it is. Then the room asks what a dense crowd asks: which presenter belongs here, and which proposal it wants. The users for whom both designs were written sit in neither room.

The first questioner speaks from the floor. He is a recognized name - someone the room respects, someone who has chaired a study group and authored several papers in this domain. His question to the Author is reasonable: "Who else has reviewed this?" The Author answers: a colleague at his company, and the engineers there who have used the design every day for a year. The room knows none of them.

A question is a touch. "The questioner knows what there is to find, but he wants actually to touch it and bring it to light." The question touches the Author in front of the room and sorts him. The Architect's paper is the incumbent. The Author's paper is the interloper. The question does not say any of this. It does not need to. The room hears it. The crowd was to free him of the fear of being touched. The touch comes from inside it.

The straw poll. The Chair reads the question carefully. He has worded it to be neutral - a comparison, not a verdict: "Which approach should the group pursue?" Hands rise for P-first, and the Author counts them. Twenty-eight. Then for P-second. Eighteen. Four do not vote. The Architect wins by ten.

The discharge comes, but for one side only. The Architect's supporters feel the relief I described: the momentary erasure of distinction, the feeling of equality, the satisfaction of having been part of a decision. The Author's supporters feel the contraction, the retreat into separate houses.

A vote is a battle in which no one dies. The raised hands are counted as soldiers are counted, and it is taken that twenty-eight would have beaten eighteen; no one is left on the field. "No one has ever really believed that the majority decision is necessarily the wiser one because it has received the greater number of votes." The man who is outvoted gives up the field and keeps his conviction, and since this battle killed no one, he expects others.

The Author sits with his laptop open and watches the next presentation begin. In a new file he types the number ten. He is already back inside his distances.

Meanwhile, in Room B, the Newcomer presents.

Room B is a parallel session, running at the same time as the discussion in Room A. It is listed on the schedule. It has its own chair. But its topic cannot compete with the fight in Room A, and the same pull that fills Room A to capacity leaves Room B nearly empty.

Six people attend. The Newcomer stands at the front of a room designed for forty and speaks to six, three of whom are checking their laptops. She gives her presentation. It is good - her examples are clear, her design is practical, her English is careful. She finishes in ten minutes. There is one question: "This seems useful. Have you talked to the study group about it?" She has not. She did not know which study group to talk to. She did not know that talking to a study group was something she was supposed to do before arriving.

There is no poll. The chair of Room B does not call one because the paper has not been through the study group process that would make a poll appropriate. The Newcomer thanks the six people. They nod politely. She returns to her seat and opens her laptop and stares at the screen.

There is no discharge. The stagnating crowd in Room A got its discharge. The stagnating crowd in Room B was never a crowd at all. Six people in a room designed for forty are not dense enough to stagnate, not compressed enough to gather pressure, not numerous enough to discharge. They are six individuals who happened to be in the same room, and they disperse without having formed anything together.

Scheduling is a form of power. A paper that never reaches the agenda never reaches a poll. The Newcomer's paper reached the agenda. It was listed, given a room and an hour, and the hour ran against the fight that drew every delegate with an opinion into Room A. No one decided to kill her paper. No one had to. The schedule killed it, and the schedule was set by people who had more important things to think about than a first-time author from a small company in a country whose national body sends one delegate to the committee.

## XXVII. The Peerage: Crowd Crystals

> "Their unity is more important than their size."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

Tuesday evening. The hotel restaurant.

The Author sits across from the Patron at a table near the window. The Patron is in his sixties. His hair is white. He wears a jacket that is slightly too formal for the venue and slightly too informal for a man of his reputation. He has authored no paper in five years. He holds no chair, no board seat, no formal title in the committee's hierarchy. But when he enters a room, people shift. They do not move toward him - that would be too obvious - but their attention shifts, the way the attention of an orchestra shifts when a former conductor enters the hall. He is consulted without being asked. He is deferred to without being named. He is a crowd crystal in retirement: still rigid, still constant, but no longer precipitating crowds. He has the crystal's permanence without its function.

"Your paper is correct," the Patron says. He cuts his steak with the careful movements of a man who has spent a lifetime being precise. "The design is clean. The edge cases are handled well. You have implementation experience. That matters more than most people here will admit." He sets his knife down. "A correct paper gets a hearing. It does not raise a single hand."

The Author asks what does.

The Patron considers this. "Trust," he says. "Not trust that your paper is correct - anyone can verify that by reading it. Trust that you are the kind of person whose work can be relied on over time. That you will address concerns. That you will not become hostile under pressure. That you will come back next meeting and the meeting after that. That your paper is not an orphan."

Crowd crystals are the "small, rigid groups of men, strictly delimited and of great constancy, which serve to precipitate crowds." The committee's peerage is such a crystal. It consists of some twenty people - chairs, former chairs, direction group members, foundation board members, conference keynote speakers. They know each other. They have dinner together. They review each other's papers. They chair each other's sessions. They are an institution within the institution, and it renews itself without anyone deciding that it should.

The crystal keeps its size and its form. "Its members are trained in both action and faith." The peerage's training has no curriculum, but it runs as reliably as one. Newcomers judged promising - competent, and institutionally compatible - are drawn into roles. They are encouraged to chair a study group. They are invited to co-author a paper with an established name. They are introduced to the right people at the right dinners. The way to influence runs through these hands. A newcomer who arrives with correct papers but no patron faces a higher burden than a newcomer who arrives with a patron but no papers.

The Patron never uses the word "patron." When he speaks of the younger engineers he has helped, the word he uses is "mentor," and the help is real. But it passes through a crystal, and a crystal holds to its own form. It admits those who already move as it moves. Within any crystal, the members who know its ways rise above the members who know only the craft.

Across the table from the Patron, the Author sets down his fork. He has watched the Architect and the Chair since Monday. Their closeness is visible to anyone who watches: they sit together at meals, they present together at conferences, the Chair schedules the Architect's papers favorably and words the polls in the Architect's terminology. The Architect has a patron, and the Author does not, and that weighs more than the difference between their papers. None of this is corrupt, and the crystal does not need it to be.

"Even when they appear separately, people always think of the rigid unit to which they belong, the monastery or the regiment." The committee never sees the Architect alone. It sees him as a member of his crystal - the cluster of delegates from his company, the co-authors on his paper, the Chair who advances his work. When he presents, the room sees the crystal behind him. When the Author presents, the room sees a man standing alone.

The Patron finishes his steak. "Find a co-author," he says. "Someone the room recognizes. Someone who has been here before. Your paper is good enough to attract one, if you ask." The Author nods. He takes out his phone and types one word into it: co-author. The vote measures the crowd behind a paper. Across five hundred pages I showed how crowds form: around density, direction, the prospect of discharge, and a crystal at the center. The merit of a cause is not on that list.

The Author thanks the Patron. They shake hands. The Patron goes to the elevator. The Author goes to the bar.

At the far end of the bar, the Newcomer sits alone. The Author does not see her. She has a plate of food she has not touched and a glass of wine she has half-finished. She is reading the schedule for tomorrow on her phone. At the next table, two delegates she recognizes from Room A, where she sat through Tuesday's session, are having dinner. They are insiders. They move through the menu, the hotel staff, and each other's company with ease. They have been here before. They will be here again. They are speaking in a language she understands, and she catches a fragment: "...another paper from nobody. Did not even get scheduled properly. Six people in the room." The two of them laugh.

Nothing in the fragment names her. She does not ask. She finishes her wine. She goes upstairs. She opens her laptop and looks at flights home. There is one tomorrow evening. She books it. She will come to one more meeting, six months from now, because she has already committed the travel budget to her company. At that meeting she will sit in the back of a room and not present. Then she will stop coming.

I designate as the Empty Seat the departure of a domain expert from a room that no longer responds to expertise. Someone else will take the seat. The committee will not notice the change, for it did not notice the Newcomer while she was there. Indifference is harder to fight than opposition. Opposition offers a body to push against. Indifference offers only the smooth wall of a process that does not see you, that was not designed to see you, that will go on producing standards whether you are in the room or not.

## XXVIII. The Double Crowd: War Between Papers

> "War has to do with killing. The enemy ranks are 'thinned.'"
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

Six months later. A different city, the same conference center layout, the same recycled air.

The Author has not been idle. After the first defeat he went home and sat with the ten-vote margin for a long time. He wrote to three of the delegates who had voted against him and asked what it would take. Two replied. Then, over six months, he brought others to the paper, one at a time. The first was a co-author, a respected library developer whose name the room would recognize; he read the design and added his name to the next revision without a speech. Two implementers who had tried the design in their own code came next, and then the two delegates who had answered his emails. This week they sit together on the paper's side of the room, near enough to touch.

The Author has done what the Patron told him. He has formed a hunting pack.

War is the double crowd in its plainest form. "It is killing wholesale; as many of the enemy as possible are cut down." Two crowds face each other. Each wants the other's number to fall. Each wants its own number to rise. The battle is decided by which crowd remains larger at the end.

Two papers now compete for the same design space: the Author's, revised to R2, and the Architect's, advancing toward the working draft at R6. The papers solve the same problem in incompatible ways, and to each camp the standard has room for one approach, not two. One must die so that the other can live. The two camps sit in the same room. They eat at the same restaurants. They are polite to each other. But when the poll comes, each will try to produce a heap of enemy dead.

The second poll. The Author presents his R2 to a room that has divided before he begins: his camp near the front, the Architect's along the far side, and the aisle between them the boundary of two crowds. He finishes. Questions come from both sides, and each side listens to how the other's are answered.

The Chair reads the comparison again. Twenty-five for the Architect's approach. Twenty-two for the Author's. Three do not vote. "The surest, and often the only, way by which a crowd can preserve itself lies in the existence of a second crowd to which it is related." The Author's camp holds together because the Architect's faces it, and the Architect's allies sit closer together than they did six months ago. Three hands apart, each crowd keeps the other alive for another meeting.

The lamenting pack forms around the Author's defeat. His co-author. The two delegates who had replied to his emails. One sympathetic elder who says, "the direction is right, the timing is wrong." They gather informally at the coffee station, at dinner, in the hallway outside the room. They speak quietly. They share the anguish of a pack that is sure it is right and could not move the crowd.

"The most impressive description of a lamenting pack known to me comes from the Warramunga in Central Australia." The mourners throw themselves on the dying man. They cut themselves with knives. They beat their heads. Their violence is the grief of a pack that has lost a member and fears its own dissolution. The Author's lamenting pack is quieter, but its form is the same. They have lost a battle. They fear that the next loss will be the last. They hold to each other, for the alternative is to scatter and abandon the paper.

The lamenting pack turns. "From the lamenting pack around a dead man there forms a war pack bent on avenging him." The Author revises again. R3. He adds a section on the Architect's design, setting out a structural limitation that no revision of the Architect's paper names. He writes it as a technical observation, without heat. The observation is correct. The limitation is real. The section's last sentence predicts that users who meet the limitation will come back with correction papers. The room, when it sees R3, will have to contend with it.

Meanwhile, the Architect's paper advances. It has won two polls, and it goes to the wording group. The Author's paper is still the challenger. The Architect's paper is on its way to becoming the law.

Then a new crowd forms around the Architect's paper, one that was never in the room. National body reviewers begin reading it as it nears the working draft. They are specialists in different countries who read the wording with the care of people who will have to implement it or explain it or defend it before their own national mirror committees. They are the closed crowd that follows the room, and they judge by other means. They read.

No ballot is open, but one reviewer raises a concern on the committee's lists: by its structure, the Architect's design loses performance in the very use case it was built to serve. The room never weighed it.

The Architect answers on the list that the point is noted.

Each crowd shows the other only its front. A paper that names its tradeoffs hands its opponents a weapon. A paper that does not name them passes smoothly, until it ships and users find what the committee did not examine. Under the committee's rule, consensus is marked by "the absence of sustained opposition," and opposition requires knowledge, and knowledge requires disclosure, and disclosure is voluntary.

The double crowd begins to turn. The Author's R3 names the same limitation. It is no longer the challenger's complaint. It is the independent confirmation of what the reviewer found. The war between the two papers moves onto the page, into the public record, where a tradeoff named in writing stays named.

The Author's crowd is growing. The Architect's is not shrinking - the weight of the institution behind him remains - but it has stopped growing. The Author's camp expects the turn to take another meeting, another revision, another poll. But the direction has changed. The wind has shifted.

## XXIX. Plenary: The Arena

> "The spectators turn their backs to the city."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

A year after the taxi. Another city. Saturday, the last day of the meeting.

Plenary is the committee's parliament - the session where the whole body votes on motions to apply reviewed wording to the working draft. Two hundred delegates sit in a large room. The chairs stand in rows, not in a circle, but the room is an arena all the same, closed twice: once against everything outside its walls, and once within its own ring of faces. An arena's spectators sit with the city behind them. "They have been lifted out of its structure of walls and streets and, for the duration of their time in the arena, they do not care about anything which happens there." For the next four hours, nothing exists outside this room. No employers, no national bodies, none of the unasked. Only the motions.

The Delegate enters and finds a seat in the middle of the room. He is one of eighty, or ninety, or a hundred. No one has defined the category. He is a delegate and only that - a working engineer, mid-career, sent by his employer to participate in the standards process. He has sat in his working group all week. He has contributed. He has asked clarifying questions. He has voted on things he understood.

Today he will vote on some thirty motions. He has read the papers behind three of them.

All week the committee has worked in six rooms at once, and now it gathers in one to vote on what all six produced. The Delegate sat in one room. The other five met without him. The papers discussed in them number in the dozens. Some of them are hundreds of pages long. He has read none of them. He has heard about some of them from colleagues at dinner. He has a rough sense of which proposals are contested and which are routine. On the contested ones, he will watch what happens in the room. On the routine ones, he will do what the room does.

Pressure grows with density. "The more people who flow into that formation, the stronger the pressure becomes; feet have nowhere to move, arms are pinned down and only heads remain free, to see and to hear; every impulse is passed directly from body to body." The Delegate feels this pressure. The room is full. The air is warm. The convener stands at the front - a different figure from the working group chair, higher in the hierarchy, further from the material, with one eye always on the clock. The convener reads the first motion and asks whether there is any objection to unanimous consent. There is none. The motion passes. Next motion.

The rhythm is hypnotic. Motion after motion, the room discharges and re-forms. Most motions pass by unanimous consent in a minute or two. When someone objects, the convener takes a count: favor, against, neutral. On most motions the Delegate has no position of his own. He watches the front rows, where the people who sat in the wording groups are. He listens for the first speaker from the floor. When a recognized name speaks in favor, the room loosens and the motion passes. When a recognized name objects, the room stiffens. Hands that would have risen in favor stay down, or rise for neutral.

Late in the session the Architect's paper comes up. The room is tired. The chair of the library wording group reads the motion in the form every motion takes: move to apply the paper to the C++ working draft. Behind the motion stand four years of revisions, every poll the design group has taken on it, and two meetings in which the wording group went through its text line by line. The motion tells the room that every group behind it has said yes already.

"Every command consists of momentum and sting." The motion's momentum reaches the Delegate.

The convener asks whether there is any discussion. Anyone could stand now and ask whether the users' existing tools are good enough. The Author stands instead, near the back, and says what his R3 says: that by its structure the design loses performance in the very use case it was built to serve, that a national body reviewer said the same on the committee's lists, and that the design group has never weighed it. He says it plainly, without heat, and it is correct. The chair of the wording group answers that the wording does what the design says; the design is not his to answer for. The convener thanks the Author and notes that the national bodies will have their say in the ballot. The Author sits down. He has spent a year trying to stop this, and he has had two minutes.

A crowd near its goal does not like to be held. The goal is the last motion of the meeting, which will send the draft out for ballot. The Author's objection is correct, and it is spoken once, and a single voice spoken once is a stone in a river: the water parts around it and closes again. Few in the room disagree with him. Fewer have heard him. The room is moving, and he is not.

The Author objects to unanimous consent, and the convener calls for a count. When the convener asks for those in favor, the hands in the front rows go up first, where the people who reviewed the wording sit, and the hands behind them follow. The Delegate's hand rises a moment after theirs. It is his thirtieth assent of the session. Most of the others he gave by staying silent when the convener asked for objections. It costs him nothing, and it leaves nothing in him. "A command to a crowd leaves no sting behind." That is how a man can give thirty in a morning. The Delegate's hand does not carry his judgment. It carries the room's mood, reflected back to the room through his body.

The count: forty-seven in favor. Three against, the Author's among them. Twelve neutral, his co-author's among them. The rest - more than a hundred delegates - raise no hand at all, though every one of them is in the room. Their silence counts for neither side, and the count is forty-seven to three. Consensus is the convener's judgment, and the convener declares it. The motion passes with the hands of fewer than a quarter of those present, and the minutes will keep only the numbers.

The Architect's paper is the law.

The working draft is larger by one feature. "The urge to grow is the first and supreme attribute of the crowd." The standard is a crowd of this kind, and it must grow at every meeting. Each plenary adds its motions to the draft, and a plenary that added nothing would feel like a crowd beginning to disperse.

The Author watches the count from his seat near the back. His shoulders drop. The discharge reaches him too, against his will - the relief, the sense that something has been decided, the tiredness that follows tension. But the commands he obeyed were given to him alone, and a command received alone leaves its sting. For a year he has done as he was told: three revisions, three defeats, one dinner with the Patron, and a hundred emails. He is harder. He is more strategic. He has a co-author. He speaks to the right people before a meeting. He knows which questions come first from the floor, and which way a poll's wording leans. The commands are lodged in him.

He looks along the back rows. At a meeting six months ago, he saw the Newcomer once, at the back of a room, listening to a session in which she did not present. She is not here. At the end of the last row there is an empty chair. No one has mentioned her departure. The empty chair is simply there, unoccupied, the way hundreds of proposals are simply there in the archive, unread. The room has moved on. The convener is already reading the last motion of the meeting.

## XXX. The Ballot: The Closed Crowd

> "The closed crowd renounces growth and puts the stress on permanence."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

The same plenary votes to send the working draft out for ballot. It goes to the national bodies as a Committee Draft, and the ballot begins. Each has its national mirror committee, a group of specialists who will read the draft and file comments on every defect they find.

Each national body is a closed crowd, and a closed crowd begins with a boundary. Here the boundary is national. One sits in a mirror committee as a citizen or resident of its country. The entrances are few: one must know that the committee exists and ask to be admitted. "In this way the crowd sacrifices its chance of growth, but gains in staying power."

The Architect's feature is in the draft, and the committee voted for it. Since the plenary that applied it, his papers have been about other things. His job, as he sees it, is done.

Then the national body comments arrive.

Four hundred and eleven comments across the entire draft. A dozen concern his feature. Each comment is a wound - small, exact, delivered in the formal language of the ballot, but a wound nonetheless. Their authors are the closed crowd reading what the arena of plenary passed without reading it.

One comment in particular. It brings back the concern first raised on the committee's lists. It is filed by a national body on another continent, written by an engineer who has never attended a committee meeting but who has tried to implement the Architect's feature in his company's codebase. The comment reads: "By its structure, the design loses performance in the very use case it was built to serve. The tradeoff was not evaluated by the working group." He has attached benchmarks. They are damning.

Of the invisible crowds, posterity is the one we still feel. "For two, or perhaps three, generations a man can count his posterity; from then on it lies entirely in the future." The room voted for a crowd it will never see: the users who will write with this feature for decades. The engineer on the other continent is that crowd arriving while it can still be counted - one man, one benchmark, one sentence. He is the first face of the unasked.

The Architect reads the comment on a Tuesday morning. He is at his desk in his employer's office, the office that paid for his flights and his hotel rooms and his fifteen years of committee attendance. He reads the benchmarks. The limitation follows from choices made early in the paper's life. No revision names it: not R1, not R3, not R5, not R6, not the wording the plenary applied. The trail lies in the archive for anyone to read.

His response arrives within the week. It is technically adequate. He proposes a resolution - an extension point that would allow future papers to address the limitation. The comment's author accepts it. The comment is marked "resolved." But the comment has left what no plenary count could: a written record of what was not evaluated. It will be there when the correction papers come.

Some features survive the ballot intact. Some are modified by the comment resolution process. Some do not survive at all. They are removed from the draft, returned to the working group for further work, or simply abandoned by authors who lack the energy to continue. These join the heap of the dead.

I have been death's enemy all my life, and I have looked long at its heap. "The heap of the dead is also felt to be a unit; some languages have a special word for it." The committee's archive is such a heap, and some of its dead have risen. Contracts entered the working draft in 2018, left it the next year, and returned, redesigned, for C++26. Concepts were deferred for a decade and came back changed. Networking became a Technical Specification and never entered the standard. In 2021 a poll found a weak consensus that it should take another design. Each lay for years among the dead. Nothing is ever taken out of the archive, and every mailing adds to it.

Over the next two meetings the comments are resolved or declined, and the draft goes out for its final ballot and comes back approved. The Architect's feature is in the standard, permanent now, or as permanent as anything in a standard that sheds features only slowly. While the standard was a draft, it had to grow. Published, it is a closed crowd. It renounces growth where it has already grown.

At the next meeting the Author's paper is heard once more, at R4, in the group where it began. Nobody disputes it. The man who once asked who else had reviewed it says now that it is correct, and the Chair agrees, courteously, and then says what the room has already concluded: the facility is in the published standard; the committee does not break what it has published; two facilities that do the same thing would be poor engineering. He thanks the Author for the work. There is no poll, because there is nothing left to decide. The paper is closed with a line in the minutes, and the line says that it was correct.

The war was fought as if the standard had room for one approach. It had.

## XXXI. The Public: The Open Crowd and the Eruption

> "I designate as eruption the sudden transition from a closed into an open crowd."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

The standard ships.

First the compilers. The major implementations release versions with partial support for the new features. Then the conference talks. Speakers who were in the room explain to audiences who were not what the new standard contains and why they should care. Then the blog posts. Engineers who have tried the new features in their own codebases report what works and what does not. Then the threads on social media, and the arguments in them.

This is the eruption. "A crowd quite often seems to overflow from some well-guarded space into the squares and streets of a town where it can move about freely, exposed to everything and attracting everyone."

The committee is the guarded space. The standard is what overflows. The crowd that receives it, the unasked who have never attended a meeting, is the true open crowd, the crowd "abandoning itself freely to its natural urge for growth." There are no limits to it. It includes everyone who writes the language, everyone who reads about it, everyone who has an opinion. The committee's two hundred delegates, with their badges and their working groups and their straw polls, are a closed crowd that made the standard in rooms the public does not enter. Now the standard is public, and the crowd that judges it is larger, harsher, and more honest than the crowd that made it.

The Blogger has never attended a committee meeting. She has never seen a national body comment or a straw poll, and she has never read SD-4. She is a senior engineer at a hardware company. She writes C++ for a living. For years her codebase used an open-source library for this job, and the library kept changing, release after release; when she hit one of its limits, a later version moved to meet her. Then the committee standardized the Architect's version of the same facility, and her team moved to it, as teams do when a facility becomes standard. The standardized version is fixed in place. The structural limitation the national body reviewer identified is in it, and it falls exactly on the pattern her codebase relies on.

She writes a blog post. The title is "Nobody Asked For This." The post is careful. She shows the code she wrote against the library, and the same code against the standard facility. She shows the error she got. She shows the workaround she had to use, which is uglier than the code she wrote before the feature existed. She names the limitation: the facility loses performance in the very use case it was built to serve. She does not attack the committee or the implementers. She writes that the library she had kept getting better, and that the standard froze one version of it and asked her to switch to something worse.

Power fixes what it holds. "The non-transformer has been set on a pedestal at a fixed height in a fixed and permanent place." No one in the room had asked whether the library she had was good enough.

The post travels from forum to forum for days, and the comments under it fall into three camps: those who agree and share similar experiences, those who defend the committee and explain that the feature was designed for a different use case, and those who say "just use another language." The third camp is a flight crowd, "created by a threat. Everyone flees; everyone is drawn along." The threat is not the feature. The threat is the gathering evidence that what works will come from somewhere else. The flight crowd presses together in forum threads and blog comments, assuring itself that the exit is real and that there is somewhere to go.

Most of the unasked stay, and the rhythm of the standard keeps them, as the great religions keep their crowds. "Their concern is to keep the followers they have won and, in order to do this and also to win new ones, they have to assemble them from time to time." The standard arrives every three years. The developers take up what works for them and leave the rest. The conferences are the assemblies. Every year the faithful travel to them, sit together in a darkened hall, hear the new standard explained, and take its examples home to their teams. They come back the next year. This is how a slow crowd is kept.

The Author reads the blog post. His paper - the one that lost the first two polls, the one he revised four times, the one the room closed with a line that said it was correct - appears nowhere in it. He reads the post to the end, and then again. He does not reply. He sends the link to his co-author with no message. The open crowd has not heard of the war.

An ocean away, the Newcomer reads the same blog post at her desk. She is at her company, in her city. She reads the Blogger's code example. She reads the error. She reads the workaround. Her hand stops on the mouse. It is the problem her paper addressed - the R0 that entered the mailing three months before the taxi, that received no reflector replies, that was scheduled against the Architect's paper, that was presented to six people in a room designed for forty, that never reached a poll, that was never discussed, that lies in the archive like a seed in dry ground.

Her solution was simpler than either the Author's or the Architect's. It would have worked for the Blogger's use case, and for most others. It had the plain elegance of a tool. Nobody read it.

She does not write a comment. She does not post in the forums. She does not email the Author, whom she has not spoken to since the taxi ride. She closes the browser tab and returns to her codebase, which is still on a standard from years ago, because her company has not adopted anything newer, because the features in the newer standards either do not work for their use case or are not yet implemented in their compiler.

Corn falls in rows, at a single stroke, and men have always read a common death in it. "The cornfield is a battlefield." The Newcomer was mown down by the field itself, with the whole row: every first paper in that mailing that no one sighted fell with hers, in the same season, and no one counted them.

## XXXII. The Survivor

> "The moment of survival is the moment of power."
>
> -- Elias Canetti, *Crowds and Power*

The Author sits at his desk.

It has been three years since the taxi from the airport. His paper is in the archive, closed. The correction papers his R3 predicted for the Architect's feature have begun to arrive. Two stones so far, each left by a user who met the limitation in his own code. The Architect still attends meetings, still presents, still holds his place. His crystal has not dissolved. The Architect's feature is hard to take apart, so the correction papers can only lie beside it, stone by stone. "In their oldest form each separate stone stands for the man who has contributed it to the heap." A monument grows only by additions.

The Author's own paper lies among the dead, with a line in the minutes that says it was correct, which is more than most of them receive. His library still runs at his company, where the engineers who depend on it have never heard of the meeting that closed it. Elsewhere, in codebases he will never see, the unasked work around the limitation his paper named and the standard kept.

The survivor is the man who stands when others have fallen. A third correction paper arrives. The Author reads it to the end, closes it, and sits for a while with his hands flat on the desk. He does not smile. "The satisfaction which follows individual deaths is more moderate and more concealed." The engineer who submitted R0 with a design that worked and no one behind it is not the man at this desk.

He searches the archive for the Newcomer's paper and cannot find it. He does not know her name anymore. He told his co-author about her once: the taxi ride, the gray suburb, the paper she described and he praised. He heard, months after the taxi, that she had stopped coming. He knows only that she was not in the room when he stood to object, that no one remarked on it, and that the problem she tried to solve is now the subject of correction papers filed against someone else's feature. He does not know about the hotel bar, the overheard laughter, the early flight.

The Patron retired last year. Before he left he sent the Author an email: "You were right, and they said so. The system works, eventually. It just costs more than it should." The Author read it twice. The first time he felt gratitude. The second time he felt the sting. The system works, eventually, for those who survive it, and what it gives them at the end is a line in the minutes. For those who do not - for the Newcomer, for the R0 papers no one opened, for the domain experts who attended one meeting and never came back - it does not work. It does not even see them.

And the Delegate - one of eighty, ninety, a hundred. The Delegate who raised his hand because the hands before his had risen. He is pleasant and competent, and he will be at the next meeting, and the meeting after that, voting on papers he has not read, evaluating rooms instead of designs. The Delegate does not know the Author. The Delegate does not know the Newcomer. The Delegate does not know the Architect, except as a name on a slide. The Delegate is the median voter, and in a consensus body, the median voter is the sovereign, and the sovereign does not read.

Around the Author, in the open-std.org archive, the papers of every mailing settle. Most will never reach the working draft. Some were technically brilliant. Some were correct. Some were already in use. They lie in the archive as the dead lie in the earth. "The earth between them is their density and, though they lie there separately, they are felt to be close to each other." The Author walks over this ground every time he opens the mailing, stepping over the papers that did not survive, reading the numbers but not the contents, for the contents are too many and only the numbers fit in a human mind.

My book ends on the survivor, and on a warning about him: "Today either everyone will survive or no-one."

The committee produces survivors, and the survivors produce the standard. A survivor measures his triumph against the dead around him. The Architect has his triumph, and the Author's paper is one of the dead it counts. The Author survived without one. Like any outvoted man, he anticipates "future battles, and many of them; in none of them will he be killed." The users keep a different count. The committee's vote is preliminary. The public's adoption is final. A feature no one takes up joins the heap, whatever number it bears in the standard.

Crowds do not change their nature because a man has suffered in one. The crowd prefers the cheaper judgment, the weight of the people behind a paper, to the dearer one, the reading of it, and it will go on preferring it while reading costs more than counting. I did not propose a solution. I proposed attention. "If we would master power we must face command openly and boldly, and search for means to deprive it of its sting."

A sting should sit no deeper than a burr on a sleeve, something a hand can pick off. The Author's stings sit deeper than that. They are lodged where his next paper will come from.

The Author opens his laptop. He is a survivor now, and survivors write papers. "The satisfaction in survival, which is a kind of pleasure, can become a dangerous and insatiable passion." Every mailing is another occasion to outlast someone. More than a hundred papers will arrive. His will be among them. He means to win the next one.

Before he writes a word, he counts, as survivors count, the papers from the last mailing that never reached the draft. He writes the first sentence. He does not know if it will survive.

He writes it anyway.

I died in 1994, yet the crowd I fed persists.
