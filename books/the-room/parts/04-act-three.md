# Act Three

## The Castle and the Committee

*After Franz Kafka, 1922*

---

I write this in Prague, thirty-five years after the philosopher in the mountains diagnosed the values. He asked where they came from and whom they serve. His questions were correct. They were correct in 1887. They are correct now. But the philosopher stood outside the walls and threw stones at them. I do not throw stones. I walk through the corridors. I knock on doors that do not open. I wait in rooms where no one comes. I fill out forms whose purpose no one remembers. For fourteen years I wrote reports for the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute, one of them on the guards for wood-planing machines, and I know how long a file can lie on a desk before anyone opens it. I have been doing this my whole life, in offices and courtrooms and insurance companies, and when I was shown the committee, I knew it from the road.

At a distance, from the road, it shows itself much as I expected. It is neither the Florentine's principality nor the philosopher's temple, but a sprawl of rooms, a few large ones and a great many small ones standing close together - working groups, study groups, telecons, the reflector, evening sessions in rooms booked for something else - and had one not known it was a castle, one might have taken it for a little town. None of the rooms stands taller than the others; from here I cannot tell which of them governs the rest. But as I come nearer, it disappoints me. It is only conference rooms after all, gathered together out of hotels in other cities, with carpet tiles and folding tables, a projector, a coffee machine in the hall, distinguished only in that perhaps everything said in them is written down. I walk the length of it looking for the room where the unseen sit, who will use whatever is decided here, and I do not find the sign for it. (Is it on another floor? There is no one at the desk to ask.)

A castle that administers without governing. A castle whose officials are visible but unreachable. A castle whose process processes but does not ask the unseen.

The Florentine looked at it four hundred and nine years ago, and the philosopher thirty-five, and since then the forms have been reprinted many times, each time with a new number in the corner and the same questions underneath. The committees have adopted train models and three-year cadences, and proposed rules about new technologies. The corridors are the same corridors. The officials are the same officials; from the road I can see them through the windows, at their tables, working late.

## XIII. Arrival

> "It was late in the evening when K. arrived. The village lay in deep snow. Nothing could be seen of the castle hill; fog and darkness surrounded it, and not even the faintest glimmer of light hinted at the great castle."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

It was late evening when the Author arrived. The city lay under a gray sky that was neither raining nor clear, the kind of sky that makes no promises. Nothing could be seen of the meeting's venue, though it was said to be close by - a conference center attached to a hotel, somewhere past the last roundabout and behind a row of identical buildings. The Author stood at the taxi stand outside the airport for a long time, looking up at what seemed to be a void.

He had a paper. It was in his bag, on his laptop, in the committee's archive with a number that had been assigned to it automatically and that told the reader nothing - not who wrote it, not what it proposed, not whether it was good. The number was the only identity the committee had given him. He had submitted the paper three months earlier with the care of a man presenting credentials at a border he has never crossed. He had checked the formatting. He had verified the examples. He had put in the deployment numbers, the years in production, and the names of the projects whose users depended on the design and had said so in writing, because these, he thought, were credentials that no one could refuse. He believed the paper would make its own case, and he had come to see it chosen. He had read the submission guidelines twice, because the guidelines were long and contradictory in places, and he was not sure whether the contradictions were intentional or whether they had accumulated, like sediment, from years of revision by people who did not coordinate with each other.

The hotel was a building of the kind that exists in every city where committees convene, equipped with conference rooms whose beige walls and adjustable lighting suggest that anything discussed within them has already been decided elsewhere. The Author checked in. His room was on the fourth floor. The window looked out on a parking lot. He opened his laptop and searched for the meeting agenda, which he had been told would be posted before the meeting but which had not been posted when he left home, and which he now discovered had been posted while he was in the air, revised once since then, and was currently listed as "provisional."

His paper was on the agenda. It was listed for Wednesday afternoon, in a parallel session, in a room he did not recognize by name. The listing gave his paper fifteen minutes. On Monday morning, at the top of the same group's agenda, another paper in the same design space was allocated forty-five minutes. He did not know why. He looked up the other paper's number and found it in the mailing archive. It was on its sixth revision. It had co-authors from two companies whose names he recognized. It had a history he could not see - prior poll results, reflector threads, hallway conversations at meetings he had not attended. He looked for that history in the archive and on the agenda and found only the forty-five minutes.

He closed the laptop. He set the alarm for seven. He lay down on the bed without undressing, meaning only to rest a moment, and fell asleep, and the sleep was deep and troubled, and twice in the night he woke and did not know where he was.

## XIV. The Telephone

> "Out of the receiver came a humming such as K. had never heard on the telephone before."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

Monday morning. The Author went down to the lobby and found that the conference center was reached through a corridor behind the breakfast room. He followed the signs. The corridor was long, and at the end of it there was a registration desk where a woman gave him a badge with his name on it and a lanyard in a color that signified something he did not understand. Other delegates wore different-colored lanyards. He did not ask what the colors meant because everyone around him seemed to know, and asking would mark him as someone who did not belong.

The reflector was the committee's internal mailing list. He had been subscribed to it for three months, since submitting his paper, and in that time he had read perhaps two hundred messages, or rather, as it seemed to him later, he had listened to them, for what came through it was not a conversation, or not one that he could follow, but something stranger, a kind of murmuring that went on at every hour whether or not anyone answered it, and that sounded, late at night, the way the telephone at the Bridge Inn sounds to K.: "It was as if, out of the humming of countless childish voices - but this humming was no humming either, it was the singing of the farthest, the very farthest voices - as if out of this humming, in a way that was frankly impossible, a single high but strong voice were forming that beat against the ear as if demanding to go deeper than into mere paltry hearing."

In the murmur there were hundreds of voices, speaking at once, about matters the Author only partly understood. Some messages were technical - detailed analysis of specification wording, edge cases in type systems, interactions between features he had never used. Some were procedural - scheduling requests, agenda changes, announcements of polls whose purpose he could not determine from the subject line. And some were political, though they did not say so. These were the messages where someone wrote "I support the direction in P-whatever" or "I have concerns about the approach in P-other," and after them the thread went quiet, or turned. The Author had learned to recognize some of these names. They recurred. They were the names of people who chaired groups, who gave keynotes at conferences, who appeared on the programs of every meeting. Now and then, reading, it was as if out of the hundreds these few names were forming a single high voice, and then he scrolled on and it was hundreds again.

Few of the threads were ever resolved. He followed some of them for weeks. They did not stop at the beginning, where each man stated his position, or in the middle, where the messages grew long and the quotations of earlier messages grew longer than the messages themselves, but near the end, at the point where one more reply, or perhaps two, would have settled the matter; there the participants fell silent, one after another and without any sign of disagreement, as if each had been called away at the same moment to something more pressing, and the thread lay where they had left it, unfinished and, as far as he could tell, unregretted. Months later the same question would come up again under a new subject line, and the same names would take it up, courteously, as if for the first time, and follow it the same distance, and stop in the same place. It was as if settling a matter there were simply not done, though no one had ever said so, at least not in any message he had read, and he could not have said who had decided it, or whether anyone had. Perhaps, he thought, though he had no way of testing it, the matters were settled elsewhere, in rooms he had not been in, and what reached the reflector was only the murmur of their being settled.

His own paper had drawn four replies on the reflector. He had read each reply many times, the way a man reads a letter from a court he has petitioned. One reply was encouraging - a delegate he did not know had written "interesting approach, I look forward to the presentation." One asked a clarifying question. Two were skeptical. "What is the deployment experience?" one asked. "Has this been through a study group?" asked the other. They did not question the design. They questioned him.

The Author had answered both. He had sent the deployment data. He had explained that his paper had not been through a study group because he did not know which study group to contact, and because the submission guidelines did not mention study groups as a requirement for the mailing. His answers were correct. No one replied to them. In the thread, below his answers, the next message was about something else, and his answers went into the archive with the rest. He began to see, though at first he would not admit it, that the committee did not operate on what was true; it operated on what was recognized, and he was not yet recognized.

He walked the corridors of the conference center looking for Room C, which was where the working group would meet. He did not ask the way, any more than he had asked what the colors meant. He passed Room A, which was large and full, and Room B, which was medium and filling up, and found Room C at the end of a hallway, past the coffee station and around a corner. It was a small room. It had thirty chairs arranged in rows and a screen at the front and no windows. It was, he thought, a room for things that did not matter very much, the room a conference center gives to the group that asks for least. He sat down. There were nine other people in the room. One of them was the chair of the study group that had forwarded his paper - a person he had never met, whom he had only heard on a telecon. The chair was setting up a laptop at the front of the room and did not look up.

The Author opened his own laptop and waited. He was not sure what he was waiting for. The agenda said his paper was scheduled for Wednesday. Today was Monday. He was here because he had been told that "you should attend the sessions related to your paper's domain before your own presentation, so you understand the context." He did not know who had told him this. It might have been the reflector. It might have been a blog post. It might have been the kind of advice that goes around such places without anyone's name on it, the way the customs in K.'s village are known to everyone without anyone having taught them.

## XV. The Village Street That Curves Away

> "The street, the village's main street, did not lead to the castle hill; it only led close to it, and then, as if on purpose, it turned aside."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

That evening, in his room above the parking lot, the Author put every message he had received about his paper since the day he submitted it into a single file, in the order in which they had come, and read it through from the beginning.

The paper had entered the mailing. That was the first step, and he had taken it. The mailings came out nearly every month, a few dozen papers in the quiet months and on the order of a hundred around each meeting, every one of them assigned a number, posted to the archive, and listed in a table of contents. His paper was in the table of contents. It existed. It had a number. These were facts, and from them he had inferred that the next step would follow - that the paper would be discussed, and either accepted or rejected on its merits. The committee's published procedures described a sequence: mailing, study group, working group, then the wording group and plenary. Each step looked like a step toward the standard.

Two weeks after the mailing he had written to the chair of the study group whose charter seemed to cover his paper. The reply had come two weeks after that: "Thank you for your interest. The study group's agenda for the next meeting is already full. We can consider scheduling your paper for the following cycle." The following cycle was months away. He did not want the design, which he had spent two years building, to wait that long, and he had written to a second study group, whose charter seemed to cover his paper as well. It had heard him briefly, at the end of a telecon, after the papers held over from the last meeting. The chair had said: "This looks interesting. We think it should go to the working group for further discussion." That had sounded like progress. The paper had been forwarded. It was moving.

But the working group's chair had a different agenda. The working group was occupied with the established facility - the one that had been in development for three years, the one with co-authors from compiler vendors, the one already in the working draft. The Author's paper proposed another approach. "We have a full agenda for this meeting," the chair had written. "We can look at your paper after we complete the current work." After. The word contains an infinity. He read the word several times that evening. After the current work meant after the established facility was finished, which meant after it had entered the standard, which meant that his alternative would reach the working group only after the thing it was an alternative to had been adopted. And yet the provisional agenda gave him fifteen minutes on Wednesday afternoon, at a quarter to four.

Late that night, looking for the reason, he found a document called SD-4, the committee's standing document on practices and procedures, and read the part about scheduling. Subgroup chairs, it said, give priority first to papers related to the direction group's priorities, to work the committee is aiming to include in the next standard, and to work that can make plenary progress at this meeting. "After that they get to things on a best-effort basis as time allows." He looked at the agenda again. His fifteen minutes stood at the end of Wednesday. The procedure had been followed. The procedure was also the problem, he thought, but the procedure could not be the problem, because the procedure was the procedure, and to question it was to question the legitimacy of everything the procedure had produced.

He had revised the paper twice since submitting it, R1 after the reflector replies and R2 after the telecon. Each revision answered every question that had been asked of the one before, each was posted in the next mailing under the same number, and the agenda listed the newest of them after the established proposal, so that the paper, though it moved no farther from the standard, came no nearer to it either. He kept expecting that now at last the next one must be heard, and only because he expected it did he go on.

It was past midnight. He scrolled back through the archive, past his own number, past the established proposal's six revisions, each of them heard at a meeting and each given more time than the last, past the mailings of the year before and the year before that, numbers and titles and revision marks going on and on. He thought of K. on the main street, amazed at the length of the village, "which had no end, again and again the little houses and the iced-over windowpanes and snow and no one anywhere."

## XVI. The Letter

> "It was not all of one piece."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

On Tuesday morning, the Author received an email from the working group chair. It was the first word from the chair since the meeting began, and he read it with the attention K. gives to the letter from Klamm.

The email said: "Thank you for your paper P-[number]. We appreciate your contribution to the committee's work on this design space. The working group has noted your paper and we look forward to discussing it when the schedule permits. In the meantime, you may wish to attend the upcoming session on the established facility, which will provide context for your proposal's interaction with existing work."

The Author read this email three times. The first time, he read it as a welcome. "We appreciate your contribution." "We look forward to discussing it." He was addressed as a colleague whose work had been noticed. The second time, he read the other places: "when the schedule permits," although his paper was already on Wednesday's agenda; "you may wish to attend"; and "context," as if he needed context for his own proposal, as if he had written a paper about a subject he did not understand. There he was a supplicant whose work would wait. The third time, he read it for the contradictions, and they were so plain that they had to be intended. The thought that no one had decided anything about his paper at all, that the two halves of the email were simply two halves, a mad thought where such a body was concerned, barely crossed his mind.

The email was courteous and devastating, and the courtesy was the devastation. One cannot object to being thanked. One cannot appeal a compliment. He wrote a reply that objected, read it over, and deleted it.

The Author replied to the email. He thanked the chair for the response. He said he would attend the session on the established facility. He asked whether there was anything he could do to help schedule his paper for the next meeting. The chair replied: "We will let you know." This sentence, which contained no information and foreclosed no possibility and committed to nothing, was the most honest thing the committee had said to him.

He read the five words the way K. had read the three words "as you know" in Klamm's letter. We: the chair, or the working group, or whoever would be chair by then. Will: at a time not named. It might be soon. It might be never. Let: as if a permission were involved, and perhaps one was. You know: and there, he saw, the burden lay, not on the one who would let, who had only to remember, but on him, who would have to be there to be told, and reachable, and waiting, for as long as it took.

He closed the laptop and went down to the session on the established facility, and sat at the back, for context.

## XVII. The Patron

> "You don't know the castle."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

On Wednesday afternoon, between sessions, the Author sat in the hotel bar and ordered a coffee. The bar was where delegates gathered when they were not in rooms - a neutral territory, neither the committee nor the outside world, a place where the lanyards stayed on but the formal rules did not apply. Here people spoke about things they would not say on the reflector. Here the real information moved.

A woman sat down across from him. She was in her fifties, tall, with the bearing of someone who had been attending these meetings for longer than the Author had been writing code. She wore her lanyard with the ease of a person who has forgotten it is there. Her badge said a name the Author recognized from the archive - she had written papers in an adjacent domain, had chaired a study group that no longer existed, and was now a kind of permanent resident of the committee: someone who held no current office but whose presence was a fixture, the way an old inn is a fixture of a village road.

She was the Patron.

"You are new here," she said to the Author. It was not a question.

"I submitted a paper three months ago," the Author said. "It is on the agenda for this afternoon."

"I know," she said. "I read it." She paused. "It is correct."

The Author felt a surge of something - gratitude, hope, the relief of being recognized after days in which no one had looked at him twice. She had read his paper. She said it was correct. These were the first words of genuine evaluation he had received from anyone in the committee.

"But you must understand," she continued, and her voice took on the quality of the landlady explaining the castle to K., patient and unhurried, as if she had explained it before. "The process asks whether it is correct and whether the room wants it. And the room wants whatever fits what it has already decided."

"But the room hasn't decided anything about my paper," the Author said. "They haven't discussed it."

"That is the decision," the Patron said. "Not discussing it is the decision. Scheduling it for fifteen minutes on Wednesday afternoon, after the established proposal has had forty-five minutes on Monday morning, is the decision. Sending you to two study groups, when the established proposal went to one, and that one among friends, is the decision. Every procedural choice is a substantive choice, and the substantive choice was made before you arrived. You are not being refused. You are being processed."

The Author looked at his coffee. The Patron told him, without his asking, about her own paper. Years ago she had brought one against the direction of a major facility. It was correct; she had known it then and she knew it now. It was heard in a working group session that lasted twelve minutes. Three people spoke. The chair said: "We appreciate the analysis but the working group has already chosen a direction," the way one acknowledges a remark at a dinner party and then changes the subject. There was no poll. The paper was never rejected. It was never scheduled again, and she did not know why. She had never submitted another paper. But she had kept coming, to every meeting, to every session in her domain, and she spoke from the floor when she had something to say, which was often. She had, he thought, the knowledge of an insider and the perspective of an outsider - the worst combination for happiness, the best for understanding.

She had kept a few things from that week: her badge; the page of the agenda with her twelve minutes on it; and an email she had asked the chair for afterward, when she wanted the decision in writing, which said the same thing in one line. "No one here gives you anything," she said. "But if you see something lying there that suits you, you can ask for it."

"What should I do?" the Author asked.

The Patron considered this. "Present your paper," she said. "Present it well. Answer the questions. Be gracious when you lose the poll. Then come back in six months and present it again. And again. And again. Eventually, the room will have heard your name enough times that it will stop hearing it as a stranger's name. Eventually, someone from the established coalition will read your paper - not because they want to, but because someone will have told them it keeps coming back, and things that keep coming back make people uneasy. Eventually, the established facility will ship and the users will discover its limitations and the correction papers will arrive, and then - maybe then - the room will remember that you proposed an alternative."

"How long?" the Author asked.

"Years," the Patron said. She finished her coffee. "I have to go back to the session. Your paper is at 3:45. The room will be thin by then. Do not be discouraged by the number of people. Be discouraged by nothing. Discouragement is the only thing a paper cannot survive. If you are discouraged, you leave, and if you leave, your paper dies. That is the only death the committee recognizes - the death of the author's will."

She stood. She picked up her bag. She looked at him with an expression that was either sympathy or recognition or simply the way one looks at someone who is about to learn something the hard way. Then she walked back toward the conference rooms, and the Author was alone with his coffee and the fifteen minutes he had been given, which were now two hours away and which felt simultaneously like an eternity and like nothing at all.

## XVIII. The Village Mayor

> "In an authority as large as the count's, it can happen now and then that one department orders this and another that."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

That morning, at breakfast, before the first session, the Author read the rest of SD-4. He had found it on Monday night and had read only the part about scheduling. Now he read it from the beginning, the way a defendant reads the legal code of the jurisdiction where he has been charged, searching for the rule that governs his case.

It began with its purpose and the code of conduct, and then it turned to consensus. It reprinted a definition from an ISO guide: "General agreement, characterized by the absence of sustained opposition to substantial issues by any important part of the concerned interests." He read it several times. It did not say how agreement was counted, and he read on, looking for the place where it did.

Then it told him what a good paper does. "Be example-based." "Be principle-based." "Show design alternatives considered." His paper did all three. He checked, at the table, while his coffee went cold, and it did.

Then it came to polls. A proposal, it said, normally advances if there are more than twice as many in favor as against. And then: "Not everyone in the room needs to vote (even Neutral)." People who had not read the paper, it said, or had not been paying close attention to the discussion, typically did not vote on that poll. The Author took a napkin and worked it out. If thirty people were in the room and twenty of them had not read his paper, the twenty would not raise their hands, and they would be counted neither for him nor against him nor as neutral; they would not be counted. The ten who had read it would decide. If seven of them were for and three against, it was more than twice as many, and the paper would normally advance, and the twenty would have had no part in it, though they had sat in the room the whole time. He had expected, without knowing why, that their silence would be counted as consent. This was stranger, and it was true.

But in the end a design went forward when it reached a general consensus of the subgroup, "as determined by the subgroup chair," and the chair made that determination after the hands were down.

Somewhere in the reading he thought of the mayor, a friendly, heavy man who received K. in bed with gout, while the mayor's wife and K.'s assistants hunted through the cupboard for the decree and the files rolled out across the floor, two big bundles of them tied up round, the way one ties firewood. The filing system had overwhelmed the files. The Author had met something worse: a process document.

From his bed the mayor had told K.: "unfortunately, we need no land surveyor." The village had said so long ago. It had answered the decree with its thanks, and the answer had gone by mistake to another department, B, instead of back to the one that had asked, and the summons had gone out anyway, and it was never withdrawn. For a moment he wondered whether the established facility solved a problem the users actually had, and whether his own paper did; but he was looking for the rule that would let his paper win, and he read on.

Further on, in the section on Technical Specifications, he came to a sentence about the standard itself: "Once a feature is in an IS, it is essentially cast in stone and much more difficult to update with breaking changes."

## XIX. Waiting for Klamm

> "He had fought for this freedom as hardly anyone else could have, and no one might touch him or drive him away."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

Wednesday afternoon. 3:15 pm.

The Author sat in Room C and waited for his slot. He had been in this room since the morning session began, apart from the hour in the bar. He had watched four presentations. He had listened to three discussions. He had seen two polls taken, each in the same order: the Chair read the question, the hands went up, and at the table at the side of the room the woman taking the minutes, who had been typing all day, typed the numbers. He had taken notes. He had drunk three cups of coffee from the machine in the hallway, each worse than the last. Now it was 3:15 and his presentation was at 3:45, and the room had forty people in it, down from forty-five at the morning peak. It had thirty chairs. The rest stood along the back wall, and no one had asked for a larger room.

He thought of K. waiting beside Klamm's sleigh in the courtyard of the Herrenhof. K. had placed himself there on purpose, hoping that Klamm would come out of the inn and have to acknowledge him. He waited a long time. The night was cold. The snow was deep. Klamm did not come. In the end the coachman unharnessed the horses and put the sleigh away, and K. was left alone in the courtyard.

The Chair was already here - he had been at the front of the room all day, managing the agenda, introducing speakers, framing polls. He was in plain view, the way Klamm is in plain view through the peephole at the Herrenhof, sitting at his desk, entirely present and entirely out of reach. The Author could see him. The Author could hear him. In thirty minutes the Author would present his paper to a room the Chair managed, and the Chair would treat him with the same even-handed courtesy with which he treated everyone. And the courtesy would be the distance.

At 3:30 the chair of the study group that had forwarded the Author's paper stood up and left the room, for another session, the Author supposed. At 3:35 four more delegates left. At 3:40 the Chair looked at the agenda and said: "We have one more paper to discuss before we break for the day. P-[number], a new proposal in this design space." He said "new" in a tone that was neither positive nor negative but that marked the paper as something the process had not yet seen, the way the established facility's name, all day, had been spoken as the name of something that had been here before.

The Author stood up. He walked to the front of the room. He connected his laptop to the projector. The screen showed the first slide of his presentation. The room was not thin. Thirty-three people looked at him from the rows and from along the back wall, and the Chair looked at him from the table at the side, where the woman taking the minutes sat with her laptop open. Some of them he recognized from the reflector. Most he did not. He did not know which of them had read his paper. He did not know which of them had formed an opinion. He did not know which of them were allies of the established facility and which were genuinely undecided and which were simply present because they had not yet left the room.

He began to present. His voice was steady. His examples were clear. He had practiced this presentation in his apartment, speaking to an empty room, adjusting his timing, making sure the technical points landed in the right order. The presentation was good. He knew it was good. He had spent two years building this design and three months writing this paper and two weeks preparing this talk, and the talk was all of it pressed into fifteen minutes, with the deployment numbers on the fourth slide and the users who depended on the design on the fifth, aimed at a room of thirty-five people who held the future of his proposal in their hands.

He finished. There was a pause. Then the Chair said: "Thank you. Are there any questions?"

One person raised a hand. It was a delegate the Author did not recognize - a man from a compiler vendor, whose lanyard, the Author noticed, was not the color of his own. He looked down at his laptop, scrolled for a moment, and asked, in the tone of one establishing a fact: "Which study group forwarded this?"

Before the Author answered, he saw the screen of the woman taking the minutes. His paper's number was already there, typed under a heading that was not his: the name of the established facility, and beneath it, "Alternative approach." The Author answered. His answer was correct. She typed it in, a line under the question, under the same heading.

There were no other questions.

The Chair said: "Thank you for the presentation. Given the time, I suggest we take a sense of the room." The Author knew the device; he had read it that morning, in the paragraph that tells anyone who thinks a new proposal should not take up extended time to say so to the chair, privately and in advance. "How many people would like to see further work in this direction?" the Chair asked. Fourteen hands rose. "And how many feel we should focus our efforts on the established approach?" Eighteen hands rose. One man in the back row did not raise his hand either time; he was reading something on his laptop.

The Author stood at the front and looked at the hands, trying to tell which of them were his. The fourteen, it seemed to him, were mostly on the left and the eighteen mostly on the right, though they were mixed; he looked for a line between them, a party he might speak to before the next meeting and win over, and found none. He counted: fourteen, and eighteen, and the man in the back row, and the Chair and the woman taking the minutes, who had raised no hand either, thirty-five, the whole of the room.

"Thank you," the Chair said. "It seems like the room is interested in further exploration but the priority remains the current work. We can revisit this in a future meeting." He closed the session for the day. At the side table, the woman taking the minutes typed the two numbers, fourteen and eighteen, and the Chair's sentence beneath them, and saved the page.

The room emptied. The Author went back to his seat and sat down. He opened his laptop. He did not look at the screen. He was thinking about the minutes. They would not say which hands had been whose, or why, or what the man in the back row had been reading. The two numbers and the sentence would be at the next meeting before he was, and at the one after that.

The Author had presented. No one had stopped him. No one had rejected his paper. He was free to wait. He stayed in the empty room a while longer, the only one holding the ground, and there was nothing more senseless, nothing more desperate than this freedom, this waiting, this invulnerability.

## XX. The Assistants

> "'Good,' said one. But the other broke in: 'You say good, and you know it is impossible.'"
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

K. is given two assistants named Artur and Jeremias. He has never seen them before. They claim to be his "old assistants," but they know nothing about land surveying. They have no instruments. They are cheerful, clumsy, and identical - K. cannot tell them apart and decides to call them both by one name and treat them as a single person. "That has the great disadvantage for me, to be sure," he tells them, "that I cannot use you for separate work, but in return the advantage that for everything I give you to do, you bear the responsibility together and undivided." They clown about. They accomplish nothing. They are simultaneously useless and inescapable.

His paper had two study groups.

Their charters overlapped. One had been formed for the domain his paper addressed and the other for the kind of facility it proposed, and his paper was both. Both met by telecon, at hours chosen for other time zones; the same four or five people spoke on both calls; both chairs signed their emails in the same courteous way, with the same closing line. He could not tell them apart. He had looked up what a study group was for, and SD-4 said that one is formed when, among other things, "there is not yet a unified initial proposal to bring to the design subgroup (typically because proposals are immature, incomplete, and/or competing)." His was competing. In the end he did what K. does with Artur and Jeremias, whom he cannot tell apart either: he treated them as one and sent everything to both.

One evening, because he could not sleep, he counted the committee's offices. Its page listed twenty-three study groups, of which eight were marked as dormant, and the fifteen that were awake had each a chair and, most of them, a vice-chair; above them stood the four main groups with their chairs and vice-chairs, and the advisory groups, and the convener and the vice-conveners and the secretary, and the editors, and an officer for the meeting rooms and another for the mailing lists. He made it fifty-seven titles. Then he counted the names, which took longer, because many of them came round more than once, chairing one group and sitting as vice-chair of another, two of them three times, and he made it forty-five. On Wednesday afternoon there had been thirty-five people in Room C. He did the arithmetic twice, because the first time he did not believe it, and the second time he believed it no more than the first: there were more offices in the committee than there had been people in the room where his paper was heard.

Along with them came requirements, which attached themselves to his paper the way Artur and Jeremias attach themselves to K. - suddenly, without his consent, and with no apparent connection to the work he was trying to do. The requirements were: a review by the first study group, a review by the second, a direction poll in the working group, and a design review. Each requirement had its own timeline, its own gatekeepers, its own criteria for completion. A study group could advise a paper and send it on, or not send it on yet. It could not change the standard. It could change the date. Each depended on the one before it, like the doorkeepers in the legend of the Law, who stand from hall to hall, each more powerful than the last, and each, as far as he could see, standing before the next doorkeeper rather than before the Law.

Later, after the meeting, when he wrote to both together to ask which of them he should follow, the answers came the same afternoon, one under the other in his inbox: "This looks interesting, take it to the working group," and "Come back next cycle."

K. asks his assistants whether they understand anything about surveying. "No," they say. "But if you are my old assistants, you must understand it," says K. They are silent. K. gives up. He will drag them along, because the castle has assigned them. They will get in the way. They will cause problems. They will, at one point, drive away the woman he loves. But they cannot be fired, because they are not his employees. They are the castle's. The Author's assistants understood a great deal. They found edge cases he had missed. Each of them, it is true, found chiefly the kind of edge case it had been formed to find, and found it everywhere: the group formed for the domain found that his design did not yet cover the whole of the domain, and the group formed for the kind of facility found that it was not yet general enough to be a facility, so that each revision he made for the one gave the other something new to find. None of them could decide whether his paper was correct.

He could not fire them. He could not decide that the first study group's review was unnecessary and skip it. He could not decide that the direction poll was a formality and go straight to the design review. He could not decide that the process was not serving him and opt out; the study groups belonged to the committee, not to him. They had existed before he arrived and would exist after he left, and it seemed to him that his paper mattered to them about as much as K.'s surveying matters to Artur and Jeremias, which is to say that they attended to it courteously, and with the serene indifference of creatures whose existence does not depend on the approval of the person they are assigned to.

It was only later, looking through the archive for something else, that he found the established proposal's own passage through the study groups. The assistants had not been clumsy with it. They had been quick. It had gone to one study group only, the one whose chair worked at the same company as two of its authors; it had been heard there on a morning when, according to the minutes, most of the people in the room worked for the same three companies; it had been forwarded the same afternoon, by a poll the minutes called strong; and it had reached the working group within the week, as though the corridor that ran so long for him ran short for others, or as though there were another corridor. He concluded nothing from this. The minutes recorded who was present and how they voted, and nothing about what anyone intended. He only noticed that the assistants who could decide nothing for him had decided everything for the established proposal, quickly and courteously, as if their existence depended, after all, on someone's approval, only not on his.

One year into his campaign, the Author understood what K. understands about his assistants only late, when Jeremias repeats the orders Galater gave them before they came: "He has just come to the village, and at once that is a great event for him, whereas in reality it is nothing at all. That is what you are to teach him." He knew of no such orders. His assistants were the castle's immune system, disguised as the castle's hospitality. They held up his paper, not because it was wrong, but because they did not recognize it; they hurried the other through, not because it was right, but because they did.

He sent his next revision to both.

## XXI. The Newcomer's Refusal

> "Nothing happened. No summons, no message, no report, no visit, nothing."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Castle*

The Newcomer was at the meeting. The Author saw her on Thursday evening, at the conference dinner.

She was younger than the Patron and quieter. She sat at the end of a long table and ate without speaking to anyone around her. Her badge showed a name the Author did not recognize and a company he had never heard of. She cut her food into small pieces and listened, or seemed to listen, to the talk at the far end of the table, and once she looked at her phone and put it face down beside her plate.

The long table talked over the week's polls. Someone mentioned the Author's paper: the room, he said, had shown interest in further exploration. Someone else said that was encouraging. The Author said that it was, and thanked them. At the end of the table the Newcomer put down her fork. "They say that to everyone at the end of the day," she said, to no one in particular, "and by Friday they have forgotten saying it, and next time you will believe them again." Then she went back to her plate. The Author smiled, as at a joke, and turned back to the man who had called it encouraging.

The Patron, who was sitting near the Author, had seen him look. "That is the Newcomer - the one who refused," she said quietly.

He thought of Amalia, in K.'s village, whose sister Olga tells K. her story: how a letter came from the castle, and Amalia read it, and tore it up, and threw the pieces in the face of the messenger waiting outside, and shut the window. The neighbors stopped coming to the house; the father's trade fell away; and when the father went to the castle offices to beg for pardon, the officials asked him what there was to forgive, since no complaint had been lodged, at least none stood in the records yet. "Could he perhaps name an official order that had been issued against him? That the father could not."

Two years earlier, the Patron said, the Newcomer had presented a paper in the working group about another major facility that was already in the working draft. Where the Author had proposed an alternative, she had argued that the facility solved no problem the users actually had. Its co-authors had put thousands of hours into it. The Chair had brought it through a dozen sessions. After her presentation the room was quiet. Then the Chair said: "Thank you. Are there any other questions?" There were no other questions. There was no poll and no reprimand. The discussion moved on, and the minutes gave her paper's number and a line. It was as if she had not spoken.

At the next meeting, she was not scheduled. She emailed the chair. The chair replied: "The working group's agenda is focused on completing the current work. We can consider your paper in a future cycle." The future cycle did not come. Nothing else happened, the Patron said. Nothing on record named a charge. Her paper was still in the archive; her membership was still active; she could still attend meetings, submit papers, and speak from the floor, and if she had asked for a remedy, she could not have named the poll she was appealing. The delegates who had built the facility stopped engaging. They stopped answering her reflector posts. They stopped sitting next to her at dinner. None of it was organized. It did not need to be. Later others said what she had said, but by then no one connected it with her.

After that, the Patron said, she came rarely. She was here this week for another group's work, and she would not present.

The Author looked at her for a long time. She had finished eating. She folded her napkin and laid it beside her plate, and looked toward the window, where the lights of the parking lot were.

The Author did not speak to her. He did not know what to say.

She stood, took her coat from the back of her chair, and left. The Author never saw her again.

## XXII. The Door That Was Meant for Him

> "No one else could be let in here, for this entrance was meant only for you. Now I am going to shut it."
>
> -- Franz Kafka, *The Trial*

I did not finish my novel. I did not need to. It breaks off in the middle of a sentence - "but what she said" - and I wrote to Max that I had to leave the castle story lying forever. I asked Max to burn it, with everything else, completely and unread, knowing he would not. But I told him how it was to end. K. was to die worn out by his struggle. Round his deathbed the villagers would gather, and word would come from the castle that K. had no legal claim to live in the village, but that, in view of certain incidental circumstances, he was nevertheless permitted to live and work there. The permission would arrive at the moment when it could no longer be used.

The Author's paper was eventually discussed. Not at the next meeting, or the one after that, but eighteen months after he first submitted it. For eighteen months he had submitted revisions, attended sessions, answered questions, and waited for the chairs to let him know. By then the established facility had shipped. It was in the standard, which had an ISO number. It was permanent. And it had begun to gather the correction papers the Patron had foretold at the bar, which the national body reviewers had flagged in their comments, and which the users had found the first time they tried to use the feature for the case it was supposed to serve.

The Author's paper, when it was finally discussed, was discussed in a different room, with a different chair, in a study group that had been formed to deal with the problems of the shipped facility. The room had twelve people in it. The discussion lasted forty-five minutes. There was a poll. The poll was unanimous in favor of "exploring this direction." The Author had waited eighteen months for a poll that took thirty seconds and that was unanimous.

Afterward he tried to find out how it had come to be scheduled. The chair did not know; the paper had been on the list he was given when the group was formed. The working group's chair, when the Author wrote to him, thought the request had come from one of the two study groups; each of their chairs thought it had come from the other. Each answer was courteous, and each was the last on its thread. It was as if the room, after eighteen months, could no longer bear the paper that kept coming back, and had settled it by itself, quite correctly but arbitrarily, and all at once.

That evening he searched eighteen months of email for a refusal and did not find one. He found "the following cycle," and "after we complete the current work," and "when the schedule permits," and "a future meeting," and "We will let you know," four times, from three different chairs.

In my other novel a chaplain tells K. a legend. Before the Law stands a doorkeeper. A man from the country comes and asks to be let in, and the doorkeeper says that he cannot grant him entry now. The man sits down beside the entrance and waits, for days and years. Whenever he asks, the doorkeeper tells him again that he cannot let him in yet: not yet. He grows old. At last his eyesight grows weak, and in the darkness he makes out a radiance that breaks inextinguishably from the door of the Law. When he is dying, he asks why, in all these years, no one but he has asked to be let in, and the doorkeeper, to reach his failing hearing, roars at him that no one else could be let in here, for this entrance was meant only for him: "Now I am going to shut it."

Afterward the chaplain and K. argue over what the legend means, and I did not settle it for them. When K. turns to go, the chaplain's last words to him are these: "The court wants nothing from you. It receives you when you come, and it releases you when you go."

The Author sits at his desk, eighteen months after his first submission, with a unanimous poll in his favor and a standard that contains the thing his paper would have prevented. The correction papers say what that thing does not do, and so do the blog posts, and somewhere the unseen, who use the language every day, are already working around it. He has won. The permission has arrived. He has not died.

He opens his laptop and writes the first sentence of his next paper. Outside, it has begun to snow. He writes the second.

I died in 1924, yet the castle I never entered persists.
