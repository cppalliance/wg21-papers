# Act One

## The Prince and the Chair

*After Niccolo Machiavelli, 1513*

---

I, Niccolo, son of Bernardo, formerly Secretary to the Second Chancery of the Republic of Florence, write this from my farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina, where fortune and the Medici once sent me, and where I spend my evenings in the company of the ancients. I have been shown an institution that governs a language written by uncounted souls across the world, that convenes its delegates three times a year in renowned cities, in rooms of no distinction, and that produces a specification revised every three years with the solemnity of a papal bull and the practical consequence of a tax code. I say, as I said at the opening of my little work, that all states are either republics or principalities, that principalities are either hereditary or new, and that they are acquired either with the arms of others or with one's own. This one calls itself a republic of merit and consensus, and it is held as a principality: a hereditary Chair, an Architect with arms of his own, and a new prince, the Author, with nothing but a paper. Under either name the question is who holds the state, and the uncounted who use the language hold nothing in it.

I recognize in it every principality I have known: the hereditary prince who keeps his state by keeping his ancestors' order, the new prince undone by a fortress he cannot see, the armed prophet who prevails and the unarmed prophet who is burned, the citizen army and the hired one, and the fortress that guards the prince against his challengers and does nothing to keep his people. I have seen all of this before. I saw it in the Romagna under Borgia. I saw it in Florence under the Medici. I saw it in the Vatican under Julius. The costumes change. The principality does not.

I have not adorned what follows, as I did not adorn the little book I wrote for Lorenzo, and Lorenzo never read it. The committee, like Lorenzo, will not read this either. But I write it for the reason I wrote that little work in 1513: it has seemed more fitting to me to go after the effectual truth of the thing than after the imagination of it, for the distance between how a room ought to choose and how it does choose is the most dangerous ground in political life, and the man who crosses it without a map is ruined on it.

---

## I. The Dedication

> "And although I judge this work unworthy of your presence, I trust greatly that your humanity will accept it, considering that I could make you no greater gift than the means to understand, in the shortest time, all that I have come to know and understand over so many years and through so many hardships and dangers of my own."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

I wrote my little work in exile. For fourteen years I had been secretary of the Second Chancery and of the Ten, a secretary sent on missions: I was sent to the Duke, I was sent to Pope Julius when he set out against Bologna, and I drafted the orders for the citizen militia that at last took Pisa. Then the Medici returned, and I was dismissed, imprisoned, tortured, and released, and fortune sent me to a small farm where I had nothing to do but think about states. In the evenings I put on courtly robes and sat down at my desk and wrote on how a state is won and how it is held. I dedicated it to Lorenzo, a young Medici prince.

In the dedication I wrote: "I have not adorned this work, nor filled it with ample periods, or with swollen and magnificent words."

The Author writes his paper in the same spirit.

He is a mid-career engineer at a mid-size company. He has spent two years building a library that solves a problem he encounters every day - a problem that tens of thousands of other engineers encounter every day, though they have learned to live with the workarounds the way one learns to live with a bad knee. His solution is clean. It compiles. It has run in production at his company for eighteen months, and the engineers there depend on it. He has written tests. He has benchmarked it. He has documented the tradeoffs, because there are tradeoffs - there always are - and he believes that honest disclosure of tradeoffs is what separates a serious proposal from a sales pitch.

He writes the paper over three weekends. He formats it according to the committee's template. He checks the numbering. He reads it aloud to himself to hear how it sounds. He submits it to the mailing with the feeling I described in a letter to my friend Francesco Vettori: "When evening comes, I return home and go into my study; and on the threshold I strip off the day's clothes, full of mud and mire, and put on royal and courtly robes."

The paper is his courtly robes. He addresses it to an institution he respects and hopes will respect him in return. He does not know the institution the way I knew the Medici; he knows it from the outside - from conference talks, from blog posts, from the public record of papers and polls and trip reports. He has never attended a meeting. He has never met the people who will judge his work. He is addressing strangers, and the strangest thing about it is that he believes the work will speak for itself.

I believed the same thing. I was wrong. Fifteen years at the study of the art of the state, pressed into a little book, counted for nothing with Lorenzo. He had power already. He did not need a secretary's advice on how to get it.

The Author's paper will arrive in the mailing before the meeting, one of some hundred and twenty papers. It will be assigned a number. It will sit in a table of contents alongside proposals from delegates who have been attending meetings for fifteen years, who have co-authors from compiler vendors, who have prior poll histories and reflector threads and hallway friendships that the Author cannot see and does not know exist. His paper will be more than correct. It will be proven. And the institution to which it is addressed will treat it the way Lorenzo treated my gift: it will not open it.

## II. The New Prince

> "In the antiquity and continuance of a dominion, the memories and the causes of innovation are spent; for one change always leaves the toothing for building the next."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

I divide princes into two kinds: those who inherit their states and those who must win them. The distinction is everything. For the hereditary prince the work is light: his subjects are accustomed to his blood, and he keeps his state almost without effort. The new prince lives in another world. He must take a state that belongs to someone else, or make one where none existed, and then hold it against everyone who preferred the old arrangement.

The Author is a new prince. He has no territory in the committee: no prior papers, no study group history, no working group presence. His paper proposes a new approach to a problem that the committee has been addressing through an established facility for several years. The established facility has a champion, the man I will call the Architect, and it has arms of its own: co-authors from two compiler vendors, favorable polls in prior meetings, an earlier version in the working draft. The Author's paper arrives as an alternative. It does not merely add to the existing design. It replaces it. It says, in effect: the approach you have been building for three years has a structural limitation, and here is a different approach that does not have it.

This is the sixth chapter of my little work: "There is nothing more difficult to handle, nor more doubtful of success, nor more dangerous to manage, than to take the lead in bringing in new orders. For the introducer has for enemies all those who do well under the old orders, and lukewarm defenders in all those who would do well under the new; and this lukewarmness arises partly from fear of the adversaries, who have the laws on their side, and partly from the incredulity of men, who do not truly believe in new things until they have seen a firm experience of them."

The Author does not yet know that this passage describes him. He believes he is entering a technical discussion. He is entering a contest for territory.

Consider, then, who does well under the old orders. The Architect, whose name is on the facility; his co-authors; the compiler vendor delegates who have begun to implement it; the working group members who voted for it in prior polls; the Chair, whose agenda has given it a place for three years. These are the Author's enemies. Not one of them is corrupt. Not one is venal. It is enough that they do well under the old orders. If the new order comes in, their years of work are spent on a design the room has set aside, their earlier polls stand in the record as errors, and their implementations become waste. No one in their place welcomes the challenger, however well his library has served its users.

And those who would do well under the new orders? They are the users who would gain from the Author's design, and they are not in the room. They are the uncounted, the developers who use the language daily, most of whom have never heard of their national body. They cannot vote. They cannot speak from the floor. They cannot raise a hand in a straw poll. I called such men "lukewarm defenders," and these are less than lukewarm, for they are absent from the place where the order is chosen.

From this I drew a conclusion that has scandalized readers for five centuries: "all the armed prophets conquered and the unarmed were ruined."

The Author has no arms, only a library that engineers depend on and an implementation that works. These are his virtu, the strength that takes and holds a state, the way a bow has virtu in how far it sends the arrow. His virtu is real. But virtu without arms is Savonarola preaching to the masses: believed today, doubted tomorrow, burned at the stake by the end of the week.

I heard the friar preach. I watched a man of considerable intelligence build a following on nothing but the force of his words, and then watched it fall the moment words stopped being enough: "he was ruined in his new orders as soon as the multitude began not to believe him, and he had no means of holding firm those who had believed, nor of making the unbelievers believe." The Author's paper will go the same road. He will present. Some in the room will be persuaded. But when the Chair calls the poll and the established proposal is the other option, and the Architect's co-authors are in the front rows, and the first questioner is a peer of the Architect who sets the Author's paper beside what already exists, then the Author will learn what the friar learned: that conviction without arms is a sermon, and a sermon holds no ground.

Those who become princes by their own virtu "acquire the principality with difficulty, but hold it with ease." The difficulty is in the beginning. The Author's beginning is now: this first meeting, this first presentation, this first encounter with a room that does not know him, has no reason to trust him, and has every reason of habit to prefer the thing it already has. The firm experience that men require before they believe in new things, the Author already has, in eighteen months of production. Yet the room credits only the experience born inside it, its own polls and its own revisions. If he survives this, the rest will follow. But most new princes do not survive the beginning.

## III. The Hereditary Prince

> "The natural prince has fewer causes and less necessity to offend, and so it follows that he is more loved; and unless extraordinary vices make him hated, it is reasonable that his own should wish him well by nature."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

The Chair has held his position for six years. The convener appointed him after working with him in two study groups, and chose him for the qualities the institution values above all others: steadiness, fluency in procedure, and an appearance of neutrality so thorough that no one in the room can tell it from the real thing. He has chaired forty sessions across a dozen meetings. He knows the history of every paper in his domain. He remembers every prior poll. He can tell you, from memory, the count on a direction poll from three years ago and the name of the delegate who asked the question that changed the room's mind.

This is the hereditary prince of my second chapter, for whom "it is enough not to pass beyond the order of his ancestors, and then to temporize with events as they come, so that if such a prince is of ordinary industry he will always keep his state, unless some extraordinary and excessive force deprives him of it."

The Chair would never call himself a prince; he calls himself a facilitator, a man who runs the room so that others can do the intellectual work. He controls the agenda, but he would say he merely organizes it. He frames the poll questions, but he would say he merely articulates what the room has discussed. He summarizes the discussion before the vote, but he would say he merely reflects what he heard. Every verb in his self-description is passive. He facilitates. He organizes. He articulates. He reflects. The active verbs - controls, frames, determines, decides - belong to a description he would not recognize as his own.

And yet.

The Chair decides which papers reach the agenda. A paper that is not scheduled is a paper that does not exist, as far as the working group is concerned. It may be in the mailing. It may have a number. But if the Chair does not put it on the agenda, it will not be presented, it will not be discussed, and it will not be polled. No one disputes that someone must manage the agenda, for there are more papers than hours, and the chairs before him managed it in the same way. So the selection looks like logistics, as the toll at a city gate looks like part of the road to carters whose grandfathers paid it.

The Chair decides the order of presentations. A paper presented first sets the terms of the discussion, and a paper presented second must answer them; the first is the incumbent and the second is the challenger. The Chair would say that the order follows revision number, the more mature paper first, which is only fair. And it is fair, as precedence at a court is fair, where the old families walk first because they have always walked first. But the man who walks first is the man the court sees first. The order is his ancestors' order. It is the weather. You do not question the weather.

The Chair frames the poll question. Of all his acts this one decides the most, and he performs it in a single sentence spoken to the room in the two minutes before it raises its hands. "Who is in favor of advancing the approach in P-first?" followed by "Who is in favor of advancing the approach in P-second?" is a different question from "Which approach should the working group adopt for this design space?" The first form treats each paper as independent and lets both advance if the room favors each; the second forces a choice, so that one paper wins and the other is beaten. The Chair chooses the form by his judgment of what the room needs. That judgment is not neutral: it was formed in six years at the front of this room, so that it belongs less to the man than to the seat, and the seat has interests of its own even where the man has none.

Of this kind of power I wrote: "Whenever neither property nor honor is taken from men in general, they live content, and one has only to contend with the ambition of a few, which can be checked in many ways and with ease." The Chair takes neither property nor honor from anyone; papers are heard in their turn, and the ambition of a few, a newcomer with a paper among them, is checked with ease. So his power asks no action of him, only that the existing order continue. Every meeting that passes without a challenge strengthens it. Every paper that passes through his agenda without incident is proof that the agenda works. Every poll with a clear result confirms the framing. The hereditary prince governs by custom, and custom, in a committee that meets three times a year with the same people in the same rooms discussing the same topics, is the strongest force there is.

The Chair does his job well. He is organized. He is prepared. He is courteous to presenters, patient with questioners, and fair in his allocation of time. He serves the process faithfully. He has given up weekends and holidays and time with his family to attend meetings, read papers, and manage the working group's business. He is, in every ordinary sense, a good steward of the institution.

But I never judged princes by whether they were good men. I judged them by effects. In the Chair's seat the effects come from advantages laid down so gradually, and so bound up with work that someone must do, that no one in the room can tell the power from the procedure, as no one can say which course of stones in an old wall holds up the rest. The agenda is the power. The framing is the power. The order of presentations is the power. The summary before the vote is the power. Each is also a task someone must perform, and the Chair performs them all, and so he is the prince of this room. The Author, who will arrive on Monday with his tested library, is about to enter the fortress of a hereditary prince who has had six years to make its walls invisible.

I was myself a man of procedure, a secretary who kept a chancery's papers, ordered a militia, and wrote dispatches for fourteen years, and I know the Chair's seat as the painter of landscapes knows a mountain, by standing low on the plain to look at it. I know also how such princes lose their states: "having never thought in quiet times that things could change." The Chair has governed in quiet times for six years. The Author's paper is the first sign of war.

## IV. On Armies

> "Bold among friends, cowards among enemies, they have no fear of God and keep no faith with men."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*, on mercenary armies

Monday morning. The Author enters the room.

He sits in the back row because he does not know where else to sit. The only face he knows in the building belongs to a woman he met in the registration line on Sunday night, another first-timer, the Newcomer, whose own paper goes before a study group on Wednesday; they compared slides on their phones while the line moved, and this morning she is in another room. The front rows are occupied by people who know each other. They arrived together from the hotel lobby, coffee cups in hand, and sat down in a formation that looks random and is not. The Architect is in the second row, flanked by two co-authors from compiler vendors. A delegate from a platform company sits behind them. They do not look at each other often, but they hold their places as a company of soldiers holds its places on the march, each man in his file without being told.

This is the Architect's army.

I gave three chapters of my little work to arms, because I watched Florence hire captains for her wars and send ten thousand Frenchmen to take Pisa for her, and I learned that nothing matters more to a state than whose arms defend it. The Architect's arms are not hired. His co-authors from the compiler vendors have begun to implement his design, and their companies' release schedules now include his feature. The delegates who voted for it before have their judgment in the record beside his. "One's own arms," I wrote, "are those composed of subjects, or citizens, or your own dependents." These are his own arms, his citizens, and they fight for the territory because it is theirs.

The Author has no army at all.

His paper has no co-authors. His company is too small to send more than one delegate. He has no allies on the reflector: a few replies, and not one friend among them. He has no prior poll history. The Chair knows him only as a line on the agenda he drew up three weeks ago. His own arms, if he had any here, would be his users, the engineers who run his library in production. They are the citizens of his design, and not one of them can enter this room. So in the room he is, in every sense I intended, unarmed, and of such princes I wrote: "I conclude, therefore, that without arms of its own no principality is secure; rather it is wholly bound to fortune, having no virtu to defend it in adversity."

Virtu without arms is a speech. Arms without virtu is a mob. You need both. The Architect has both: his design has real merit, and the men around him turn merit into raised hands. The Author has only his virtu. He is the unarmed prophet.

Beside the armed prophets I set a lesser example, Hiero, who rose from a private man to prince of Syracuse by making arms of his own: "He did away with the old militia and ordered a new one; he left his old friendships and took new ones; and once he had friends and soldiers who were his own, he could build any edifice on that foundation." The Author does not know this story, but he will live it. Over the next six months, after his first defeat, he will do what Hiero did: find a co-author, build a second implementation, leave his solitude, and gather the beginnings of arms of his own. But that is the future. Today he is in the back row of a room where the Architect's forces are already in their places, and the Chair's agenda, drawn up three weeks ago, has laid out the field so that it favors the incumbent.

The Chair calls the people in his room a working group, never an army. But of the great men of a principality I wrote that "either they govern their conduct so as to bind themselves wholly to your fortune, or they do not. Those who bind themselves, and are not rapacious, are to be honored and loved." The Architect has bound himself wholly to the Chair's fortune. His paper is the working group's flagship; its success is counted as the Chair's success, and each step it takes through the process is a mark in favor of six years of the Chair's stewardship. The Chair did not choose this arrangement, but he benefits from it, and the benefit shapes his decisions in ways he cannot see because the decisions look like procedure.

The Author, in the back row, watches the Architect's presentation. He watches the co-authors nod. He watches the Chair manage the room with the smooth efficiency of a man who has done this forty times. And he begins to learn, without yet having words for it, what fifteen years at the study of the art of the state taught me: that a man can be right about the substance and still lose the contest, for arms decide the contest, and the substance decides only what the victory is worth. Around him sit the Delegates, twenty-six of them, most of whom have read neither his paper nor the Architect's, and those who stay will raise their hands this afternoon on what they hear in this room, not on what they read in the mailing.

## V. On Cruelty and Compassion

> "Every prince must desire to be held merciful and not cruel; nonetheless he must take care not to use this mercy badly."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

The Chair schedules the Author's paper for the late afternoon.

The decision that will settle the session is made three weeks before the meeting. The Chair sits at his desk at home, reviewing the papers that have come in for his working group. There are twelve papers and too few hours for them. Something must be cut. The Architect's paper, at R6, with prior polls and implementation experience and co-authors from two vendors, is plainly the first item: it is the most mature, the most discussed, the most expected. The Author's paper, at R0, with no prior discussion and no co-author, plainly goes later in the queue. It is new. It needs less time. It can go late in the day, or it can be deferred to a future meeting if time runs short.

Injuries, I wrote, "must be done all together, so that, being tasted less, they offend less; benefits must be given little by little, so that they are savored more." The late slot is an injury done at one stroke: three weeks before the meeting, at a desk at home, in one line of an agenda, and no one tastes it until 4:30 on Monday afternoon, when the room has thinned. The incumbent's benefits come little by little: the morning slot, each revision heard, each earlier poll, each "continuing" in a poll question. The hours have decided the session. The Chair does not experience this as cruelty. He experiences it as scheduling.

I judged cruelty and mercy by their effects, never by their names: "Cesare Borgia was held cruel; nonetheless that cruelty of his had mended the Romagna, united it, and brought it back to peace and loyalty." The Chair is no Duke, and his agenda is a severity that no one names.

The Author presents at 4:30 in the afternoon. He has been sitting in the room since 9:00 am. He watched the Architect's presentation first thing in the morning, when the room was full and alert. He watched two other papers after that. He ate a sandwich at lunch and came back. He watched two more papers. Now it is his turn. The room has thinned: eight people have left for parallel sessions or coffee or phone calls. The remaining eighteen are tired. Their laptops are open. Their attention is divided.

He presents. His slides are adequate. His examples are clear. His tradeoff disclosures are honest: he names the things his design cannot do, which the Architect's paper has not done in six revisions. He finishes. Hands go up, and the Chair has time for one. The first questioner is a recognized name, a peer of the Architect, and the question is this: "How does this differ from what is already in the working draft?"

It is a fair question and a devastating one, and the Author does not know why. To him it is a technical question with a technical answer: his design differs in these ways, handles these cases better, has this structural advantage. He answers it clearly and correctly. But the question was not technical. It was political. The answer does not matter. The frame has already set the bounds of every truth the room will hear this afternoon.

A prudent prince, I wrote, lets his wise men speak the truth only of the things he asks. A room keeps the same rule without having read it. It has asked how the paper differs from what is "already in the working draft," and so the Author's paper is the deviation and the draft is the measure. Different is risky. Different is uncertain. Different is the new prince asking the old prince's subjects to change their allegiance.

Nobody asks how many of the users would gain by the new design, and that truth, which the Author knows better than anyone in the room, is never asked for and so is never spoken.

The straw poll. Six for the Author's approach. Ten for the Architect's. Two for neither.

The Chair reads the numbers aloud, in the same even tone he uses for every poll. He does not comment. He does not interpret. He moves to the next agenda item. The Author sits in his chair and looks at the numbers on the screen and feels something he will later recognize as the first cut of a knife he did not see coming.

The room has voted by the eyes. "Everyone sees what you seem," I wrote; "few touch what you are." One presentation late in the day, a few slides, a framed question: on these the room has judged what each paper seemed. The few who have touched the Author's design are his users, and they are not in the room. The few delegates who read both papers do not stand against the many who have the working draft behind them. Sixteen hands counted what the design seemed, and no hand was raised for the engineers who know what it is. Consensus is not correctness.

The Chair's scheduling was not cruel. It was orderly. The first questioner's framing was not cruel. It was reasonable. The poll result was not cruel. It was democratic. Yet the Author lost by four, and the order of presentations, the hour, the Architect's arms in the front rows, and the frame of the first question decided it among them. The order and the hour were the Chair's.

A wise prince founds himself on what is his own. The schedule, the framing, the poll question, and the summary are the Chair's own. The Author's own is his paper. They are playing different games, and the Chair's game determines the outcome of the Author's.

## VI. A Ruler and His Promises

> "How praiseworthy it is in a prince to keep faith, and to live with integrity and not by cunning, everyone understands. Nonetheless one sees by experience in our times that the princes who have done great things are those who have held faith of little account."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

The committee presents itself as a republic of technical merit. This is its promise. A paper rises or falls on its engineering. The room judges the work, not the man. The sounder design prevails. The process is open, published, and governed by consensus. Anyone can submit a paper, and every paper on the agenda will be heard. The committee exists to serve the language and its users.

I recognize this promise, and I filed it long ago under my eighteenth chapter: "It is not necessary, then, for a prince to have in fact all the qualities written above, but it is very necessary to seem to have them."

The Chair seems neutral. He gives each presenter the time the agenda allots. He does not speak for or against any proposal during the discussion. He frames the poll question in balanced terms. He reads the results without commentary. Every outward sign of neutrality is present. The Author, new to the process, reads these signs and concludes that the process is what it says it is: that the room weighs designs on their engineering, that the poll reflects its judgment of the papers, and that the sounder design will prevail in the end if it is presented clearly enough and revised patiently enough.

He is living among the imagined republics. "How one lives is so far from how one ought to live," I wrote, "that he who lets go of what is done for what ought to be done learns his ruin rather than his preservation."

The Author behaves as men ought to behave. He discloses his tradeoffs. He answers questions honestly, even when honesty means conceding that his design has limits. He does not court delegates at dinner. He does not arrange for the first question from the floor to come from an ally. He does not win friends for his paper in the hallways before he presents it. He thinks them unnecessary, for he believes the committee is the republic of merit it promises to be, where the quality of the work is enough.

The Chair lives in the principality that actually exists. He promises neutrality and keeps the promise, and he governs in everything the promise does not cover. The promise says: "each paper will be heard." The power says: "the order in which they are heard determines their fate." The promise says: "the poll reflects the room's judgment." The power says: "the framing of the poll question shapes the judgment." The promise and the power live in the same house without quarreling, and both are real. The Chair keeps his word. He just has more words than the ones he gives.

A prince, I wrote, must be fox and lion both, for "the lion cannot defend himself from snares, and the fox cannot defend himself from wolves. One must therefore be a fox to recognize the snares and a lion to frighten the wolves." The Chair is fox and lion at once. The fox is in the schedule: quiet decisions, each defensible by procedure, that favor the incumbent and leave the challenger the worst hour of the day. The lion is in the summary spoken from the front of the room, at the moment the room is readiest to be told.

The Author does not play the fox. He does not play the lion. He plays only the man, who fights with laws, as the beasts fight with force: honest, open, technically able, and trusting that if he presents his work clearly and answers questions honestly the institution will do the rest. Of such a man I wrote that he "must come to ruin among so many who are not good." The snares in this room are laid by its order, and not by its men.

The Author does not want to "learn to be able not to be good." He should not have to. The institution's promise tells him that being good is enough. The promise is the trap. The man who keeps his promises in a world of fox and lion is the man who loses, not because he is wrong but because he is fighting on a field that does not exist.

In the corridor after the session, a woman who has sat in this working group for twelve years, whom I will call the Patron, falls into step beside him. She was in the room at 4:30, one of the two who raised a hand for neither paper. She does not console him. "Correctness is the entry fee, not the prize," she says. "Next time, bring a co-author. Answer the objections in writing before anyone raises them. And talk to people before you present, not after." Then she is gone, into a knot of delegates who greet her by name.

That night the Author goes back to his hotel room and opens his laptop, and the hotel room becomes his study. He rereads the poll results: six to ten. He rereads his paper, and then the Architect's. He makes a list of every technical point where his design is stronger, and the list is long. He makes a second list, of everything the Architect holds, and it is also long, but its items are of another kind: co-authors, prior polls, an implementation in a compiler, a reflector thread of supportive analysis, a relationship with the Chair. He is doing what I did on my farm in the evenings, after afternoons of cards at the inn with the host, a butcher, a miller, and two kiln-men: I noted down what I had gained from the ancients before I could lose it. The Author notes down what the room has taught him. He stares at the second list.

## VII. On Fortresses

> "Some princes, to hold their state securely, have disarmed their subjects; others have kept their subject towns divided into factions; others have fed enmities against themselves; others have turned to winning over those they held suspect at the beginning of their rule; some have built fortresses; some have ruined and destroyed them."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

The Chair keeps fortresses, though he would not call them that.

The first fortress is the agenda. It is the outermost wall. The agenda is published before each meeting, and its composition reflects the Chair's judgment about what the working group should discuss. This judgment is constrained - there are deadlines, mailing dates, paper numbers, a general expectation that papers submitted on time will be heard - but within those constraints the Chair has enormous latitude. He decides what gets ninety minutes and what gets fifteen. He decides what gets a direction poll and what gets a "sense of the room." He decides what gets deferred to the next meeting with a polite note that "we ran out of time." Each of these decisions is a gate, and the Chair holds the key.

The second fortress is the study group routing. Before a paper reaches the working group, it often passes first through a study group - a smaller body with its own chair, its own agenda, its own politics. The working group chair does not control the study group, but he influences it. He and the study group chair talk. They coordinate. They agree on which papers are "ready" for the working group and which need "more incubation." A paper that the working group chair considers premature will be sent back to the study group for further work, and the study group, as I judge, will accept this guidance, because its chair respects the working group chair and shares his assumptions. The routing is invisible to the one it routes. On Wednesday the Newcomer, the woman from the registration line, presents her paper to a study group of twelve. Three of them tell her afterward that they liked it, and the study group sends it back for "more incubation." She sees only that her paper will not reach the working group this week. She does not see the conversation that preceded the decision.

The third fortress is the poll question. A fortress exists to make attack costly, and the poll question does the same: it raises the cost of the Author's challenge by building in assumptions that favor the incumbent. "Who is in favor of continuing to explore the approach in P-first?" is a fortress. The word "continuing" tells the room that this approach has been explored before - that it has history, momentum, and weight. The Author's paper has no such word. It is new. There is nothing to "continue."

The fourth fortress is the summary before the vote. The Chair summarizes the discussion in thirty seconds, standing at the front of the room, speaking from the only position of institutional authority. "It seems like we heard broad support for the existing direction, with some interest in exploring the alternative further." This sentence is a fortress. It frames the existing direction as the consensus and the Author's paper as the alternative. It frames "broad support" as belonging to the incumbent and "some interest" as belonging to the challenger. The words are accurate - there was broad support for the existing direction, and there was some interest in the alternative. But accuracy is not neutrality, and the summary's frame will be the last thing the uncommitted middle hears before it raises its hands.

Near the end of my chapter on fortresses I judged that "the best fortress there is, is not to be hated by the people; for though you may have fortresses, if the people hold you in hatred they will not save you, since a people that has taken up arms never lacks foreigners to come to its aid." I wrote that sentence in 1513. In 1526 I was chancellor of the five citizens charged with fortifying the walls of Florence, and the walls I kept were the Medici's; so I have kept a prince's fortresses myself, and I know which way they face.

No one hates the Chair; he is respected, and his fortresses pass for the room's furniture, which no one hates because no one sees it. The Author does not hate the Chair. He does not even know the Chair stands across the field from him. He thinks the Chair is the referee. He thinks the agenda is logistics. He thinks the poll question is a neutral instrument. He thinks the summary is a fair description of what was said. He thinks the fortress is a door.

A people needs no hatred to find its foreigners. The people here are the uncounted who use the language. Their foreigners are every other language and every other library they can move to. They will storm no gate; they will write a blog post or a forum thread, and then they will begin their next project somewhere else. The worst a prince can expect from a people turned against him, I wrote, is to be abandoned by it, and abandonment sounds no trumpet: it simply stops showing up. Of the four fortresses, not one faces outward.

The Author leaves the room thinking he lost on the merits. He lost on the architecture - on the arrangement of walls and gates and summaries that the Chair has kept and repaired over six years, that the Chair calls procedure, and that the Author could not have seen without having stood inside them.

Count, then, the polls the Chair calls. At 4:30 on Monday he calls a poll between the approach in P-first and the approach in P-second. At a later meeting he will call a poll on whether to forward the winner to the next group. Each is a poll the peerage would want.

"In every city," I wrote, "these two diverse humors are found": the great seek to command, and the people seek only not to be oppressed. The peerage of this room is mild, and its humor is only to add: to advance, to forward, to see its work in the draft. The users' humor is milder still. Their poll would be a single count: how many of them would actually benefit.

Twenty-six delegates sat in the room that morning and eighteen were left at 4:30, and not one of them asked for it. Of all the polls in the room, it is the one the Chair never calls.

## VIII. The Role of Fortune

> "It is not unknown to me that many have held, and hold, the opinion that the things of the world are so governed by fortune and by God that men with their prudence cannot correct them, and indeed have no remedy for them at all."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

Six months pass. The Author revises his paper. He finds a co-author whose company already ships the design in its own product, and who said: "This is better. I will put my name on it." He builds a second implementation in a public repository. He answers every objection from the first meeting in writing. He wins friends before the meeting: he emails delegates, he talks in hallways, he does what the Patron told him in the corridor.

He returns and presents R2. The room has shifted. His co-author sits in the front row. The implementation is public. The objections are answered. The first questioner this time is an engineer who has tried the second implementation, and the bounds of the truth the room will hear are wider than before. The Author answers well. The poll: seven to eight, with three not voting. He has lost again, but the margin has narrowed from four to one. The trend is visible.

In wholly new principalities, I wrote, "one finds more or less difficulty in holding them, as he who acquires them has more or less virtu." The Author's virtu has not changed; his knowledge of the ground has. He has gathered arms of his own. He has learned the fox. He cannot yet play the lion, for he has too few arms for that, but he is no longer the unarmed prophet preaching to the masses; he is Hiero, doing away with the old militia and ordering a new one.

But the decisive turn comes from fortune, and from nothing the Author does.

My most famous passage, quoted and misquoted for five centuries, compares fortune to a river in flood: "I liken her to a ruinous river that, when it rages, floods the plains, tears down trees and buildings, takes earth from this side and sets it down on the other; everyone flees before it, everyone yields to its fury without being able to resist it at all. And though it is made this way, it does not follow that men, when the times are quiet, cannot make provision against it with barriers and embankments."

The Chair built no barriers and no embankments. He kept fortresses, inside the room, where his power works, facing the working group, the challengers, and the kind of threat the Author brought. Toward the river he built nothing, because the river is not in the room. The river is the public.

The Architect's revision is forwarded, and its wording is applied to the working draft. It advances to the ballot. The national bodies review it, and their comments arrive, written by engineers in other countries who read the wording with the care of people who must implement it, explain it, or defend it. One comment names a structural limitation that none of the Architect's six revisions disclosed. Another says that the interaction with a related language feature "was not evaluated." A third notes that the novel parts of the design have no deployment experience behind them.

These reviewers are the prudent physicians of my third chapter, who say of the hectic fever that "at the beginning it is easy to cure and hard to recognize, but in the course of time, not having been recognized or treated at the beginning, it becomes easy to recognize and hard to cure." A reviewer has recognized the fever from afar, while the cure is still a revision. The room answers the comments, and the fever goes untreated.

The standard ships. A senior engineer at a startup tries to use the Architect's feature and meets, in practice, the structural limitation the reviewer found in the wording. She writes a blog post. It spreads: five hundred upvotes, two hundred comments. The river is over the plain. The Chair, in his office three thousand miles away, reads the post on a Tuesday morning like anyone else.

I judged that "fortune is the arbiter of half our actions, but she still leaves the other half, or a little less, for us to govern." The Author's half was the paper, the implementation, the co-author, the revision. Fortune's half was the hour the public's verdict arrived and the voice it arrived in. He could not have planned for the blog post, but he had prepared for it by doing what the Architect's paper never did: disclosing his tradeoffs, naming his limits, and building on a foundation that had already survived contact with users who test every assumption and forgive nothing.

He prospers, I wrote, "whose way of proceeding matches the quality of the times." The Chair's way of proceeding was made for the room, and the times have moved outside it. The agenda for his next meeting looks like the agenda for his last. His fortresses held against the Author. They did not hold against the users.

## IX. Why Italian Rulers Have Lost Their States

> "Therefore let these princes of ours, who had been many years in their principalities and then lost them, not accuse fortune but their own sloth."
>
> -- Niccolo Machiavelli, *The Prince*

The Chair did not lose his state. He is still the Chair. He still schedules. He still frames. He still summarizes. The hereditary prince sits on his hereditary throne, and the room looks exactly as it looked before.

But something has changed, and the change is in the public record.

The Architect's feature is in the standard, in a document that bears an ISO number. It is permanent, or as permanent as anything in a specification revised every three years. And it is gathering correction papers: three so far, filed by implementers and users who found in practice what the national body reviewer found in the wording, that the structural limitation hurts the very uses the design was meant to serve. A fourth correction comes from the Architect himself, and it arrives quickly, within weeks of the blog post. Each correction paper is a public record of what the room did not weigh, an entry in the institution's own ledger in the institution's own hand.

The Author's paper, which lost two polls in the room, is mentioned favorably in the blog post. A commenter writes: "The alternative approach actually handles this case." The Author lost in the room and won in the field. The uncounted, who write the code, ship the products, and pay in the end for the delegates' seats, have given their verdict, and it is not the verdict the Chair's room produced.

"It is a common defect of men," I wrote, "not to reckon on the storm in fair weather," and the Chair's order made no provision for a public that reads the wording. He governed for the room: for the two dozen delegates who sit in his sessions, vote in his polls, hear his framing, and weigh his judgment. The room was his state. The public stood beyond its frontier, too many to count, too scattered to gather, too far away to matter, and the order he inherited built nothing toward it, because by that order the public was someone else's charge: the conference speakers', the bloggers', the compiler vendors'. The Chair's charge was the room, and the room he governed well.

But the feature now ships with the compilers and waits in the build of every project that reaches for it. What the room makes, the public must live with, as a river lives with whatever the mill lets into its water.

I wrote that "those defenses alone are good, certain, and lasting that depend on you yourself and on your own virtu." The Author's defenses were his own: code in production, tradeoffs disclosed, users who depend on it. He had built no fortress, only a foundation, and a foundation is what Hiero built on.

The Chair's defenses were the institution's: the agenda, the framing, the polls, the rule of consensus. They are real defenses, and they held for six years, but they are the arms of others. The process was built by those who came before him, the consensus rule is ISO's definition reprinted in the committee's own procedures, and the agenda's form was inherited. The Chair kept all of these in good repair and built none of them. When the process raised up a feature with a flaw it was never arranged to look for, the Chair had nothing of his own to stand on. The walls were never his.

I ended my little work with an appeal to the young Lorenzo to drive out the foreigners and give Italy back to its people. The appeal failed, and Italy waited three hundred and fifty years for its unity. But I did not write it for Lorenzo alone. I wrote it for anyone who would go after the effectual truth of the thing and look, without turning away, at the gap between how states are held and how they ought to be. The Author is such a man. He is at his desk at home, after the standard has shipped, after the post has spread, after the correction papers have begun to gather, and beside the post he has opened the two lists he made in his hotel room on the night of the first poll. He reads the second list again. Beside each item he writes what he now has and what he still lacks. It is the inventory a new prince makes of his arms.

He understands that the committee is a principality, not a republic. He understands that the Chair is a prince, not a referee. He understands that the process is a fortress, not a door.

"I have not adorned this work," I wrote in the dedication, and the Author will not adorn his. The paper he submits to the next mailing will be as honest and as plain as the first. But this time he will bring it through the gate himself, armed, with friends, having studied the fortresses and learned the lie of the ground. He will present first, or he will not present at all. He will arrange the first question. He will read the room as the Chair reads it, and be the fox when the fox is needed and the lion when the lion is needed, and the man always, for the man is what survives. He will do these things not because they are right but because they are necessary, and necessity is the word that beats through my little work like a pulse. What he will write is a little work of his own, on what a paper is, how it is won, how it is kept, and why it is lost, and he still means to win.

The Author sits back from his laptop. Outside, his own city is dark. He remembers the Newcomer from the registration line, who never came to another meeting. Like the friar, she was ruined in her new orders as soon as the multitude, a room of twelve, began not to believe her. He wonders whether she would have stayed if someone had told her what the Patron told him in the corridor: that correctness is the entry fee, not the prize.

No one told her. The institution does not tell anyone. It promises meritocracy and delivers principality, and the distance between the promise and the delivery is the space in which careers are made and broken.

I have one more thing to say. Of the founders of new orders I wrote that "without that occasion the virtu of their spirit would have been spent, and without that virtu the occasion would have come in vain." The Author's virtu is his library, needed and proven before it ever reached the room. The occasion is a committee that asks whether a design is correct and whether the room wants it, and never how many users will benefit. Out of that occasion have come a standard with a flaw, correction papers, a blog post, and an engineer who now understands how the room is held.

Whether that understanding is a victory or a defeat depends on what he does with it.

The mailing deadline is in six weeks. He begins to write.

I died in 1527, yet the principality I served persists.
